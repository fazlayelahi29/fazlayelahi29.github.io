# Bring Wine

_Maulana Jalal al-Din Rumi_

  

Bring wine, for I am suffering crop sickness from the vintage;

  

God has seized me, and I am thus held fast.

  

By love’s soul, bring me a cup of wine that is the envy of the sun,

  

for I care aught but love.

  

Bring that which if I were to call it “soul” would be a shame,

  

for the reason that I am pained in the head because of the soul.

  

Bring that whose name is not contained in this mouth,

  

through which the fissures of my speech split asunder.

  

Bring that which, when it is not present, I am stupid and ignorant,

  

but when I am with it, I am the king of the subtle and crafty ones.

  

Bring that which, the moment it is void of my head, I become black and dark,

  

you might say I am of the infidels.

  

Bring that which delivers out of this “bring” and “do not bring”;

  

bring quickly, and repel me not, saying, “Whence shall I bring it?”

  

Bring, and deliver the roof of the heavens through the long night

  

from my abundant smoke and lamentations.

  

Bring that which after my death, even out of my dust,

  

will restore me to speech and thanksgiving even as Najjar.

  

Bring me wine, for I am guardian of wine like a goblet,

  

for whatever has gone into my stomach I deliver back completely.

# Synopsis 

> The central theme of Rumi’s poem "Bring Wine" (Poem 216 from _Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi_) is the intense longing for divine union, the transcendence of the physical body, and the complete dissolution of the human ego.
> 
>   

## 1. Overview and Context

- **Source Text:** Poem 216 from _Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi_, translated by A.J. Arberry.
    
- **Literary Tradition:** Sufi mystical poetry (_ghazal_).
    
- **Core Subject:** The spiritual agony experienced by the human soul after tasting divine consciousness and returning to ordinary earthly existence.
    

## 2. The Metaphor of Divine Wine and Spiritual "Crop Sickness"

In the opening lines, a desperate plea is made for "wine" to cure "crop sickness" (a hangover). Within the Sufi tradition, these terms carry specific symbolic meanings:

  

- **Wine:** Divine love, spiritual grace, and ecstatic union with God.
    
- **Crop Sickness (_Khumar_):** The painful state of spiritual withdrawal and disorientation felt when an ecstatic mystical experience fades.
    

It is suggested that once the soul has been "seized" by the Divine, ordinary reality becomes unbearable. Just as a physical hangover is traditionally cured by consuming more wine, spiritual separation can only be healed by being completely submerged in divine presence once again.

  

## 3. The Limits of Language and Human Perception

As the poem progresses, the nature of this divine essence is described as being far beyond normal human understanding:

  

- **Ineffability:** The spiritual force is depicted as so vast that even calling it "soul" is considered inadequate, and human speech literally breaks apart when attempting to name it.
    
- **Spiritual Awakening:** Without this divine intoxication, the human being is described as spiritually blind and cut off from the truth. Once the wine is received, true inner vision is awakened.
    

## 4. Transcendence of Duality and the Vessel of the Ego

A request is made to be freed from the constant cycle of earthly desire—represented by the opposing actions of "bring" and "do not bring."

  

- The human self is compared to an empty goblet.
    
- Rather than hoarding spiritual wisdom, the ego is emptied so that divine truth can be received and poured back out into the world through poetry and spiritual guidance.
    

## 5. The Allegory of Habib Najjar: Body Versus Spirit

In the second half of the poem, the narrative voice merges with Habib Najjar ("the Carpenter"), a legendary martyr from Islamic tradition who was killed by his own people for defending divine messengers. Through his voice, a sharp distinction is drawn between the mortal body and the immortal spirit:

  

- **Ascension of the Soul:** Just as Jesus ascended to heaven while leaving his donkey behind on earth, Najjar’s physical body is left in the dirt while his spirit climbs a spiritual ladder to the seventh heaven.
    
- **Warning Against Shallow Vision:** A reference is made to Eblis (Satan), who refused to bow to Adam because only physical "water and clay" were seen, while the divine spark within was ignored. The reader is urged to look past the fragile physical exterior of a mystic and recognize the "hundred thousand rose bowers" (infinite spiritual beauty) blooming inside.
    

## 6. The Climax: Ego-Dissolution (_Fana_) and Shams-e Tabrizi

In the final lines, the poetic voice shifts entirely to Shams-e Tabrizi, Rumi’s revered spiritual mentor whose name translates to "The Sun of Tabriz."

- **Union with the Beloved:** By speaking directly as Shams, complete ego-dissolution (_fana_) is demonstrated; the separation between student, teacher, and the Divine is completely erased.
    
- **The Eternal Sun:** The physical body is dismissed as temporary "mire" (mud) and a "veil." Even when the physical form sets or disappears into the earth, the true spiritual essence is proclaimed to be the eternal sun, which continues to rise every morning regardless of whether the spiritually blind are able to perceive it.

# Analysis 

> At its core, Rumi’s **"Bring Wine"** (Poem 216 in _Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi_, translated by A.J. Arberry) is about **spiritual withdrawal** and the **annihilation of the ego**.
> 
>   

Rather than a casual celebration of divine love, this specific poem captures an urgent, almost desperate crisis: Rumi has tasted a higher state of spiritual consciousness, returned to ordinary human awareness, and now finds normal existence unbearable.

  

## 1. The Spiritual "Hangover" (Lines 1–2)

> _"Bring wine, for I am suffering crop sickness from the vintage; / God has seized me, and I am thus held fast."_
> 
>   

In Persian poetry, **"crop sickness"** (_khumar_) means a hangover. Rumi isn't asking for his first taste of God; he has already been "seized" by a massive spiritual experience. Now that the wave of ecstasy has receded, ordinary reality feels like a painful headache. In folk remedies, the cure for a hangover is "hair of the dog"—more wine. Spiritually, Rumi is saying that once you have experienced divine union, the only cure for the ache of returning to the material world is to be submerged in that divine presence again.

  

## 2. Beyond the Soul and Beyond Words (Lines 3–6)

> _"Bring that which if I were to call it 'soul' would be a shame... / Bring that whose name is not contained in this mouth, through which the fissures of my speech split asunder."_
> 
>   

Here, Rumi pushes past standard religious concepts. Even calling this divine force "soul" feels too small and limiting. He describes a state so vast that human language literally breaks apart trying to name it. Without this spiritual intoxication, Rumi feels spiritually blind, dull, and "of the infidels" (cut off from truth); with it, his inner perception awakens completely.

  

## 3. Escaping Duality (Lines 7–10)

> _"Bring that which delivers out of this 'bring' and 'do not bring'..."_
> 
>   

Rumi asks for an awareness that frees him from the push-and-pull of human desire—the constant cycle of wanting ("bring") and rejecting ("do not bring"). He compares himself to a **goblet**: an empty vessel that does not hoard the wine for itself, but takes it in and pours it back out completely into the world (through his poetry and spiritual state).

  

## 4. The Martyr's Ascent: Habib Najjar (Lines 11–15)

> _"Najjar said, 'After my death would that my people might be open-eyed to the ecstasy within me... Do not like Eblis see in Adam only water and clay; see that behind the clay are my hundred thousand rose bowers.'"_
> 
>   

In the second half of the poem, Rumi's own voice dissolves and merges with **Habib Najjar** ("the Carpenter"), a figure from Islamic tradition who was martyred by his townspeople for defending divine messengers.

  

- **The Body vs. The Spirit:** Just as Jesus ascended while leaving his donkey ("ass") behind on earth, Najjar’s physical body is left in the dirt while his spirit climbs a "ladder" to the seventh heaven.
    
- **The Warning Against Shallow Vision:** Najjar references Eblis (Satan), who famously refused to bow to Adam because he only saw physical "water and clay" and missed the divine spark inside. Rumi uses this to urge the reader to look past the fragile, mortal exterior of a mystic and see the "hundred thousand rose bowers" blooming within.
    

## 5. The Sun Rising from the Mire: Shams-e Tabrizi (Lines 16–18)

> _"Shams-e Tabrizi rose up from this flesh saying, 'I am the sun. Bring up my head from this mire... Every morning I will rise up, despite the blind'"_
> 
>   

At the climax of the poem, the voice shifts one final time to **Shams-e Tabrizi**—Rumi’s beloved spiritual mentor whose name literally translates to _"The Sun of Tabriz"_.

  

- By speaking as Shams, Rumi demonstrates total ego-dissolution (_fana_): the boundary between student and teacher, and between human and Divine, has vanished.
    
- Shams declares that the physical body is merely "mire" (mud) and a temporary "veil". Even when he sets (dies or disappears into the earth), his true essence is the eternal sun, which will keep rising every morning regardless of whether "the blind" (the spiritually asleep) can see it.

# Habib al-Najjar and His Significance in Rumi’s "Bring Wine"

> Habib al-Najjar is a revered martyr in Islamic tradition whose story of spiritual triumph over physical death is used by Maulana Jalal al-Din Rumi in his poem "Bring Wine" to illustrate the immortality of the soul and the blindness of worldly perception.
> 
>   

## 1. Historical and Religious Identity of Habib al-Najjar

- **Background:** Habib al-Najjar (literally translated as "Habib the Carpenter") was a humble craftsman living on the outskirts of the ancient city of Antioch (modern-day Antakya, Turkey). His story is rooted in Islamic tradition and Quranic exegesis (_Tafsir_) concerning _Surah Ya-Sin_ (Chapter 36, Verses 20–27).
    
- **Acceptance of Truth:** When divine messengers (identified in classical tradition as disciples of Jesus) arrived in Antioch to preach monotheism, they were rejected by the city's leaders. Habib, who suffered from leprosy, was healed by the messengers and embraced their spiritual truth.
    
- **Martyrdom and Compassion:** Upon learning that the townspeople intended to execute the messengers, Habib hurried into the city to defend them publicly. Enraged by his testimony, the crowd stoned him to death. According to the Quran, Habib was immediately admitted into Paradise, where his first utterance was not a curse against his killers, but a compassionate wish: _"Would that my people knew of how my Lord has forgiven me and placed me among the honored!"_ (Quran 36:26–27).
    

## 2. Relationship to Rumi’s Poem "Bring Wine"

In the second half of "Bring Wine" (Poem 216 from _Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi_), the persona of Habib al-Najjar is adopted by Maulana Rumi to reinforce the central mystical themes of the poem:

- **Direct Paraphrase of Spiritual Compassion:** The famous Quranic exclamation of Habib al-Najjar is echoed directly in the poem (_"Najjar said, 'After my death would that my people might be open-eyed to the ecstasy within me...'"_). Through this allusion, it is demonstrated that an enlightened soul harbors no resentment toward ignorant persecutors, desiring only that others awaken to divine joy.
    
- **Separation of the Immortal Soul from the Mortal Body:** Habib’s physical execution on earth contrasted with his immediate elevation to Paradise serves as a metaphor for spiritual ascension. Just as Jesus ascended to heaven while leaving his donkey behind on earth, Habib’s physical body is left in the dirt while his spirit climbs a ladder to the seventh heaven.
    
- **Contrast Between Outward Appearance and Inner Reality:** The citizens of Antioch judged Habib merely by his lowly status as a sick carpenter—an error compared in the poem to Eblis (Satan), who looked at Adam and saw only physical "water and clay." Habib’s story is used to warn readers against shallow materialism, proving that behind a fragile human exterior lie "hundred thousand rose bowers" of divine beauty.
    
- **Parallel to Shams-e Tabrizi:** Within the broader context of the poem, Habib al-Najjar functions as a spiritual mirror for Rumi’s beloved mentor, Shams-e Tabrizi. Just as the people of Antioch were spiritually blind to the truth carried by Habib, Rumi’s own jealous disciples were blind to the divine light within Shams, ultimately driving him away.

# Religious and Mystical Allusions in Rumi’s "Bring Wine"

> In Rumi’s poem "Bring Wine," the religious allusions to Eblis, Jesus’s donkey, the Seventh Heaven, and Shams-e Tabrizi are employed to contrast shallow, materialistic vision with the infinite, immortal nature of the human spirit.

## 1. Eblis (Satan) and "Water and Clay"

- **Scriptural Context:** According to Quranic tradition, when God created Adam and commanded the angels to bow before him, Eblis (Iblis/Satan) refused out of arrogance. He argued that he was superior because he had been created from fire, whereas Adam was merely formed from earthly mud ("water and clay").
    
- **Role in the Poem:** Eblis is referenced in the poem as the ultimate symbol of spiritual blindness and superficial judgment. Because Eblis focused strictly on Adam’s physical exterior, he failed to perceive the divine spirit breathed into Adam by God.
    
- **Mystical Lesson:** The reader is warned not to imitate Eblis by judging a mystic or spiritual master solely by their fragile, mortal body. Behind the humble exterior of "water and clay" lie "hundred thousand rose bowers"—representing infinite inner beauty and divine truth.
    

## 2. Jesus and His Donkey ("The Ass")

- **Literary and Sufi Context:** In classical Persian Sufi literature (frequently utilized throughout Rumi’s _Masnavi_ and _Divan_), Jesus (_Isa_) is revered as a symbol of pure spirit and detachment from worldly desires, while his donkey (_khar-e Isa_) represents the physical body and base animal instincts (_nafs_). According to Sufi tradition, when Jesus ascended to heaven, his earthly donkey was left behind on the ground.
    
- **Role in the Poem:** This imagery is paired with the martyrdom of Habib al-Najjar. Just as Jesus left his donkey on earth while rising to heaven, the mystic’s physical body is left behind in the dirt while the liberated soul ascends freely.
    
- **Mystical Lesson:** It is emphasized that the human body is merely a temporary vehicle for the journey of life. Physical death is therefore not an end, but the necessary shedding of a heavy earthly burden so the spirit can return to its divine origin.
    

## 3. The Ladder to the Seventh Heaven

- **Cosmological Context:** In traditional Islamic and medieval cosmology, the universe is structured into seven concentric heavens (_Haft Aseman_), with the Seventh Heaven representing the highest spiritual realm closest to the Divine Throne.
    
- **Role in the Poem:** After leaving the physical body behind, the spirit is depicted as climbing a spiritual ladder directly to the Seventh Heaven.
    
- **Mystical Lesson:** True spiritual awakening elevates human consciousness far above worldly concerns, granting the soul direct access to the highest state of divine proximity.
    

## 4. Shams-e Tabrizi ("The Sun of Tabriz")

- **Historical Context:** Shams-e Tabrizi was Rumi’s wandering spiritual mentor whose sudden, mysterious disappearance (and suspected murder by Rumi’s jealous disciples in Konya) transformed Rumi into an ecstatic poet. His name literally translates to "The Sun of Tabriz."
    
- **Role in the Poem:** In the closing lines, Rumi speaks directly in the voice of Shams, declaring that even though his physical form has been buried in the "mire" (earth) behind a "veil," his true essence is the eternal sun that rises every morning.
    
- **Mystical Lesson:** Two core Sufi truths are illustrated through this final allusion:
    
    - **Ego-Dissolution (_Fana_):** By speaking as Shams, Rumi demonstrates that the boundary between lover and beloved has completely vanished.
        
    - **Permanence of Truth:** Just as the physical sun continues to shine regardless of whether blind people can see it, divine truth remains eternal and unaffected by worldly rejection or physical death.

# Additional Insights into Rumi’s "Bring Wine"

> Beyond its spiritual symbolism, Rumi’s poem "Bring Wine" features remarkable poetic structure, musical rhythm, and literary techniques in the original Persian that heighten its ecstatic urgency.
> 
>   

## 1. The Persian Poetic Form (_Ghazal_ and _Radif_)

- **Structure:** In the original Persian (_Divan-e Shams-e Tabrizi_, Ghazal 216), the poem is composed as a _ghazal_—a traditional lyric form consisting of rhyming couplets (_bayts_) that share a unified meter and refrain.
    
- **The Insistent Refrain (_Radif_):** Almost every couplet in the Persian original ends with the urgent imperative verb _"biyar"_ ("bring!"). This rhythmic repetition acts like a drumbeat—echoing the spinning whirling-dervish (_Sema_) ceremonies Rumi was famous for—and creates a sense of breathless spiritual desperation.
    

## 2. The _Takhallus_ (Pen-Name Signature) Twist

- **Traditional Rule:** In classical Persian _ghazals_, the poet traditionally inserts their own name in the final couplet as a signature (_takhallus_).
    
- **Rumi’s Innovation:** Instead of signing his own name ("Rumi" or "Mawlana"), Rumi signs the poem with the name of his beloved mentor, Shams-e Tabrizi. Across thousands of poems in his _Divan_, Rumi habitually replaced his own identity with Shams's name to signify that his individual ego had completely merged into his spiritual master.
    

## 3. Literal vs. Mystical Wordplay (_Iham_)

Rumi frequently uses a Persian literary device called _iham_ (double entendre), where a single word carries both an everyday physical meaning and a hidden mystical meaning simultaneously:

  

- **Wine (_Sharab_ / _Mey_):** In medieval Islamic society, literal alcohol was forbidden, making the use of tavern imagery intentionally provocative. By borrowing the language of rebel tavern-goers (_rinds_), Rumi signals that true divine love breaks past rigid, outward religious formalities into direct inner experience.
    
- **Crop Sickness (_Khumar_):** In Persian, _khumar_ does not only mean a painful hangover; it also describes the heavy-lidded, dreamy, intoxicated gaze of a lover. Thus, Rumi’s "sickness" is simultaneously an agony of separation and a lingering trace of divine beauty.
    

## 4. The Dramatic Shift in Voices (Polyphony)

Rather than speaking from a single static viewpoint, the poem unfolds like a three-act spiritual drama through shifting personas:

1. **Act I (The Desperate Lover):** Rumi speaks as the aching human soul begging for relief from worldly separation.
    
2. **Act II (The Martyred Saint):** The voice transforms into Habib al-Najjar, speaking from beyond the grave to teach detachment from the physical body.
    
3. **Act III (The Eternal Sun):** The voice culminates in Shams-e Tabrizi, speaking not as a human being at all, but as the undying cosmic light of truth itself.
