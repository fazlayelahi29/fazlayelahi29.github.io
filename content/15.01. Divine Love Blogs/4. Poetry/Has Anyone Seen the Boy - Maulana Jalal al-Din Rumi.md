# Has Anyone Seen the Boy

_Maulana Jalal al-Din Rumi_

_Translated by Coleman Barks and John Moyne_


  

Has anyone seen the boy who used to come here?

  

Round-faced trouble-maker, quick to find a joke,

  

slow to be serious, red shirt,

  

perfect coordination, sly, strong-muscled,

  

with things always in his pocket: reed flute,

  

worn pick, polished and ready for his Talent,

  

you know the one.

  

Have you heard stories about him?

  

Pharoah and the whole Egyptian world

  

collapsed for such a Joseph.

  

I’d gladly spend years getting word

  

of him, even third or fourth hand.


--- 

# Analysis

> A line-by-line analysis of Maulana Jalal al-Din Rumi’s poem "Has Anyone Seen the Boy" reveals how each individual verse builds a vivid portrait of the Divine Beloved—Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala—and Rumi’s vanished spiritual mentor, Shams-e Tabrizi, capturing the intense spiritual longing of the human soul.
> 
>   

## Line 1

> _"Has anyone seen the boy who used to come here?"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The poem opens with an urgent, conversational question addressed to passersby or companions, asking about the whereabouts of a lively young man who once visited regularly.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** This opening line establishes the central theme of spiritual separation (_firaq_). In Sufi mysticism, the human soul experiences moments of close spiritual illumination followed by periods where that divine presence feels hidden. By asking "Has anyone seen...", the seeker expresses the humble, desperate state of a lover searching everywhere for signs of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala (and, biographically, Rumi searching for his vanished mentor, Shams-e Tabrizi). The phrase "who used to come here" reminds the reader that divine grace once visited the heart directly, making its sudden absence feel all the more acute.
    

## Line 2

> _"Round-faced trouble-maker, quick to find a joke,"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The speaker begins to describe the missing youth’s appearance and personality: he has a full, round face, causes playful disruption, and constantly finds humor in situations.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** In classical Persian poetry, a "round face" is a traditional metaphor for the full moon, symbolizing complete, radiant beauty and perfection. Calling the Beloved a "trouble-maker" highlights how true love for Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala shatters comfortable worldly routines, rigid habits, and self-centered ego. Being "quick to find a joke" indicates that divine wisdom refuses to be trapped by stiff, overly solemn human pride; instead, spiritual awakening brings joy, lightness, and liberation from worldly anxiety.
    

## Line 3

> _"slow to be serious, red shirt,"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The description continues by noting that the youth resists dry seriousness and wears a bright red shirt.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** Being "slow to be serious" reinforces the Sufi rejection of hollow, mechanical religious formalism that lacks genuine love in the heart. The "red shirt" is a deeply significant symbol in Persian mystical literature: the color red represents spiritual passion, celebration, bridal joy, and divine intoxication (_mast_). It signifies that the presence of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala brings vibrant life and warmth into a dull, monochrome material world.
    

## Line 4

> _"perfect coordination, sly, strong-muscled,"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The speaker praises the youth’s physical grace, cleverness, and athletic strength.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** "Perfect coordination" reflects the flawless harmony, balance, and order through which Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala governs the universe and guides the human soul. Being "sly" points to the subtle, mysterious, and unexpected ways in which divine grace tests, surprises, and awakens the seeker—often working behind the scenes in ways the limited human intellect cannot predict. "Strong-muscled" symbolizes the irresistible majesty and spiritual power (_qudrah_) of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala, against which no worldly obstacle or ego can stand.
    

## Line 5

> _"with things always in his pocket: reed flute,"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The youth is remembered for always carrying musical items in his pocket, beginning with a reed flute.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** The reed flute (_ney_) is the single most iconic symbol in all of Maulana Rumi’s poetry. A reed is cut from its native reed bed and hollowed out so that air can pass through it; similarly, the human soul is separated from its heavenly origin and emptied of ego so that the sacred breath of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala can flow through it. Carrying the flute "always in his pocket" signifies that the Divine Beloved is constantly ready to breathe spiritual life and haunting, soulful music into any heart that makes itself empty and receptive.
    

## Lines 6–7

> _"worn pick, polished and ready for his Talent, / you know the one."_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** Alongside the flute, he carries a well-used musical pick (plectrum), polished from frequent use and ready for his great musical skill—a talent so famous that the listener immediately recognizes who is being described.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** A "worn pick" is used to pluck stringed instruments such as the Persian _rabab_ or _oud_. The fact that the pick is "worn" and "polished" proves that Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala is the eternal, tireless Master Musician who has continuously played upon the strings of human hearts throughout all ages, awakening spiritual ecstasy (_wajd_) and remembrance (_dhikr_). The intimate closing phrase, "you know the one," appeals directly to the reader’s innate spiritual memory (_fitrah_), implying that deep down, every human soul already recognizes its Creator.
    

## Line 8

> _"Have you heard stories about him?"_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** Shifting from physical description back to inquiry, the speaker asks whether the listeners have heard any tales or reports circulating about this youth.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** In the Sufi tradition, listening to stories, parables, and accounts of divine grace (_hikayat_) is a vital means of keeping spiritual longing alive when direct vision feels distant. Asking "Have you heard stories about him?" demonstrates how the seeker feeds their love for Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala through remembrance, sacred teachings, and the shared experiences of other seekers.
    

## Lines 9–10

> _"Pharaoh and the whole Egyptian world / collapsed for such a Joseph."_
> 
>   

- **Literal Meaning:** The speaker compares the missing youth to Prophet Joseph (Hazrat Yusuf, peace be upon him), recalling how Pharaoh and the entire civilization of Egypt were completely overwhelmed by his unmatched beauty and presence.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** In Islamic tradition and Persian poetry, Prophet Joseph (Yusuf) represents the highest earthly manifestation of divine beauty bestowed by Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala. "Pharaoh and the whole Egyptian world" symbolize supreme political authority, worldly wealth, and human arrogance. Their "collapse" before Joseph illustrates a core spiritual truth: when the overwhelming beauty and majesty of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala appear, all earthly pride, kingship, and material power crumble into nothingness.
    

## Lines 11–12

> _"I’d gladly spend years getting word / of him, even third or fourth hand."_

- **Literal Meaning:** In the concluding couplet, the speaker declares a willingness to spend many years just to receive news about the boy—even if that news is indirect, passed down through three or four different people.
    
- **Mystical Analysis:** These final lines capture the noble Sufi station of patient search and devotion (_talab_ and _sabr_). The seeker does not arrogantly demand immediate miracles or direct possession; instead, the love for Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala is so profound that even the faintest, most indirect trace or rumor of the Divine Beloved is treasured above all worldly gains. Biographically, this also reflects Maulana Rumi’s own years of tireless searching across Syria and Anatolia for any distant rumor of his vanished teacher, Shams-e Tabrizi—proving that on the spiritual path, sincere longing itself is a sacred reward.

# Synopsis 


> In Maulana Jalal al-Din Rumi’s poem "Has Anyone Seen the Boy," the speaker conducts an urgent, affectionate search for a lively, vanished youth—symbolizing both the elusive presence of Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala and Rumi’s disappeared spiritual mentor, Shams-e Tabrizi—whose captivating beauty and playful grace leave the human soul in a state of joyful, lifelong longing.

- **The Urgent Inquiry:** The poem begins with a conversational plea to passersby, asking if anyone has seen a charismatic young man who once visited regularly, immediately establishing a mood of intimate spiritual yearning (_firaq_).
    
- **Portrait of the Beloved:** The missing youth is vividly described as a playful, round-faced "trouble-maker" dressed in a vibrant red shirt, possessing effortless physical coordination, strength, and wit—symbolizing how divine love breaks down rigid human ego and brings radiant joy.
    
- **The Master Musician:** Always carrying a reed flute (_ney_) and a well-worn, polished pick in his pocket, the youth represents the Divine Beloved who awakens spiritual ecstasy and remembrance by breathing life into the hollowed-out human soul.
    
- **The Allusion to Prophet Joseph:** Elevating this lively portrait to a sacred level, the speaker compares the boy’s overwhelming radiance to Prophet Joseph (Hazrat Yusuf, peace be upon him), noting how the pride and worldly power of Pharaoh and all of Egypt collapsed before such unmatched beauty.
    
- **Patient Devotion:** The poem concludes with a declaration of selfless perseverance, as the seeker gladly commits to spending years waiting for even the faintest, third- or fourth-hand news of the Beloved—demonstrating that sincere spiritual longing (_talab_) is itself a profound blessing.
