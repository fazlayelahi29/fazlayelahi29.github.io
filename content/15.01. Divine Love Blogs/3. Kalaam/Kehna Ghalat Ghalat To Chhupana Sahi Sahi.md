# 3. Lyrics English Translation 


To speak what is false is wrong, but to conceal what is true is also wrong;
Tell me exactly what the messenger conveyed to you.

Your face looks pale this morning—
Tell me, where were you last night?

Holding my heart in his hands, he asks me,
"What price will you take for this?"—tell me truly.

Turn your gaze toward others, and pour a cup of wine for me;
I implore you, O cup-bearer, serve it properly.

O wine-seller, there is a crowd at your shop;
I, too, am a customer—show me your wares.

'Sajid' is yours, heart and soul—
Tell me, are you his, too?

# Synopsis 


At its heart, **"Kahna Ghalat Ghalat To Chhupana Sahi Sahi"** is a dramatic plea for unfiltered truth in love, moving from playful romantic suspicion to complete spiritual surrender.

In classical Urdu and Qawwali literature, poems like this operate on two levels at once: **Ishq-e-Majazi** (human, romantic love) and **Ishq-e-Haqiqi** (divine, spiritual love). Each couplet (_sher_) captures a distinct emotional stage of a lover confronting secrecy, jealousy, and devotion.


1. The Messenger’s Half-Truths (_Matla_)

> _kahnā ġhalat-ġhalat to chhupānā sahīh-sahīh_  
> _qāsid kahā jo us ne batānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"You are telling me lies, yet hiding the truth so carefully—O messenger, tell me honestly what my beloved actually said."_
- **Deeper Meaning:** In traditional Urdu poetry, lovers rely on a messenger (_qasid_) to carry letters and replies. Here, the messenger returns with a harsh rejection from the beloved and tries to soften the blow by fabricating a polite excuse. The lover sees right through the lie and demands the raw, painful truth rather than comforting illusions.

2. The Morning-After Interrogation

> _ye sub.h sub.h chehre kī rañgat uḌī huī_  
> _kal raat tum kahāñ the batānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"Why is the color drained from your face so early in the morning? Tell me truthfully, where were you last night?"_
- **Deeper Meaning:** This couplet shifts directly to the beloved. Seeing the beloved pale and exhausted (_rangat udi hui_) at dawn sparks immediate jealousy and heartbreak—a sign that they spent the night awake with someone else. The line blends teasing intimacy with the quiet agony of suspecting betrayal.

3. Putting a Price on the Heart

> _dil le ke merā haath meñ kahte haiñ mujh se vo_  
> _kyā loge is kā daam batānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"Holding my heart in their hand, they ask me: 'What price will you take for this?'—tell me truthfully."_
- **Deeper Meaning:** This highlights the cruel contrast between the lover’s total vulnerability and the beloved’s cold detachment. The lover has surrendered their entire soul, yet the beloved treats that heart like an ordinary object in a bazaar. Asking for its price (_daam_) is deeply ironic, because true devotion is priceless and cannot be traded.

4. Jealousy at the Tavern (_Mai-Khana_)

> _āñkheñ milāo ġhair se do ham ko jām-e-mai_  
> _saaqī tumheñ qasam hai pilānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"Go ahead and lock eyes with my rival while handing me the cup of wine—but I swear you to an oath, O cupbearer, pour mine properly."_
- **Deeper Meaning:**
    - **Romantic Layer:** Even if the beloved favors a rival (_ghair_) with affectionate glances, the lover begs not to be cheated out of their own rightful share of love.
    - **Sufi Layer:** In Sufi poetry, the **Saqi** (cupbearer) represents the Divine or a spiritual master, and **Mai** (wine) symbolizes spiritual enlightenment and divine love. The seeker prays that regardless of how much grace others receive, their own spiritual awakening remains pure and undiluted.

5. Demanding the Real Thing

> _ai mai-farosh bhiiḌ hai terī dukān par_  
> _gāhak haiñ ham bhī maal dikhānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"O wine-seller, there is a massive crowd at your shop; I am a genuine buyer too, so show me your real wares."_
- **Deeper Meaning:** Continuing the tavern metaphor, the poet steps forward as a serious seeker amidst a crowd of casual onlookers. Spiritually, the "shop" is the threshold of the Divine. While many people chase superficial rituals or worldly rewards, the poet demands authentic, life-altering spiritual truth (_maal_).

6. The Final Surrender (_Maqta_)

> _'sājid' to jān-o-dil se faqat aap kā hai bas_  
> _kyā aap bhī haiñ us ke batānā sahīh-sahīh_

- **What it actually means:** _"'Sajid' belongs exclusively to you with his life and heart—tell me truthfully, do you belong to him too?"_
- **Deeper Meaning:** In the closing couplet, the poet drops all metaphors of messengers, taverns, and rivals, signing off with his pen name (**Sajid**). After all the teasing, suspicion, and philosophical imagery, the entire poem strips down to the simplest, most terrifying question in human existence: _I have given you everything—is my love returned?_

Why "Batana Sahi Sahi" Repeats

The recurring phrase **"batana sahi sahi"** (_tell me truthfully / tell me straight_) acts as the emotional anchor of the poem. Whether addressing a messenger, a lover, a cupbearer, or God, the human heart constantly wrestles with uncertainty. In Qawwali performances (such as Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan’s rendition), repeating this phrase builds a hypnotic tension that mirrors a soul knocking relentlessly on the door of truth until an answer is given.


___ 

While the lyrics remain the same, **Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan’s** classic Qawwali rendition and **modern indie/fusion covers** (such as those by Sanam Puri, Jyotica Tangri, or contemporary acoustic artists) treat the poem as two completely different emotional experiences.

Nusrat turns the ghazal into a **transcendent spiritual dialogue**, while modern covers frame it as an **intimate, bittersweet romantic confession**.

Side-by-Side Comparison

|Dimension|Ustad Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (Traditional Qawwali)|Modern Covers (Indie, Pop & Acoustic)|
|---|---|---|
|**Core Perspective**|**Ishq-e-Haqiqi** (Spiritual/Divine Love): The beloved is often interpreted as the Divine or a spiritual guide (_Murshid_).|**Ishq-e-Majazi** (Earthly Romance): Framed strictly as a modern lover confronting a partner about loyalty and mixed signals.|
|**Emotional Arc**|**Playful -> Intense -> Ecstatic**: Starts with witty banter and builds through vocal improvisation into spiritual intoxication (_wajd_).|**Melancholic -> Reflective -> Vulnerable**: Maintains a steady, soft, and yearning mood from start to finish.|
|**Use of Repetition**|Uses _takrar_ (rhythmic repetition) of **"batana sahi sahi"** to mimic a soul knocking relentlessly on God's door for truth.|Repeats the hook cleanly as a catchy chorus, emphasizing romantic heartbreak and longing.|
|**Musical Arrangement**|Driven by the harmonium, tabla, and synchronous hand-clapping (_taali_) that accelerates the heartbeat.|Relies on acoustic guitars, soft synths, lo-fi beats, or minimal piano to create a personal, late-night vibe.|
|**Role of the Audience**|**Communal & Interactive**: Performed live for a crowd that responds with vocal praise (_wah-wah_) and ecstatic movement.|**Solitary & Introspective**: Designed for headphones, personal reflection, and social media reels.|

How Each Style Transforms Specific Verses

- **The Tavern Couplets (_Saqi_ & _Mai-farosh_)**:
    - **Nusrat’s Version**: The wine (_mai_) and cupbearer (_saqi_) are clearly felt as Sufi metaphors for divine knowledge and spiritual awakening. His soaring _taans_ (rapid vocal runs) make the tavern feel like a sacred gathering.
    - **Modern Covers**: These lines often feel like a literal late-night bar scene where a heartbroken lover is trying to drown their sorrows while watching their crush flirt with someone else.
- **The Final Couplet (_'Sajid' to jaan-o-dil se..._)**:
    - **Nusrat’s Version**: Delivered as a triumphant, fearless declaration of total surrender—the ego disappears completely into devotion.
    - **Modern Covers**: Sung softly, almost like a whispered text message sent at 2:00 AM, highlighting the fear of unrequited love.
