## 1. Definition, Etymology & Spiritual Philosophy

### 1.1 What is Qawwali?

**Qawwali** (pronounced _kuh-waa-lee_; Urdu: قوّالی) is the centuries-old tradition of Sufi devotional performance in the cultural history of **Islam** that originated in the Indian subcontinent in the late 13th century. Unlike the _ghazal_—which is a literary structure—Qawwali is a collective devotional performance traditionally intended to soften the heart, inspire remembrance of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** (_Dhikr_), express deep reverence for the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him), and encourage spiritual reflection in light of **Islam**.

  

### 1.2 Etymology (Origin of the Word)

- **Linguistic Root:** The word comes from the classical Arabic root _q-w-l_ (ق و ل).
    
- **Literal Meanings:**
    
    - **_Qaul_ (قول):** Means "an utterance," "a sacred saying," or "a statement of truth." In Islamic scholarship, _Qaul_ specifically refers to the sayings of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) or revered scholars and elders of **Islam**.
        
    - **_Qawwal_ (قوّال):** The intensive Arabic form meaning "one who speaks or repeats truth continuously"—the lead devotional reciter and vocalist.
        
    - **_Qawwali_ (قوّالی):** Literally "that which is uttered repeatedly by the _Qawwal_"—the melodic medium through which devotional poetry and spiritual reminders are proclaimed.
        

### 1.3 Core Spiritual Philosophy: _Sama_ (Spiritual Listening)

In classical Islamic mysticism (_Tasawwuf_ / Sufism), Qawwali is the South Asian expression of **_Sama_** (سماع)—literally "spiritual listening."

  

- **The Rational & Spiritual Premise:** Classical Sufi thinkers observed that the human soul (_Ruh_) was created by **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** and carries a deep, innate yearning to return to its Creator while navigating the trials of this temporary world. While dry intellect alone can sometimes struggle to soften a heedless heart, meaningful devotional poetry and rhythmic recitation—when kept purified of worldly vanity and ego—were viewed by Sufis as a means to awaken spiritual longing (_Ishq-e-Haqiqi_).
    
- **Not Mere Entertainment:** Traditionally, Qawwali was never intended as casual background music or worldly amusement. Rather than replacing formal obligatory worship (_Ibadat_) prescribed in **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah, it was treated within Sufi circles as a disciplined gathering of spiritual remembrance (_Dhikr_) where melody served strictly as a vehicle to carry moral and devotional words deep into the listener's heart.
    

## 2. Historical Origins & Evolution

### Phase 1: Early Sufi Roots in Baghdad & Persia (9th–12th Centuries)

- Long before Qawwali took shape in South Asia, early spiritual masters in Baghdad and Khorasan (Persia)—such as Junayd al-Baghdadi, Imam Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, and Jalaluddin Rumi—held _Sama_ gatherings, reciting devotional Arabic and Persian poetry to inspire love and mindfulness of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**.
    
- The **Chishti Order** (_Silsila-e-Chishtiyya_), founded in Chisht (modern-day Afghanistan) and brought to Ajmer, India by Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti in the late 12th century, placed regulated _Sama_ at the heart of its spiritual gatherings.
    

### Phase 2: The Birth of Qawwali — Hazrat Amir Khusrau (13th Century)

- **The Architect:** The formal creation of Qawwali is universally credited to **Hazrat Amir Khusrau** (1253–1325), a brilliant poet, scholar, musician, and the beloved disciple of the great Chishti Sufi saint Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi.
    
- **Cultural Synthesis:** Khusrau realized that to convey the peaceful ethical message of **Islam** and devotion to **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** among the local population of Hindustan, purely Arabic and Persian verses were not always accessible. He skillfully blended Persian poetic traditions with Indian classical melodies, rhythms (_talas_), and local _Hindavi_ / _Braj Bhasha_ dialects.
    
- **The _Qawwal Bachchon ka Gharana_:** Khusrau trained a group of young students in his specialized devotional style. Their descendants formed the _Qawwal Bachchon ka Gharana_ (the oldest surviving vocal lineage in South Asia), who have maintained this heritage at the shrine of Nizamuddin Auliya for over 700 years.
    

### Phase 3: The Era of the _Dargahs_ & Royal Patronage (14th–19th Centuries)

- Qawwali became a well-known cultural tradition at Sufi shrines (_dargahs_) across Delhi, Ajmer, Pakpattan, Multan, and the Deccan, particularly during the _Urs_ (the annual commemoration of a Sufi saint's passing—viewed symbolically in Sufi heritage not as a day of despair, but as the believer's return to the mercy and presence of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**).
    
- During the Mughal era, rulers patronized historical Sufi centers, further refining the classical melodic frameworks used in Qawwali.
    

### Phase 4: The 20th Century to Present (From Shrines to Global Stages)

- **Recording & Cinema:** In the 20th century, Qawwali moved beyond the courtyards of Sufi centers onto gramophone records, radio, and South Asian cinema.
    
- **Global Phenomenon:** Renowned vocalists like Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan and the Sabri Brothers carried traditional Sufi Qawwali to international concert halls in Europe, North America, and Japan, demonstrating that devotional poetry rooted in **Islam** could deeply move audiences regardless of language or background.
    

## 3. The Islamic Perspective & Classical Debate on _Sama_

To understand Qawwali from a balanced, rational, and grounded perspective in **Islam**, it is essential to examine how classical Islamic jurisprudence (_Fiqh_), guided by **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah, views this tradition:

  

### 3.1 The Historical Debate Among Scholars

**Islam** places immense importance on keeping worship pure, adhering to **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah, and avoiding distractions from the remembrance of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**. Because Qawwali involves musical instruments and heightened emotion, classical Islamic scholars held two primary viewpoints:

  

1. **The Majority / Strict Jurisprudential View (e.g., classical Hanbali, Shafi'i, Maliki, and mainstream Hanafi jurists):** The majority of classical jurists prohibited musical instruments (_Ma'azif_) based on authentic Prophetic narrations and principles derived from **the Holy Quran**, warning that music can distract the mind from **the Holy Quran**, stir lower desires (_Nafs_), or lead to excesses outside prophetic practice.
    
2. **The Sufi / Conditionally Permissive View (e.g., Imam al-Ghazali, Chishti & Suhrawardi masters):** Great Sunni scholars like Imam Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (in his monumental work _Ihya Ulum al-Din_) and Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya argued that melodic sound and rhythmic poetry—specifically excluding prohibited instruments and immoral contexts—are neutral tools like the human voice. If used for vanity or sin, they are strictly forbidden (_Haram_); if used with strict discipline solely to deepen love for **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** and His Messenger without violating the laws of **Islam**, they were considered permissible (_Mubah_) or spiritually stirring.
    

### 3.2 The Four Strict Conditions of _Sama_ (Set by Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya)

To ensure that devotional listening remained strictly within the ethical boundaries of **Islam** and never degenerated into worldly entertainment, Chishti Sufi masters established four mandatory conditions (_Shara'it-e-Sama_):

  

1. **_Zaman_ (Proper Time):** It must take place at a time when the participants' hearts are calm and focused, never interfering with obligatory prayers (_Salah_) or religious duties.
    
2. **_Makan_ (Proper Place):** It must be held in a clean, dignified, and spiritually respectful space—never in places associated with sin or immorality.
    
3. **_Ikhwan_ (Proper Company):** The gathering should consist of sincere, spiritually minded seekers—not people looking for cheap amusement or mockery.
    
4. **_Marboot_ (Proper Content):** The lyrics must be completely free of vulgarity, falsehood, exaggeration, or anything that contradicts **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah.
    

## 4. Thematic Hierarchy of Qawwali Lyrics

A traditional Qawwali gathering (_Mehfil-e-Sama_) is never random; it follows a strict, respectful thematic hierarchy reflecting the worldview of **Islam**:

  

1. **_Hamd_ (حمد):** Poems written exclusively in praise, glorification, and gratitude to **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**, the sole Creator and Sustainer of the universe—echoing the spirit of praise that opens **the Holy Quran**. Every authentic Qawwali session must begin with _Hamd_.
    
2. **_Naat_ (نعت):** Poems expressing deep love, reverence, and praise for the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him), describing his mercy, noble character, and prophetic mission.
    
3. **_Manqabat_ (منقبت):** Poems honoring the _Ahl al-Bayt_ (the Prophet's noble household, especially Hazrat Ali, may Allah be pleased with him), the Companions (_Sahaba_), and righteous scholars and spiritual elders of **Islam** (_Awliya Allah_, such as Shaykh Abdul Qadir Gilani or Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti), strictly within Islamic respect without attributing divine powers to any human being.
    
4. **_Ghazal_ / _Kafi_ (غزل / کافی):** Reflective Sufi poetry (often written by Rumi, Hafez, Amir Khusrau, or Bulleh Shah) exploring themes of longing for **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**, humility, self-purification, and spiritual surrender.
    
5. **_Munajat_ (مناجات):** Heartfelt supplications and prayers humbly begging **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** for forgiveness, mercy, and steadfast guidance.
    

## 5. Anatomy & Structure of a Qawwali Performance

A single Qawwali piece typically lasts between 15 and 45 minutes (and sometimes over an hour) and unfolds in a deliberate psychological and reflective arc:

  

### 5.1 The Ensemble (The Qawwali Party)

A traditional group consists of 8 to 12 men seated cross-legged on the floor in two rows:

  

- **Lead Singer (_Qawwal_):** Usually one or two master vocalists who carry the main verses, improvise melodies, and guide the emotional direction of the piece.
    
- **Side Vocalists (_Humnawa_):** Sit beside the lead singer, supporting him during complex vocal runs and taking over refrains so the lead can catch his breath.
    
- **Chorus (_Saazinda_ / Clappers):** Sit in the back row, singing the main refrain in unison and providing the driving rhythmic handclaps.
    

### 5.2 The Stages of a Performance

1. **_Alap_ / _Nagma_ (Melodic Prelude):** A gentle, meditative introduction establishes the classical melodic mode (_Raga_) and settles the audience's attention.
    
2. **_Rubai_ / _Girah_ (Introductory Verses):** Before starting the main piece, the lead vocalist recites short four-line verses (_Rubaiyat_) in free rhythm (without percussion) to introduce the moral or spiritual theme.
    
3. **_Aamad_ (The Entry):** The rhythm and handclapping enter steadily, launching the main composition (_Bandish_) at a measured, focused tempo.
    
4. **_Takrar_ (Repetition & Improvisation):** The core technique of Qawwali. The vocalist repeats a single powerful line numerous times—starting softly, then rising in pitch and intensity—allowing the meaning of the verse to sink past surface thought and into the heart.
    
5. **_Girah-Bandi_ (Knotting / Weaving Verses):** A hallmark of a scholarly _Qawwal_. In the middle of a poem, the vocalist seamlessly inserts a matching couplet in another language (e.g., weaving a Persian verse by Rumi or an Arabic phrase inspired by **the Holy Quran** into an Urdu Qawwali) to deepen the spiritual point, then smoothly returns to the main refrain.
    
6. **_Charhta_ (The Ascent / Climax):** The tempo accelerates, the handclaps grow intense, and the vocalists deliver rapid, intricate classical runs (_Sargam_ and _Taans_) until the piece reaches a powerful emotional peak and concludes with a humble prayer to **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**.
    

## 6. Instrumentation & Rhythmic Philosophy

Traditional Qawwali uses simple, portable acoustic elements chosen historically for projection and rhythmic unity (though conservative jurists in **Islam** prefer purely unaccompanied vocal poetry to remain safely within the strictest bounds of Islamic law):

  

- **Synchronized Handclapping (_Taali_):** The most iconic sound of Qawwali. In Sufi symbolism, the sharp, steady clapping represents the heartbeat of a believer absorbed in the remembrance of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** (_Dhikr_), keeping listeners anchored in attentive focus.
    
- **Harmonium:** A hand-pumped reed organ introduced in the 19th century that replaced older instruments like the _sarangi_ and _rabab_ because its sustained notes could easily support a large vocal chorus outdoors.
    
- **Tabla & Dholak:** Percussion instruments that provide a steady, driving rhythmic cycle (most commonly _Keherwa_ of 8 beats or _Dadra_ of 6 beats).
    
- **Absence of Heavy Electronic Noise:** Authentic traditional Qawwali avoids heavy electric guitars or synthetic beats so that the human voice and sacred poetry remain clear and unmistakable.
    

## 7. Cultural Etiquette & Spiritual States (_Mehfil-e-Sama_)

Attending a traditional Qawwali gathering follows strict ethical manners (_Adab_) rooted in the humility taught by **Islam**:

### 7.1 Audience Conduct

- **Respectful Posture:** Everyone sits together on the floor on carpets, symbolizing human equality before **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**—whether rich or poor, scholar or laborer.
    
- **_Nazrana_ / _Vel_ (Charitable Offering):** When a verse deeply moves a listener, they walk forward respectfully and place money near the lead vocalist. According to tradition, this money is not viewed as a worldly "tip" for entertainment; it is given as a charitable offering (_Sadaqah_) in gratitude to **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** for a moving spiritual reminder, which is later shared among the reciters and the needy.
    

### 7.2 Emotional & Spiritual States (_Haal_ and _Wajd_)

- **_Haal_ (Spiritual State):** A sudden wave of intense awe, gratitude, or tearful repentance before **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** that touches a listener during _Sama_.
    
- **_Wajd_ (Overwhelming Spiritual Emotion):** When _Haal_ becomes so deeply felt that a listener involuntarily stands up or weeps in humbled surrender. In authentic, sober Sufi gatherings grounded in **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah, fake, theatrical, or immodest displays are strictly condemned; genuine emotion is expected to remain restrained, sincere, and free from showmanship (_Riya_), with the vocalists calmly repeating the moving verse until composure returns.
    

## 8. Renowned Figures of Qawwali

### 8.1 Founding Poets & Spiritual Figures

- **Hazrat Amir Khusrau (1253–1325):** The historical father of Qawwali; composed timeless classics still recited widely, including _"Chhap Tilak Sab Chheeni"_, _"Man Kunto Maula"_, and _"Aaj Rang Hai"_.
    
- **Maulana Jalaluddin Rumi (1207–1273):** His Persian poetry, deeply rooted in **the Holy Quran** and **Islam**, forms the philosophical backbone of countless Qawwalis.
    
- **Baba Bulleh Shah (1680–1757) & Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai:** Wrote deeply reflective Punjabi and Sindhi Sufi poetry (_Kafis_) widely rendered in the Qawwali style.

## 9. Master Summary Checklist

- **What it is:** A collective Sufi devotional tradition (_Sama_) within South Asian **Islam**, historically intended to awaken love for **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala** (_Ishq-e-Haqiqi_) and remembrance of Him (_Dhikr_).
    
- **Origins:** Created in the 13th century in Delhi by Hazrat Amir Khusrau under the guidance of Hazrat Nizamuddin Auliya, blending Persian Sufi poetry with Indian classical melodies.
    
- **Islamic Framework:** Subject to classical scholarly debate in Islamic jurisprudence (_Fiqh_) regarding musical instruments, while Sufi authorities governed it by strict ethical rules (_Shara'it-e-Sama_) ensuring proper time, place, company, and pure lyrics aligned with **the Holy Quran** and Sunnah—moving in a respectful hierarchy from _Hamd_ (praise of **Almighty Allah Subhanahu Wa Ta'ala**) to _Naat_ (praise of the Prophet, peace be upon him) and _Manqabat_ (honoring righteous figures).
    
- **Performance Style:** Performed by a group ensemble (_party_) using harmonium, tabla/dholak, and synchronized handclapping, relying on heartfelt repetition (_Takrar_) and rising tempo to inspire reflection and spiritual humility (_Haal_).
