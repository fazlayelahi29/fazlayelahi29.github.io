### 2. CONCEPTUAL THEORY

The analysis of physical phenomena within electrical engineering and computational systems requires the formal categorization of signals. Signals are categorized as either continuous or discrete, depending entirely upon the nature of their independent variables. A continuous-time signal is represented mathematically as $x(t)$. Within this continuous framework, the independent variable, denoted as $t$, possesses the capacity to take on any real value along the continuous temporal axis. Such signals represent physical realities that exist at every infinitesimally small fraction of time, appearing conceptually as a smooth, unbroken line when mapped onto a Cartesian coordinate system. A sinusoidal wave generated by an analog oscillator serves as a primary example of this continuous-time nature.

  

In stark contrast to continuous phenomena, a discrete-time signal is formally denoted as $x[n]$. The foundational distinction lies in the independent variable, $n$, which is strictly constrained to only take integer values. Consequently, discrete-time signals are not defined for fractional or decimal values of time. When visualized graphically, these signals appear solely as a series of distinct points plotted on a graph, rather than a continuous, unbroken line. The data utilized to construct discrete signals is inherently quantized in the temporal domain. For instance, a sequence of daily stock prices is a classical representation of a discrete-time signal, as a singular value is recorded per integer unit of time (a day), with no mathematically defined values existing between these specific recorded instances. The significance of discrete-time signals within computational physics and digital signal processing cannot be overstated; these foundational sequences are crucial because they can be mathematically combined and manipulated to construct significantly more complex signals and to analyze the behavior of discrete systems.

  

Among the vast array of possible discrete-time signals, several specific types are recognized as elementary or fundamental building blocks. These include the unit sample sequence, the unit step sequence, the unit ramp sequence, and various real and complex exponential signals. The synthesis and operational manipulation of these standard discrete-time signals form the absolute basis of digital system analysis.

  

The most fundamental of all discrete sequences is the unit impulse sequence. The unit impulse sequence is alternatively classified within academic literature as the unit sample sequence or the Kronecker Delta function. This mathematical entity is universally denoted by the symbol $\delta[n]$. The definition of the unshifted unit impulse sequence is highly precise: the amplitude of the signal is exactly $1$ when the independent variable $n = 0$, and the amplitude is identically $0$ for all instances where $n \neq 0$. Mathematically, this is expressed as a piecewise function: $\delta[n] = 1$ for $n=0$, and $\delta[n] = 0$ for $n \neq 0$.

  

The concept of the unit impulse must be expanded to accommodate temporal shifting, thereby allowing the impulse to exist at arbitrary points along the discrete timeline. When the unit impulse sequence is subjected to a temporal shift by a discrete integer value $n_0$, the generalized mathematical expression is denoted as $\delta[n-n_0]$. The definition dictates that the shifted impulse evaluates to an amplitude of $1$ exactly when $n = n_0$, and evaluates to $0$ for all instances where $n \neq n_0$. This represents a single, solitary spike of unit magnitude occurring precisely at the discrete index $n_0$.

  

Following the unit impulse, the unit step sequence serves as the next fundamental building block. The unit step sequence is formally denoted by the symbol $u[n]$. Its mathematical definition establishes an asymmetric behavior relative to the origin of the temporal axis. The sequence evaluates to an amplitude of $1$ for all independent variable indices where $n \geq 0$, and evaluates to $0$ for all indices where $n < 0$. This creates a mathematical structure that effectively "turns on" at the origin and remains indefinitely activated at a unit magnitude for all positive future values of discrete time.

  

Similarly to the impulse, the unit step sequence can be subjected to a generalized temporal shift. The shifted unit step sequence is denoted as $u[n-n_0]$. Under this generalization, the transition point is shifted from the origin to the integer index $n_0$. The piecewise mathematical definition states that the sequence is equal to $1$ for all instances where $n \geq n_0$, and is equal to $0$ for all instances where $n < n_0$. The temporal shift parameter $n_0$ dictates the exact moment the sequence transitions from zero to unity.

  

The third foundational discrete sequence is the unit ramp signal. The unit ramp sequence introduces a linearly increasing amplitude over time and is denoted formally as $r[n]$. The mathematical expression of the unshifted unit ramp sequence dictates that the amplitude precisely mirrors the index value for non-negative indices; thus, the amplitude is exactly $n$ for all values where $n \geq 0$. Conversely, the amplitude remains $0$ for all instances where $n < 0$.

  

When the generalized temporal shift is applied to the unit ramp, the mathematical expression becomes $r[n-n_0]$. The definition of this shifted ramp sequence mandates that the amplitude evaluates to $n - n_0$ for all indices where $n \geq n_0$, and evaluates identically to $0$ for all indices where $n < n_0$. This produces a sequence that remains zero until the designated temporal index $n_0$, at which point it begins to increase linearly, with the amplitude always being equal to the distance from the shifting origin.

  

In order to construct these mathematical models within a digital computational environment, the concepts must be mapped into finite arrays of integer indices and corresponding amplitude vectors. # ---------------------------------------------------------
# Computation of the Unit Impulse Sequence
# ---------------------------------------------------------
def unit_impulse(n0, n1, n2):
    n = np.arange(n1, n2 + 1, 1)
    x = (n == n0)
    return x, n

n0 = 0
n1 = -5
n2 = 5
x, n = unit_impulse(n0, n1, n2)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.stem(n, x)
plt.title(r'$\delta[n]$')
plt.xlabel('n (index)')
plt.ylabel('x[n] (Amplitude)')
plt.grid(True, linestyle='--')
plt.xticks(n)
plt.show()

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# Computation of the Unit Step Sequence
# ---------------------------------------------------------
def unit_step(n0, n1, n2):
    n = np.arange(n1, n2 + 1, 1)
    x = (n >= n0)
    return x, n

n0 = 2
n1 = -5
n2 = 8
x, n = unit_step(n0, n1, n2)

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4))
plt.stem(n, x)
plt.title('u[n]')
plt.xlabel('n (index)')
plt.ylabel('x[n] (Amplitude)')
plt.grid(True, linestyle='--')

# Computation of the Unit Ramp Signal
# ---------------------------------------------------------
def unit_ramp(n0, n1, n2):
    n = np.arange(n1, n2 + 1, 1)
    x = (n - n0) * (n >= n0)
    return x, n

n1 = -5
n2 = 8
x, n = unit_ramp(n0, n1, n2)

### 2. CONCEPTUAL THEORY

Within the broad discipline of digital signal processing, the manipulation of discrete-time sequences involves mathematical operations applied either to the dependent variable (the amplitude) or the independent variable (the time index). When modifying the characteristics of a signal without altering its fundamental position on the temporal timeline, mathematical transformations are applied directly to the amplitude of the signal. The two most fundamental amplitude transformations are amplitude scaling and amplitude shifting.

  

Amplitude scaling is a linear mathematical operation wherein the magnitude of each individual sample within a discrete-time signal is multiplied by a defined constant scalar value. If a discrete-time input signal is denoted as $x[n]$, the resulting scaled signal, denoted as $y[n]$, is derived by multiplying $x[n]$ by a constant factor $A$. This mathematical relationship is formally expressed as $y[n] = Ax[n]$.

  

The scalar value $A$ dictates the physical classification of the scaling operation. If the absolute value of the scaling factor $A$ is strictly greater than unity ($A > 1$), the operation is formally classified as amplification. In physical electrical systems, amplification corresponds to an increase in the voltage or current magnitude of the signal, demanding an external energy source. If the absolute value of the scaling factor is strictly less than unity ($A < 1$), the mathematical operation represents attenuation. Attenuation compresses the magnitude of the discrete samples toward the zero-axis, corresponding physically to signal loss or forced dampening. In the specific case where the scaling factor is exactly equal to unity ($A = 1$), the output sequence is mathematically identical to the input sequence; in discrete hardware systems, this condition is referred to as an input follower or a buffer.

  

Furthermore, the sign of the scaling factor $A$ dictates the phase relationship. If $A$ is a negative scalar, the discrete-time signal undergoes a total phase inversion. Every positive amplitude is mathematically forced into a negative value, and every negative amplitude is inverted to a positive value. This results in the entire discrete waveform being physically flipped across the horizontal time axis, an operation often referred to as polarity reversal.

  

The second major amplitude transformation is amplitude shifting, universally referred to within electrical engineering literature as applying a Direct Current (DC) offset. While scaling involves multiplication, amplitude shifting involves the addition or subtraction of a constant scalar value to every discrete sample within the signal vector. If a discrete-time input signal is given by $x[n]$, the resultant offset signal, denoted as $y[n]$, is mathematically constructed by adding a constant $C$ to the signal. This operation is defined by the discrete equation $y[n] = x[n] + C$.

  

The addition of a positive constant $C$ physically shifts the entire discrete sequence upwards along the vertical amplitude axis. The overall morphological shape and the dynamic range (the peak-to-peak variation) of the signal remain completely undisturbed; solely the absolute mean value of the sequence is mathematically altered. Conversely, the addition of a negative constant (or subtraction of a positive scalar) forcibly shifts the entire discrete sequence downward across the amplitude axis. In practical applications such as analog-to-digital conversion, applying a precise DC offset is absolutely critical. Signals that swing bidirectionally into negative voltages must frequently be shifted entirely into the positive domain to adhere to the strictly positive voltage limitations of unipolar digital acquisition hardware. The application of the scalar $C$ mathematically achieves this necessary unipolar transposition without distorting the informational content embedded within the alternating fluctuations of the primary sequence.

  

In a computational environment, processing these mathematical transformations requires the application of scalar values to vast vectors of numerical data. Because discrete signals are instantiated in memory as highly structured, contiguous blocks of numbers (arrays), an efficient compiler relies on an architecture known as array broadcasting. When a computational command dictates the multiplication or addition of a singular scalar entity (like $A$ or $C$) against a complex, multi-element vector sequence (like $x[n]$), the numerical backend inherently replicates or "broadcasts" the scalar value across memory space so that the operation is executed individually and independently against every single localized index within the vector. This allows massive sets of discrete time samples to be transformed algebraically in a singular algorithmic step without the necessity for explicit, high-level sequential looping architectures.

### 2. CONCEPTUAL THEORY

In the domain of digital signal processing, a discrete-time signal is defined as a sequence of quantitative values wherein the independent variable occurs at discrete, uniformly spaced intervals. This sequence is mathematically represented as $x[n]$, where $n$ represents the discrete integer time index. Unlike continuous-time analog signals that exist for all real numbers in the time domain, discrete-time signals are solely defined at integer values of $n$.

  

A fundamental operation within this domain is time reversal, often referred to as signal folding. The time reversal of a discrete signal $x[n]$ is achieved by reflecting the sequence across the vertical axis (the origin where $n=0$). Mathematically, this operation transforms the independent variable $n$ into $-n$, producing the reversed sequence $x[-n]$. In this transformation, every discrete sample located at an index $n$ is transposed to the corresponding negative index $-n$. For instance, the amplitude value of the signal located at the index $n = 3$ is deterministically relocated to the index $n = -3$.

  

The symmetry properties of a signal constitute a vital analytical metric. Any arbitrary real-valued sequence can be definitively expressed as the linear superposition of two distinct sequences: an even sequence and an odd sequence.

  

An even signal, denoted as $x_e[n]$, is defined by absolute symmetry concerning the vertical axis at the origin. The mathematical condition for an even signal is expressed as:

  

$$x_e[n] = x_e[-n]$$

This equation asserts that the magnitude and polarity of the signal at any positive temporal index $n$ are strictly identical to the magnitude and polarity at the corresponding negative index $-n$.

  

Conversely, an odd signal, denoted as $x_o[n]$, exhibits anti-symmetry concerning the origin. The defining mathematical condition for an odd signal is:

  

$$x_o[n] = -x_o[-n]$$

This establishes that the signal's amplitude at index $n$ is the exact mathematical negation of its amplitude at index $-n$. A corollary of this definition dictates that for an odd signal, the amplitude at the exact origin ($n=0$) must inherently equal zero, as $x_o[0] = -x_o[0]$ can only be mathematically satisfied if $x_o[0] = 0$.

  

The fundamental decomposition theorem postulates that any arbitrary signal $x[n]$ is formulated by the simple addition of these two symmetrical components:

  

$$x[n] = x_e[n] + x_o[n]$$

To derive the independent equations required to computationally extract $x_e[n]$ and $x_o[n]$, the time-reversal operator must be applied to the entire formulation. When $x[n]$ is time-reversed, the resulting equation is:

  

$$x[-n] = x_e[-n] + x_o[-n]$$

By substituting the foundational definitions of even and odd symmetry ($x_e[-n] = x_e[n]$ and $x_o[-n] = -x_o[n]$) into the reversed equation, it simplifies to:

  

$$x[-n] = x_e[n] - x_o[n]$$

A system of linear equations is thus established. To isolate the even component $x_e[n]$, the original equation and the time-reversed equation are algebraically summed:

  

$$x[n] + x[-n] = (x_e[n] + x_o[n]) + (x_e[n] - x_o[n])$$

$$x[n] + x[-n] = 2x_e[n]$$

$$x_e[n] = \frac{1}{2} (x[n] + x[-n])$$

Similarly, to systematically isolate the odd component $x_o[n]$, the time-reversed equation is subtracted from the original equation:

  

$$x[n] - x[-n] = (x_e[n] + x_o[n]) - (x_e[n] - x_o[n])$$

$$x[n] - x[-n] = 2x_o[n]$$

$$x_o[n] = \frac{1}{2} (x[n] - x[-n])$$

In a computational environment, the sequences are stored as discrete, finite-length numerical arrays. In higher-order mathematical operations, a specific functional operator is deemed commutative if altering the spatial or chronological order of the operands engaged in the operation has absolutely zero effect on the ultimate computed outcome. Standard algebraic addition is commutative ($a + b = b + a$), and standard scalar multiplication is commutative ($a \times b = b \times a$).

  

Within the realm of discrete digital signal processing, an incredibly critical operation is convolution. Convolution acts as the primary mathematical foundation for analyzing linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, mathematically combining an input signal with an impulse response to determine the output. The operation of convolution is definitively characterized by the commutative property. The mathematical theorem establishes that convolving sequence $x[n]$ with sequence $y[n]$ is absolutely identical to convolving sequence $y[n]$ with sequence $x[n]$.

  

However, a highly similar mathematical operation known as cross-correlation fundamentally lacks this commutative parity. Cross-correlation is an analytical technique utilized in systems such as radar, active sonar, and digital communications to accurately gauge the structural similarity between two totally distinct signal waveforms as one is systematically shifted against the stationary anchor of the other.

  

For two deterministic energy sequences $x[n]$ and $y[n]$, the foundational equation for calculating the cross-correlation output sequence $r_{xy}[l]$ as a function of the temporal lag parameter $l$ is strictly defined by the summation equation:

This formulation dictates a highly specific sequence of operations. The first defined sequence $x[n]$ is maintained strictly stationary on the primary time axis. The secondary sequence $y[n]$ is subjected to a spatial shift dictated by the variable $l$. If the structural order of the mathematical operands is reversed, calculating the cross-correlation of $y[n]$ against $x[n]$, the notation is strictly defined as $r_{yx}[l]$. The corresponding summation equation structurally transforms into:

  

$$r_{yx}[l] = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} y[n]x[n-l]$$

  

In this secondary formulation, it is the sequence $y[n]$ that is maintained strictly stationary, while the sequence $x[n]$ is structurally shifted and swept across the axis.

Therefore, the resultant sequence arrays are definitively not equal:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] \neq r_{yx}[l]$$

This inequality represents the absolute failure of the commutative property.

  

While cross-correlation is fundamentally non-commutative, it is bound by an intrinsic geometric relationship. The cross-correlation sequence computed in one specific order is exactly equal to the mathematically time-reversed cross-correlation sequence computed in the opposite order. The theoretical proof relies on variable substitution. If the index mapping $m = n - l$ is substituted into the $r_{xy}[l]$ summation, the equation reformulates into $r_{xy}[l] = \sum x[m+l]y[m]$. This reorganized summation is precisely identical to the fundamental definition of $r_{yx}[-l]$.

  

Thus, the exact governing mathematical relationship is defined strictly as:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] = r_{yx}[-l]$$

### 2. CONCEPTUAL THEORY

The study of signals and systems often necessitates the decomposition of complex signals into fundamental, symmetric building blocks. This methodology facilitates mathematical analysis, particularly within the domains of Fourier analysis and system characterization. In discrete-time signal processing, a signal is mathematically represented as a sequence of numbers, denoted as $x[n]$, where the independent variable $n$ represents an integer index known as discrete time.

  

Symmetry in discrete-time signals is categorized primarily into even symmetry and odd symmetry. A signal is defined as possessing even symmetry if it is identical to its time-reversed counterpart. Mathematically, this condition is expressed as $x_e[n]=x_e[-n]$ for all values of $n$. Conversely, a signal is defined as possessing odd symmetry (or anti-symmetry) if its time-reversed counterpart is the exact negative of the original signal. This condition is formally expressed as $x_o[n]=-x_o[-n]$ for all values of $n$. A direct consequence of the definition of odd symmetry is that the signal amplitude at the origin index ($n=0$) must inherently be zero, because $x_o[0]=-x_o[0]$ can only be satisfied if $x_o[0]=0$.

  

A fundamental theorem in signal processing dictates that any arbitrary real-valued discrete-time signal $x[n]$ can be uniquely represented as the linear superposition of an even signal $x_e[n]$ and an odd signal $x_o[n]$. This relationship is expressed through the fundamental equation:

  

$$x[n]=x_e[n]+x_o[n]$$

To derive the expressions for isolating the even and odd components, the concept of time reversal, also known as signal folding, must be introduced. Time reversal involves reflecting the signal across the vertical axis at $n=0$. Applying time reversal to the fundamental equation yields:

  

$$x[-n]=x_e[-n]+x_o[-n]$$

By substituting the symmetric properties of the even ($x_e[-n]=x_e[n]$) and odd ($x_o[-n]=-x_o[n]$) components into the reversed equation, an alternate expression is formulated:

  

$$x[-n]=x_e[n]-x_o[n]$$

A system of linear equations is thus established. To isolate the even component $x_e[n]$, the equation for $x[n]$ and the equation for $x[-n]$ are added together. The odd components cancel out, resulting in $x[n]+x[-n]=2x_e[n]$. Rearranging this algebraic relationship provides the definitive mathematical definition for the even counterpart:

  

$$x_e[n]=\frac{1}{2}(x[n]+x[-n])$$

Similarly, to isolate the odd component $x_o[n]$, the equation for $x[-n]$ is subtracted from the equation for $x[n]$. The even components cancel out, resulting in $x[n]-x[-n]=2x_o[n]$. Rearranging this algebraic relationship provides the definitive mathematical definition for the odd counterpart:

  

$$x_o[n]=\frac{1}{2}(x[n]-x[-n])$$

The execution of these mathematical definitions programmatically requires two distinct signal operations. First, signal folding must be executed to generate $x[-n]$. Second, signal addition (and subtraction, which is addition with a negated signal) must be executed. A critical complexity in discrete-time computational processing is that signal addition is strictly an element-wise operation that must occur at identical time indices $n$. If an original signal $x[n]$ exists on a temporal domain from $n_{min}$ to $n_{max}$, its folded counterpart $x[-n]$ will exist on a mirrored temporal domain from $-n_{max}$ to $-n_{min}$. Convolution is the paramount mathematical operation in linear time-invariant (LTI) system theory. It describes exactly how a discrete-time signal $x[n]$ interacts with the impulse response of a system $h[n]$ to produce an output signal $y[n]$. The discrete-time linear convolution sum is mathematically defined by the infinite summation:

  

$$y[n] = x[n] * h[n] = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} x[k]h[n-k]$$

This mathematical formula dictates that one signal is time-reversed (folded), shifted by a variable $n$, multiplied point-by-point with the other signal, and summed across all independent integer variables $k$.

  

Similar to algebraic multiplication, linear convolution possesses several fundamental properties that drastically simplify complex system analysis, including commutativity, distributivity, and associativity. The associative property dictates that when three or more sequences are convolved, the grouping of the operations does not affect the final mathematically convolved output sequence.

  

Given three arbitrary sequences, $x_1[n]$, $x_2[n]$, and $x_3[n]$, the associative property is defined by the following equation:

  

$$(x_1[n] * x_2[n]) * x_3[n] = x_1[n] * (x_2[n] * x_3[n])$$

This property is profoundly significant in system engineering. It proves that if a signal is passed sequentially through two cascaded LTI systems with impulse responses $h_1[n]$ and $h_2[n]$, the overall system is entirely equivalent to a single system possessing an impulse response equal to $h_1[n] * h_2[n]$.

## PROBLEM 12: NON-COMMUTATIVITY OF DISCRETE-TIME CROSS-CORRELATION

### 1. PROBLEM STATEMENT

### 2. CONCEPTUAL THEORY

Cross-correlation is a mathematical tool utilized to identify similarities between two entirely different, independently originating signals as a function of the time displacement (lag) applied to one of them. While structurally similar to linear convolution, the core geometrical operation differs significantly. In convolution, one of the input sequences is inherently time-reversed (folded) prior to shifting and multiplication. In cross-correlation, time-reversal is completely omitted.

  

For two deterministic real sequences $x[n]$ and $y[n]$, the mathematical cross-correlation of $x$ with respect to $y$, denoted as $r_{xy}[l]$, is formally defined across lag parameter $l$ as:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n]y[n-l]$$

The objective is to analyze whether swapping the order of the operands alters the resulting mathematical sequence. Commutativity dictates that $r_{xy}[l]$ must equal $r_{yx}[l]$ for all values of $l$. The cross-correlation of $y$ with respect to $x$ is defined as:

  

$$r_{yx}[l] = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} y[n]x[n-l]$$

To analyze the relationship algebraically, an independent variable substitution must be enacted on the equation for $r_{xy}[l]$. Let a new independent summation variable be defined as $m = n-l$. Consequently, $n = m+l$. Substituting these variables into the foundational equation for $r_{xy}[l]$ transforms it to:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x[m+l]y[m]$$

By rearranging the multiplicands, this is rewritten as:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] = \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} y[m]x[m-(-l)]$$

Comparing this derived equation with the fundamental definition of $r_{yx}[l]$ yields an inflexible, defining mathematical relationship:

  

$$r_{xy}[l] = r_{yx}[-l]$$

This mathematical proof definitively dictates that the cross-correlation sequence $r_{xy}[l]$ is strictly the time-reversed (folded) version of the sequence $r_{yx}[l]$. Because a generic sequence is virtually never identical to its own time-reversed counterpart (unless the sequence itself possesses perfect even symmetry, which random sequences do not), it must be concluded theoretically that $r_{xy}[l] \neq r_{yx}[l]$. Thus, cross-correlation is fundamentally non-commutative. The order in which sequences are passed into a cross-correlation algorithm strictly matters.

  

To computationally verify this theory, the specific arrays provided must be anchored to correct independent variables based on the arrow markers.

For $x[n] = \{1, 2, 1, 1\}$, the arrow is under the first '1'. Therefore, the domain of $x[n]$ spans from $n=0$ to $n=3$.

For $y[n] = \{3, 5, 8, 13, 21\}$, the arrow is under the '5'. Therefore, the domain of $y[n]$ spans from $n=-1$ to $n=3$.
