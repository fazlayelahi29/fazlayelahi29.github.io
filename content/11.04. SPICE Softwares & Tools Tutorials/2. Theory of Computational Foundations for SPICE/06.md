# CHAPTER 6: COMPANION MODELING AND THE MATHEMATICS OF SPICE LINEARIZATION

## INTRODUCTION

The fundamental architecture of the Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis (SPICE) rests upon a strictly linear computational foundation. Developed originally at the University of California, Berkeley, the simulation engine relies on a set of linear equations governed by Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) and Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL), formulated mathematically as a sparse matrix mapping of conductances and source vectors . The problem, however, is that the reality of physical electronics is inherently nonlinear. Semiconductors, such as junction diodes, bipolar junction transistors (BJTs), and metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs), exhibit highly complex, exponential, and transcendental current-voltage characteristics that fundamentally cannot exist within the rigid confines of a linear algebraic matrix . 

The resolution to this paradox is the concept of "Companion Modeling," a rigorous mathematical discipline of translating complex, nonlinear, and dynamic semiconductor physics into transient, linearized topologies known as Norton equivalent circuits . By computing the partial derivatives of a device’s nonlinear equations at a specific operating point, the simulator effectively constructs a shadow network comprising simple, linear conductances and independent current sources. These elements are easily digested by the simulator's Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix . 

This volume provides an exhaustive mathematical exploration of companion modeling. The text details the Newton-Raphson linearization loop, the derivation of companion models for highly complex devices like the Gummel-Poon BJT, the behavior of reactive elements during DC bias point computation, and the critical fail-safes required to prevent matrix singularity. Furthermore, the volume bridges the gap between circuit theory and pure numerical methods, demonstrating the exact mathematical equivalence between the MNA matrix and the Jacobian matrix of a multivariate system. Through this definitive analysis, the underlying mechanics of modern circuit simulation are revealed.

## 6.1 THE NECESSITY OF LINEARIZATION

The Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix is the computational heart of SPICE. However, the architectural constraint of the MNA matrix is severe: it is a linear operator that only accepts scalar constants representing linear conductances and scalar constants representing independent source vectors . Given a linear system formulated as $\mathbf{A} \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}$, the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ contains the network's admittances, the vector $\mathbf{z}$ contains the known excitations, and the vector $\mathbf{x}$ contains the unknown node voltages and branch currents to be solved via sparse matrix factorization . 

If a circuit consists entirely of resistors, independent voltage sources, and independent current sources, the formulation is mathematically trivial. A resistor of value $R$ contributes a constant conductance $G = 1/R$ to the diagonal and off-diagonal elements of the $\mathbf{A}$ matrix, and the system is solved in a single mathematical pass. However, when a nonlinear component, such as a semiconductor diode, is introduced to the network, the direct formulation breaks down immediately . The current through a diode is an exponential function of the voltage across it. Because the current depends exponentially on the very unknown node voltages that the system is attempting to solve for, the relationship cannot be represented as a fixed scalar entry. The MNA matrix cannot process an algebraic equation; it can only process a finite numerical value.

This architectural bottleneck necessitates a structural transformation of the nonlinear device into a linear equivalent. The simulation engine replaces the highly complex, mathematically intractable physical transistor or diode with a purely theoretical, linear proxy network that perfectly mimics the nonlinear device at one specific, microscopic point of operation . This proxy network is the companion model. It consists exclusively of basic linear components: a parallel conductance ($G_{eq}$) and an independent current source ($I_{eq}$). Because these are purely linear elements, their values can be stamped directly into the $\mathbf{A}$ matrix and $\mathbf{z}$ vector, respectively .

The mathematical challenge lies in the fact that this linear proxy is only accurate for an infinitesimally small region around the chosen operating point. If the node voltages in the circuit shift by even a fraction of a millivolt, the original nonlinear device would exhibit a different current and a different internal dynamic resistance, rendering the static companion model totally inaccurate. Therefore, the SPICE engine cannot simply linearize the circuit once. It must engage in an iterative Newton-Raphson loop. At every single iteration of the solver, the simulator evaluates the complex nonlinear equations based on the voltage guesses from the previous step, recalculates the tangent line to the exponential curve, generates a brand new companion model with updated $G_{eq}$ and $I_{eq}$ values, and reconstructs the MNA matrix entirely from scratch . The necessity of continuous linearization is thus the driving force behind the immense computational overhead of circuit simulation, transforming a static matrix inversion into a dynamic, cyclical search for equilibrium.

## 6.2 THE DIODE COMPANION MODEL

To understand the mathematical genesis of a companion model, one must examine the simplest nonlinear semiconductor: the PN junction diode. The macroscopic current-voltage relationship of an ideal diode is dictated by the Shockley equation, a transcendental function that maps the non-ohmic conduction of majority carriers across a depletion region:

$I_D = I_S \left( e^{\frac{V_D}{n V_T}} - 1 \right)$

where $I_D$ is the diode current, $V_D$ is the voltage across the diode, $I_S$ is the reverse saturation current, $n$ is the emission coefficient, and $V_T$ is the thermal voltage (approximately 25.85 mV at 300 K) . Because the MNA matrix requires a linear relationship of the form $I = G V + I_{source}$, the exponential function must be linearized around a specific operating point, which represents the simulator's current numerical guess for the diode voltage during the $k$-th iteration of the solver, denoted as $V_D^{(k)}$ .

Linearization is achieved by executing a first-order Taylor series expansion of the Shockley equation around the operating point $V_D^{(k)}$. The expansion states that the value of the current function at the subsequent iteration, $V_D^{(k+1)}$, can be approximated by the value of the function at the current iteration plus the first derivative of the function multiplied by the proposed voltage step size :

$I_D(V_D^{(k+1)}) \approx I_D(V_D^{(k)}) + \left. \frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_D} \right|_{V_D^{(k)}} \left( V_D^{(k+1)} - V_D^{(k)} \right)$

The partial derivative of the diode current with respect to the diode voltage represents the incremental conductance, or the slope of the tangent line touching the exponential curve at that specific operating point. This derived term becomes the equivalent dynamic conductance of the companion model, $G_{eq}$:

$G_{eq} = \left. \frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_D} \right|_{V_D^{(k)}} = \frac{I_S}{n V_T} e^{\frac{V_D^{(k)}}{n V_T}}$

Substituting the newly defined $G_{eq}$ back into the Taylor series expansion yields a linear polynomial expression:

$I_D^{(k+1)} = I_D^{(k)} + G_{eq} \left( V_D^{(k+1)} - V_D^{(k)} \right)$

Distributing the conductance term across the voltage step provides a structural equation that identically matches the requirements of Kirchhoff's Current Law for a nodal matrix framework:

$I_D^{(k+1)} = G_{eq} V_D^{(k+1)} + \left( I_D^{(k)} - G_{eq} V_D^{(k)} \right)$

The term $G_{eq} V_D^{(k+1)}$ represents the current flowing through a theoretical linear resistor of conductance $G_{eq}$ driven by the newly calculated system voltage. The remaining grouped term, $\left( I_D^{(k)} - G_{eq} V_D^{(k)} \right)$, is entirely composed of known scalar constants derived from the previous $k$-th iteration. It represents a fixed, static current value. This constant term is defined as the equivalent independent current source of the companion model, $I_{eq}$ :

$I_{eq} = I_D^{(k)} - G_{eq} V_D^{(k)}$

Thus, the final linearized algebraic equation for the diode is:

$I_D^{(k+1)} = G_{eq} V_D^{(k+1)} + I_{eq}$

Structurally, this equation describes a dynamic Norton equivalent circuit: a linear conductance $G_{eq}$ in parallel with an independent current source $I_{eq}$ . The diode has been completely mathematically dismantled and replaced by these two linear components, allowing the numerical solver to embed the device into the system.

| Node Position | Contribution to MNA Conductance Matrix $\mathbf{A}$ | Contribution to Current Vector $\mathbf{z}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive Node ($N_+$) | $+G_{eq}$ | $-I_{eq}$ |
| Negative Node ($N_-$) | $+G_{eq}$ | $+I_{eq}$ |
| Off-Diagonal ($N_+, N_-$) | $-G_{eq}$ | 0 |
| Off-Diagonal ($N_-, N_+$) | $-G_{eq}$ | 0 |

For the duration of the $(k+1)$-th iteration, the MNA matrix is completely oblivious to the existence of a semiconductor. It only registers the presence of a resistor and a parallel current source . Once the sparse matrix solver calculates the new network node voltages, yielding $V_D^{(k+1)}$, the diode evaluation routines within the SPICE engine are called again to compute a new $G_{eq}$ and a new $I_{eq}$ based on the updated voltage, continually shifting the companion model closer to the true mathematical root of the nonlinear system.

## 6.3 LINEARIZING THE BJT GUMMEL-POON MODEL

While the companion model of a two-terminal diode requires a relatively straightforward substitution, linearizing a three-terminal Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) utilizing the advanced Gummel-Poon integral charge-control model introduces extreme mathematical complexity . The original Ebers-Moll static model treated the BJT as two interacting diodes, but the Gummel-Poon model captures highly nuanced physical phenomena such as high-level injection effects, base push-out (the Kirk effect), and Early voltage modulations by linking the terminal currents not just to the junction voltages, but to the total integrated majority carrier charge residing in the base region, $q_b$ .

The normalized base charge $q_b$ itself is a highly nonlinear, interdependent variable defined simultaneously by the internal base-emitter voltage ($V_{B'E'}$) and the internal base-collector voltage ($V_{B'C'}$) . The primary collector current $I_C$ in the Gummel-Poon model is formulated as the difference between forward and reverse current components, explicitly modulated by the inverse of this integrated base charge:

$I_C = \frac{1}{q_b} \left( I_S \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'E'}}{N_F V_T}} - 1 \right) - I_S \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'C'}}{N_R V_T}} - 1 \right) \right) - \frac{I_S}{\beta_R} \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'C'}}{N_R V_T}} - 1 \right) - I_{BC}^{rec}$

Simultaneously, the total base current $I_B$ is modeled as the sum of ideal diffusion currents and non-ideal space-charge recombination currents across both the forward and reverse junctions :

$I_B = \frac{I_S}{\beta_F} \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'E'}}{N_F V_T}} - 1 \right) + I_{SE} \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'E'}}{N_E V_T}} - 1 \right) + \frac{I_S}{\beta_R} \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'C'}}{N_R V_T}} - 1 \right) + I_{SC} \left( e^{\frac{V_{B'C'}}{N_C V_T}} - 1 \right)$

Because the BJT is a multi-port device with deeply coupled internal physics, its companion model cannot be represented by a single Norton equivalent circuit. Instead, the BJT is linearized into a massive, interconnected web of nodal conductances and voltage-controlled dependent current sources. This requires computing the multivariate Jacobian matrix of the terminal currents with respect to the terminal voltages. The four primary partial derivatives generated during this linearization process dictate the small-signal conductances that must be stamped into the MNA matrix .

| Conductance Parameter | Physical Definition | Mathematical Formulation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Input Conductance ($g_{\pi}$)** | The change in base current relative to the base-emitter voltage. | $g_{\pi} = \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial V_{B'E'}}$ |
| **Reverse Feedback Conductance ($g_{\mu}$)** | The change in base current relative to the base-collector voltage. | $g_{\mu} = \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial V_{B'C'}}$ |
| **Forward Transconductance ($g_{m}$)** | The change in collector current relative to the base-emitter voltage. | $g_{m} = \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{B'E'}}$ |
| **Output Conductance ($g_{o}$)** | The change in collector current relative to the base-collector voltage (Early effect). | $g_{o} = \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{B'C'}}$ |

Each of these derivations requires the rigorous application of the chain rule through the highly complex $q_b$ base charge equations, creating an immense computational burden during the simulation loop . Once the four core conductances are evaluated at the current iteration's operating point, the equivalent currents $I_{eq}$ must be established to balance the Norton representation for the multiple branches. For the BJT, two independent equivalent current sources are synthesized and injected to represent the residual static currents at the base and collector nodes:

$I_{eq,B} = I_B^{(k)} - g_{\pi} V_{B'E'}^{(k)} - g_{\mu} V_{B'C'}^{(k)}$
$I_{eq,C} = I_C^{(k)} - g_{m} V_{B'E'}^{(k)} - g_{o} V_{B'C'}^{(k)}$

The resulting companion model for the Gummel-Poon BJT is an extraordinarily robust matrix stamp . The internal base, collector, and emitter nodes of the transistor model are interconnected by these newly calculated conductances ($g_{\pi}$, $g_{\mu}$, $g_o$) and a voltage-controlled current source representing the forward gain ($g_m V_{B'E'}$), operating alongside the fixed equivalent independent sources ($I_{eq,B}$, $I_{eq,C}$) . To process a single BJT, the simulator must embed this entire interdependent sub-circuit into the global MNA matrix at every Newton-Raphson iteration. In large-scale integrated circuit topologies containing tens of thousands of transistors, the evaluation and stamping of these multi-port companion models exponentially scales the matrix complexity and strictly dictates the overall runtime of the simulation .

## 6.4 CAPACITOR COMPANION MODELS IN DC

The rigorous mathematical linearization detailed in the preceding chapters applies exclusively to static, memoryless nonlinearities such as semiconductor junctions. However, circuit simulation inherently involves energy storage elements—capacitors and inductors—which exhibit time-dependent differential equations that cannot be evaluated algebraically. Before a SPICE engine can perform a transient time-domain simulation or a small-signal AC frequency sweep, it must first establish the quiescent operating point of the circuit. This initialization process is known as the DC bias point calculation, commonly invoked via the `.OP` analysis directive . 

During the DC bias point calculation, the concept of time is strictly held invariant. The simulator mathematically forces the assumption that the circuit has been energized for an infinite amount of time, allowing all transient electrical phenomena to decay to a definitive steady state . Mathematically, this enforces the global condition that the derivative of any network variable with respect to time is precisely zero:

$\frac{d}{dt} = 0$

For a linear capacitor, the constitutive branch equation relates the current through the device to the time derivative of the voltage across it:

$I_C = C \frac{dV_C}{dt}$

Because the system is mathematically constrained to an absolute steady state in DC analysis, the derivative $\frac{dV_C}{dt}$ inevitably evaluates to zero, regardless of the magnitude of the capacitance $C$ or the static voltage $V_C$ resting across the plates . Consequently, the current through the capacitor is strictly forced to zero ($I_C = 0$). To construct a companion model for the capacitor under these specific static constraints, the simulation architect must evaluate its equivalent conductance. By analyzing the fundamental admittance of the component in the Laplace domain ($Y = sC$) and taking the limit as the complex frequency approaches zero ($s \to 0$ for DC), it is evident that the admittance—and thus the equivalent static conductance $G_{eq}$—is absolute zero:

$G_{eq} = \lim_{s \to 0} sC = 0$

An electrical component with an equivalent conductance of exactly zero allows zero current to pass, regardless of the externally applied voltage. This represents a perfect, ideal open circuit. Therefore, during the initialization of the MNA matrix for the DC operating point calculation, the simulator entirely excises capacitors from the matrix architecture . Their row and column entries in the conductance block remain unpopulated, effectively severing them from the companion model network. This topological truncation isolates DC current paths, ensuring that the Newton-Raphson solver converges strictly on the quiescent resistive equilibrium of the circuit without the mathematical interference of zero-valued state variables. The only exception to this architectural rule is if the user invokes the `UIC` (Use Initial Conditions) flag during a transient run, which entirely bypasses the steady-state DC matrix computation and forces the solver to inject predefined voltages directly onto the capacitive nodes .

## 6.5 INDUCTOR COMPANION MODELS IN DC

The topological dual of the capacitor is the inductor, an energy storage device whose unique behavior during the DC bias point calculation presents a highly distinct structural challenge to the Modified Nodal Analysis matrix framework. The constitutive branch equation for a linear inductor relates the voltage across the device to the time derivative of the current flowing through its coils:

$V_L = L \frac{dI_L}{dt}$

Applying the identical steady-state mathematical constraint required during the DC operating point calculation ($\frac{d}{dt} = 0$), the derivative of the inductor current evaluates to exactly zero . Consequently, the voltage across the inductor is forced to strictly zero ($V_L = 0$), regardless of the inductance $L$ or the magnitude of the steady-state direct current traversing the device. 

To deduce the companion model for the inductor in a DC state, the impedance of the component in the Laplace domain ($Z = sL$) is evaluated at the DC limit ($s \to 0$). The impedance approaches zero, mathematically indicating that the admittance, or equivalent conductance $G_{eq}$, approaches infinity:

$G_{eq} = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{1}{sL} = \infty$

A component possessing an infinite equivalent conductance represents an ideal short circuit, or a perfect lossless wire. However, attempting to insert an infinite value into a digital computational matrix immediately induces a catastrophic numerical overflow or a singular matrix exception within the solver . The floating-point architecture of the MNA matrix simply cannot harbor infinite conductances without suffering total algorithmic failure. 

To resolve this profound architectural incompatibility, SPICE treats the inductor identically to an independent ideal voltage source set to exactly 0 volts . In the strict framework of Modified Nodal Analysis, ideal voltage sources cannot be modeled simply by injecting a conductance into the standard $\mathbf{A}$ matrix, as their internal conductance is inherently infinite. Instead, they require the formulation of an entirely new, linearly independent branch equation . 

| MNA Matrix Modification | Implementation for Inductors in DC |
| :--- | :--- |
| Node Voltage Vector ($\mathbf{v}$) | Remains populated with standard nodal unknowns. |
| Branch Current Vector ($\mathbf{j}$) | Appended with a new unknown variable: the current through the inductor ($I_L$). |
| Voltage Constraint Equation | Appends a row forcing $V_{node+} - V_{node-} = 0V$. |

When the simulator encounters an inductor during the DC bias calculation, it generates a companion model consisting not of an infinite $G_{eq}$, but rather an augmented row and an augmented column appended to the global MNA matrix . This structural augmentation introduces the unknown inductor current $I_L$ as an entirely distinct state variable to be solved by the system, while simultaneously enforcing the rigid mathematical constraint that the voltage difference between the inductor's positive and negative topological nodes must exactly equal zero . Through this elegant structural manipulation, inductors are safely linearized as ideal 0V voltage sources, completely bypassing the infinite conductance anomaly while seamlessly preserving the vital DC continuity of the surrounding network.

## 6.6 UPDATING THE COMPANION MODEL

The derivation and stamping of a companion model is not a solitary initialization event; it is an aggressively iterative computational cycle that dictates the speed and stability of the entire software package. Because the linear proxy networks accurately mimic nonlinear semiconductor physics only at the exact coordinate of the tangential line approximation, the MNA matrix must be continuously destroyed, recalculated, and rebuilt . This cyclical process is governed by the Newton-Raphson algorithm, the numerical workhorse and mathematical backbone of the SPICE engine. 

At the initiation of the simulation, the engine assumes a generic, often purely heuristic, initial guess for all node voltages in the circuit (commonly defaulting to 0V). Utilizing these preliminary voltages, the simulator enters the primary software loop. For every single semiconductor device detailed in the netlist, the engine must execute highly complex device evaluation code to refresh the companion model . This intensive operation requires recalculating the partial derivatives—the $G_{eq}$ terms—and the residual equivalent currents—the $I_{eq}$ terms. In devices governed by the Gummel-Poon model or advanced sub-micron MOSFET models like BSIM3 and BSIM4, this step demands the serial evaluation of hundreds of complex transcendental functions, including exponentials, logarithms, and hyperbolic trigonometric functions, all appropriately scaled by temperature dependencies and intricate geometric parameters .

Once all individual $G_{eq}$ and $I_{eq}$ values have been freshly recalculated across the entire circuit topology, they are restamped into the MNA matrix, completely overwriting the previous iteration's linear network . The linear solver—typically utilizing a specialized sparse LU factorization algorithm to manage memory constraints—then inverts the newly formed matrix to calculate the subsequent set of node voltages . Modern variants of SPICE utilize advanced pivot selection strategies, such as Markowitz ordering or the specialized KLU solver, to maintain extreme matrix sparsity and avoid generating dense fill-in elements during the Gaussian elimination process, drastically accelerating matrix inversion times .

This massive computational overhead associated with model evaluation and matrix inversion constitutes the vast majority of CPU time consumed in circuit simulation . The Newton-Raphson loop repeats this cycle endlessly until the circuit officially achieves convergence. Convergence is strictly mathematically defined by the simulator's internal error tolerances. At the end of each matrix inversion, SPICE compares the newly calculated voltages and branch currents against those generated in the previous iteration. If the numerical delta between the iterations successfully falls below the relative tolerance limit (`RELTOL`, defaulting to 0.1% accuracy) and the absolute tolerance floors for voltage (`VNTOL`, typically 1$\mu$V) and current (`ABSTOL`, typically 1pA), the system is deemed mathematically stable and a root has been found . If these strict convergence criteria are not met, the new voltages immediately become the seeds for the next iteration, and the companion models are systematically updated once more. The engine's raw ability to swiftly and continuously update these thousands of companion models without numeric overflow is the defining metric of its algorithmic power.

## 6.7 CONDUCTANCE CLIPPING AND LIMITING

The iterative updating of companion models harbors a fatal mathematical vulnerability inherent to the Newton-Raphson method. Because semiconductor devices exhibit profound exponential current-voltage relationships, Newton-Raphson iterations can easily overshoot the true solution trajectory, proposing radical voltage swings that lead to computational catastrophe . This is particularly dangerous for forward-biased PN junctions where current rises asymptotically. 

Consider a standard diode evaluated at an early Newton-Raphson iteration. If the solver algorithm proposes a massive forward voltage step—for instance, blindly guessing that the node voltage jumps from an initial 0.7V to 5.0V—the Shockley equation evaluates an exponential parameter of $e^{5.0 / 0.025}$. This results in a number so astronomically large that it vastly exceeds the standard 64-bit floating-point dynamic range limit of the host processor . Consequently, the calculated equivalent conductance $G_{eq}$ approaches infinity. If a near-infinite conductance is stamped into the MNA matrix, the matrix loses all numerical conditioning, the condition number skyrockets, and the sparse linear solver immediately fails, triggering a singular matrix exception and aborting the simulation entirely .

To prevent immediate matrix failure, SPICE employs deeply integrated mathematical safety nets known as limiting algorithms . The most crucial of these is the `pnjlim` (PN Junction Limiter) function, a highly optimized C-routine that ruthlessly intercepts aggressive voltage updates before they are ever passed to the exponential evaluation functions . 

When the solver suggests a new junction voltage ($V_{new}$), the `pnjlim` function compares it to the voltage from the previous iteration ($V_{old}$) alongside a predefined critical voltage threshold ($V_{crit}$) . The critical voltage represents the mathematical inflection point on the exponential curve where the derivative begins changing so rapidly that standard linear extrapolation fundamentally breaks down. If $V_{new}$ exceeds $V_{crit}$ and represents a dangerously large delta from $V_{old}$ (specifically, changes exceeding $2 V_T$), the `pnjlim` algorithm overrides the Newton-Raphson solver . Instead of allowing the exponential function to explode, the algorithm severely limits the allowable voltage update, effectively forcing the voltage to climb logarithmically rather than linearly .

For modern MOSFETs, a parallel algorithmic limiter known as `fetlim` prevents similar catastrophic numerical overshoots in the gate-to-source voltage ($V_{GS}$) calculations, safeguarding the transconductance ($g_m$) bounds and protecting the inversion layer models . By artificially clipping the allowable voltage steps across nonlinear junctions, these limiting functions forcibly constrain the maximum possible value of $G_{eq}$ during volatile matrix iterations, rescuing the MNA matrix from singularity and coaxing the nonlinear system toward monotonic convergence. 

| Convergence Failure Strategy | Algorithmic Mechanism | Primary Use Case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Limiting (`pnjlim`/`fetlim`)** | Logarithmically clips voltage steps to prevent exponential overflow. | Default protection against singular matrices. |
| **GMIN Stepping** | Injects a microscopic parallel conductance (e.g., $10^{-12}$ S) across all nonlinear nodes, gradually reducing it to zero as the solver finds stable ground. | Overcoming floating nodes and extreme non-linearities . |
| **Source Stepping** | Ramps independent voltage and current sources from zero to full power in fractional steps, forcing the circuit to solve highly localized, incremental operating points. | Overcoming severe latch-up conditions or bistable states . |

If standard junction limiting ultimately fails to achieve convergence within the maximum iteration limit (`ITL1` for DC, `ITL4` for transient), the simulator automatically defaults to auxiliary stabilization algorithms like GMIN stepping or source stepping . These secondary heuristics manipulate the environmental variables of the companion models to forcefully drag the matrix into a well-conditioned state, proving that raw calculus must often be tempered by programmatic ingenuity to achieve robust simulation.

## 6.8 STAMPING THE COMPANION MODEL

With the companion model's equivalent conductances ($G_{eq}$) safely bounded and evaluated, and the equivalent independent currents ($I_{eq}$) precisely calculated, the mathematical abstraction must be physically embedded into the global system of equations. This highly procedural process is known as "stamping" . The Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix is traditionally partitioned into four distinct quadrants, typically denoted as block matrices $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$, and $\mathbf{D}$, paired with an unknown variable vector $\mathbf{x}$ and a known excitation vector $\mathbf{z}$ .

The comprehensive matrix equation is structured exactly as follows:

$\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A} & \mathbf{B} \\ \mathbf{C} & \mathbf{D} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{v} \\ \mathbf{j} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{i} \\ \mathbf{e} \end{bmatrix}$

The upper-left quadrant, the $\mathbf{A}$ matrix, is an $N \times N$ matrix (where $N$ represents the total number of circuit nodes, rigidly excluding the global ground reference node) dedicated exclusively to nodal conductances . When stamping a companion model's $G_{eq}$ that bridges node $n_1$ and node $n_2$, the simulator executes a localized, four-point operation: it adds the value $G_{eq}$ to the main diagonal elements located at $(n_1, n_1)$ and $(n_2, n_2)$, and it subtracts $G_{eq}$ from the off-diagonal symmetric elements located at $(n_1, n_2)$ and $(n_2, n_1)$ . This specific geometric placement explicitly enforces Kirchhoff's Current Law, mathematically ensuring that current leaving node $n_1$ perfectly matches the current entering node $n_2$.

The dependent and independent equivalent current sources derived from the companion models, $I_{eq}$, are stamped directly into the upper half of the right-hand side excitation vector, $\mathbf{i}$. If the companion current $I_{eq}$ flows from node $n_1$ to node $n_2$, its scalar value is subtracted from the $n_1$-th row of the vector $\mathbf{i}$ and added to the $n_2$-th row .

The remaining quadrants of the MNA matrix—$\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$, and $\mathbf{D}$—are aggressively mobilized to handle devices that simply cannot be represented as standard node-to-node conductances, specifically those components requiring explicit voltage branch constraints . 

| Component Type | Matrix Quadrants Utilized | Stamp Methodology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Resistors / $G_{eq}$** | $\mathbf{A}$ Matrix | Populates diagonal and off-diagonal entries. |
| **Independent Voltage Sources** | $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$ Matrices | Appends $+1$ and $-1$ to represent node connections; adds unknown branch current to $\mathbf{j}$ vector . |
| **Voltage-Controlled Current Sources (VCCS)** | $\mathbf{A}$ Matrix | Transconductance value stamped directly into off-diagonal conductance entries based on controlling nodes . |
| **Current-Controlled Voltage Sources (CCVS)** | $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{C}$, $\mathbf{D}$ Matrices | Requires complex constraint mapping; transresistance value stamped into the $\mathbf{D}$ matrix quadrant . |

The $\mathbf{B}$ matrix (dimension $N \times M$, where $M$ is the number of independent voltage branches) and its transpose counterpart $\mathbf{C}$ are populated with discrete $+1$ and $-1$ integers to stamp the topological connectivity of voltage sources, ideal inductors, and voltage-controlled dependent sources . The $\mathbf{D}$ matrix (dimension $M \times M$) accommodates the transresistance of Current-Controlled Voltage Sources (CCVS) . Through this rigorous, quadrant-based stamping methodology, the simulator elegantly fuses the disparate linear components of thousands of distinct companion models into a single, cohesive, computationally solvent matrix equation that perfectly reflects the physical circuit topography.

## 6.9 THE JACOBIAN AS A CONDUCTANCE MATRIX

While the stamping of companion models into quadrants is highly intuitive from the graphical perspective of electrical circuit theory, viewing the MNA matrix purely as an assembly of physical conductances obscures a profound and beautiful mathematical truth. To a numerical analyst viewing the source code of the simulation engine, there are no resistors, no ground nodes, and no circuits; there is only a multivariable system of nonlinear algebraic equations to be solved . The profound brilliance of the SPICE architecture lies in the exact mathematical congruence between the physical MNA matrix and the algorithmic Jacobian matrix of the generalized Newton-Raphson method .

To deeply reveal this connection, consider a nonlinear network formulated entirely through nodal KCL equations. Let the vector $\mathbf{v}$ represent the set of all unknown node voltages. The sum of all currents leaving each arbitrary node must equal zero, yielding a dense system of nonlinear functions:

$\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}$

The Newton-Raphson algorithm solves this immense multidimensional root-finding problem by employing the Jacobian matrix, $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{v})$, which is defined strictly as the matrix of all first-order partial derivatives of the vector function $\mathbf{f}$ evaluated at the current iterative guess $\mathbf{v}^{(k)}$ . The iterative update is mathematically determined by solving the linear system:

$\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{v}^{(k)}) \Delta \mathbf{v}^{(k)} = -\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v}^{(k)})$

where the delta vector $\Delta \mathbf{v}^{(k)} = \mathbf{v}^{(k+1)} - \mathbf{v}^{(k)}$ represents the required voltage step vector traversing the solution space .

Examine the theoretical construction of the Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{J}$. Its entry at the $i$-th row and $j$-th column is strictly $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial v_j}$, representing the instantaneous rate of change of the net current leaving node $i$ with respect to a minute change in the voltage at node $j$. In the discipline of circuit physics, the change in current with respect to a change in voltage is the very literal definition of small-signal conductance . 

When the SPICE engine calculates $G_{eq}$ for a simple diode, or the heavily parameterized $g_{\pi}$ for a Gummel-Poon BJT, by taking the partial derivative of the physical device equations, it is literally calculating the exact entries of the Jacobian matrix . The physical act of "stamping" $G_{eq}$ into the positive diagonals and negative off-diagonals of the MNA matrix is functionally and algorithmically identical to systematically populating the $\mathbf{J}$ matrix . Furthermore, the equivalent companion current vector $I_{eq}$ stamped into the right-hand side is mathematically identical to rearranging the residual error vector $-\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{v}^{(k)})$ and isolating the linear terms . Therefore, the MNA matrix filled with physical companion conductances is not merely an engineering approximation of the Jacobian; it is the exact, unadulterated Jacobian matrix. This elegant duality seamlessly bridges the physical intuition of electrical engineering with the rigorous calculus of multivariate numerical root-finding.

## 6.10 BRIDGING DC TO TRANSIENT

The exhaustive linearization architecture designed to establish the quiescent DC operating point is brilliantly conserved when the simulator ultimately transitions into the time domain for transient analysis. While the DC companion models forcefully drive energy storage elements into topological extremes—creating perfect open circuits for capacitors and ideal 0V sources for inductors—transient simulation requires a companion model that accurately captures dynamic memory over discrete, sequential time steps . Remarkably, this is achieved using the exact same topological proxy used for nonlinear semiconductors: a dynamic Norton equivalent circuit comprising a parallel $G_{eq}$ and $I_{eq}$ .

To evaluate transient behavior, continuous-time differential equations must be strictly discretized using specialized numerical integration algorithms, predominantly the Trapezoidal rule, Backward Euler (BE), or Gear's second-order methods . Consider the linear capacitor differential equation $I = C \frac{dV}{dt}$. Applying the highly robust Backward Euler integration method to approximate the derivative at discrete time $t_{k+1}$ relative to the immediate past time step $t_k$, with a defined step size of $\Delta t = t_{k+1} - t_k$, yields the algebraic difference equation:

$I^{(k+1)} = C \frac{V^{(k+1)} - V^{(k)}}{\Delta t}$

Rearranging this algebraic equation into the standard linear nodal form $I = G V + I_{source}$ allows the simulation engine to extract the precise companion model components immediately:

$I^{(k+1)} = \left( \frac{C}{\Delta t} \right) V^{(k+1)} - \left( \frac{C}{\Delta t} \right) V^{(k)}$

The scalar term multiplying the unknown current voltage $V^{(k+1)}$ defines the equivalent transient conductance to be stamped into the $\mathbf{A}$ matrix:

$G_{eq} = \frac{C}{\Delta t}$

The remaining term contains the known historical voltage preserved from the previous time step, forming the equivalent historical current source to be stamped into the excitation vector :

$I_{eq} = - \frac{C}{\Delta t} V^{(k)}$

This revelation dictates that in transient analysis, the companion models no longer merely represent the geometric slope of a nonlinear static I-V curve; they actively embody the numerical integration of time itself. The equivalent conductance $G_{eq}$ of a capacitor is inversely proportional to the integration time step $\Delta t$, meaning as time steps become microscopically small to track rapid switching transients or digital clock edges, the capacitor behaves increasingly like a high-conductance short circuit . The equivalent current $I_{eq}$ serves as the device's electrical memory, carrying the inertial state of the previous time step directly into the current matrix evaluation . 

To maintain accuracy, the simulator continuously monitors the Local Truncation Error (LTE), which estimates the divergence between the actual continuous curve and the discretized approximation (e.g., comparing Trapezoidal predictions against actual evaluations) . If the LTE exceeds predefined tolerance bounds, the engine forcefully rejects the time step, drastically reduces $\Delta t$, recalculates $G_{eq}$ and $I_{eq}$ for all reactive elements, and re-solves the matrix . The simulator stamps this transient $G_{eq}$ and historical $I_{eq}$ directly into the MNA matrix using the exact same quadrant mapping rules utilized for static nonlinear diodes and complex BJTs . By unifying device nonlinearities, differential equations, and dynamic numerical integration beneath the singular mathematical umbrella of companion modeling, SPICE achieves profound architectural harmony. The complex calculus of temporal physics and the exponential behavior of quantum semiconductors are entirely abstracted away, leaving only a massive, dynamic, linear matrix—a mathematical engine uniquely capable of simulating the boundaries of the physical world.
