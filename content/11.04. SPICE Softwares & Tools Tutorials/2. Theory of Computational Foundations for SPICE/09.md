# CHAPTER 9: THE FREQUENCY DOMAIN - FREEZING TIME WITH COMPLEX MODIFIED NODAL ANALYSIS

## INTRODUCTION 

The mathematical simulation of electronic circuits inherently presents a fundamental dichotomy between the transient, time-domain reality of physical electrons and the analytical elegance of frequency-domain steady-state approximations. In the preceding volumes of this definitive textbook, the computational focus remained strictly fastened on the time domain. Utilizing numerical integration algorithms—specifically the Backward Euler, Trapezoidal, and Gear methods—the Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis (SPICE) engine meticulously computed the progression of voltages and currents across discrete, sequential time steps ($\Delta t$) . While transient analysis represents the most physically accurate approximation of a non-linear circuit's operation in real time, it is computationally exhaustive. Time-domain simulation is heavily burdened by initialization constraints, truncation errors, numerical artifacts, and the constant threat of Newton-Raphson convergence failures at every time step . 

The frequency domain offers a profound and necessary computational paradigm shift. By completely abandoning the chronological concept of time, the SPICE engine transforms ordinary differential equations (ODEs) into purely linear algebraic equations . Volume 9 deeply explores this transformation, dissecting the precise mathematical mechanisms by which SPICE suspends the progression of time, linearizes highly complex semiconductor physics at a specific thermodynamic and electrical bias point, and executes the Small-Signal Alternating Current (AC) Analysis. This volume will exhaustively detail the transition from the real numbers of the Direct Current (DC) Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix to the massive complex-number architectures required to model phase shifts and reactive energy storage . By mastering the core algorithms of complex LU factorization, frequency sweeping routines, stochastic noise analysis, and pole-zero eigenvalue extraction, the simulation architect gains an intimate understanding of both the immense computational power and the strict theoretical limitations of small-signal AC analysis.

## 9.1 ABANDONING TIME

The fundamental premise of AC analysis in SPICE is the complete, permanent, and mathematical removal of the temporal variable ($t$) and its differential counterpart ($\Delta t$). In a standard transient simulation, energy storage elements such as capacitors and inductors dictate the dynamic, time-varying behavior of the network through differential equations. The continuous current through a capacitor is governed by the physical law $i(t) = C \frac{dv(t)}{dt}$, and the continuous voltage across an inductor is defined by $v(t) = L \frac{di(t)}{dt}$ . To solve these differential equations in the time domain, the SPICE engine must rely on implicit numerical integration, dynamically converting the continuous calculus derivative into a discrete algebraic difference equation using a continually adjusted time step . 

AC analysis fundamentally bypasses this arduous numerical integration by explicitly assuming the circuit is operating in a perfect, uninterrupted sinusoidal steady state . Under this strict mathematical assumption, every voltage and current waveform in the network is a sinusoidal wave oscillating at a single, identical angular frequency ($\omega = 2\pi f$). Because the driving frequency is uniform and continuous across the entire linear network, the chronological progression of time becomes completely irrelevant to the shape and mathematical relationship of the waveforms. Instead of tracking the wave's amplitude at varying moments in time, the simulator only needs to track two static parameters: the absolute magnitude of the wave, and its phase shift relative to the original input stimulus .

Mathematically, this elimination of time is achieved by transforming the circuit equations from the time domain directly into the frequency domain using the Laplace transform variable, $s$. The complex frequency variable is canonically defined as $s = \sigma + j\omega$, where $\sigma$ represents exponential decay or growth, and $\omega$ represents the continuous oscillatory angular frequency . For standard AC analysis, the system is assumed to be in a perfect steady state, meaning there is absolutely no transient growth, decay, or initial condition ringing, thereby mathematically enforcing $\sigma = 0$. Consequently, the Laplace variable collapses purely to the imaginary axis, becoming exactly $s = j\omega$ . 

By adopting this substitution, the cumbersome time derivative operator $\frac{d}{dt}$ is directly and elegantly replaced by simple algebraic multiplication with the imaginary operator $j\omega$. The integral operator $\int dt$ is analogously replaced by mathematical division by $j\omega$ . This elegant substitution instantaneously eliminates all differential equations from the matrix solver. The continuous, flowing timeline of the circuit is essentially "frozen" into a static mathematical snapshot of sinusoidal behavior, allowing the simulator to solve the network algebraically for any arbitrary frequency $f$ without ever having to chronologically step through time to observe the wave's physical propagation. 

## 9.2 THE PREREQUISITE DC OPERATING POINT

Despite its absolute mathematical independence from time, AC analysis cannot exist in a vacuum. It is wholly, inescapably, and computationally dependent upon a preceding Direct Current (DC) operating point calculation (`.OP`). A frequent misconception among novice engineers is that AC analysis simply injects a sinusoidal wave into the schematic and dynamically measures the output . In reality, the AC analysis engine does not even begin to initialize its matrices until the non-linear DC solver has completely converged upon a stable, static bias point for the entire network . 

The absolute necessity of the DC operating point stems from the inherently non-linear physical nature of real-world circuit components. Diodes, Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJTs), and Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors (MOSFETs) possess highly non-linear, exponential current-voltage (I-V) characteristics . In the time domain, a large voltage swing applied to a MOSFET's gate will cause the device to transition dynamically and non-linearly through the cutoff, linear, and saturation operational regions. However, because AC analysis algebraically assumes a perfectly linear network, these non-linear behaviors cannot be directly processed in the frequency domain . 

To bridge this computational gap, SPICE utilizes the DC operating point to establish the exact, static electrical state of every node and branch in the circuit prior to the application of any AC stimulus. The DC solver shorts all inductors to $0\ \Omega$ and opens all capacitors to $\infty\ \Omega$, utilizing the iterative Newton-Raphson algorithm to solve the highly non-linear semiconductor equations . Once the DC matrix converges to a residual error below the relative tolerance limit (`RELTOL`), the exact DC voltages across all device terminals are mathematically "locked" in the simulator's memory space.

These locked DC terminal voltages are then fed directly into the respective highly complex device models (such as BSIM3, BSIM4, or the symmetric BSIM-SOI model) . The device models calculate the localized small-signal parameters for that specific thermodynamic and electrical bias state . For example, in advanced Silicon-On-Insulator (SOI) models, the DC solver computes the exact surface potential mid-points and inversion charge densities ($q_{im}$), allowing the simulator to accurately model the smooth dynamic depletion (DD) transition between partial depletion (PD) and full depletion (FD) regimes . Without the precise, converged resolution of the DC operating point, the fundamental parameters that dictate the linear AC behavior of the transistors would be unknown, rendering the frequency sweep mathematically impossible.

## 9.3 FREEZING NONLINEAR MODELS

Once the DC operating point is firmly established and the small-signal parameters are successfully extracted, the SPICE engine executes the most crucial conceptual step of the AC analysis: freezing the non-linear models. This process is formally known in computational numerical methods as linearization. AC analysis is strictly a small-signal simulation, meaning it operates under the core mathematical assumption that the applied AC stimulus is infinitesimally small—so small that it does not measurably perturb or shift the established DC operating point . 

Linearization is achieved by executing a first-order Taylor series expansion of the non-linear device equations, centered exactly at the established DC bias point . Consider a non-linear voltage-controlled current source representing the drain current of a MOSFET, where $I_d = f(V_{gs}, V_{ds})$. The rigorous Taylor series expansion around the DC operating voltages ($V_{GSQ}, V_{DSQ}$) is mathematically given by:

$I_d(V_{gs}, V_{ds}) = I_d(V_{GSQ}, V_{DSQ}) + \frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_{gs}} \bigg|_{Q} \cdot v_{gs} + \frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_{ds}} \bigg|_{Q} \cdot v_{ds} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 I_d}{\partial V_{gs}^2} \bigg|_{Q} \cdot v_{gs}^2 + O(v^3)$

In the small-signal AC domain, the massive DC offset current $I_d(V_{GSQ}, V_{DSQ})$ is immediately discarded because AC analysis only tracks the dynamic change in the signal, not the absolute magnitude . Furthermore, because the AC variations ($v_{gs}, v_{ds}$) are assumed to be infinitesimally small, all higher-order terms ($v_{gs}^2, O(v^3)$ and beyond) approach zero exponentially faster than the linear terms, and are therefore mathematically truncated and permanently ignored by the solver. 

What remains is the pure, first-order partial derivative—the Jacobian tangent—evaluated at the exact DC operating point . The complex partial derivatives are permanently transformed into static linear constants. The term $\frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_{gs}}$ becomes the forward transconductance $g_m$, and $\frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_{ds}}$ becomes the output conductance $g_{ds}$. By applying this symmetric charge linearization method, SPICE ensures error-free representation of higher-order harmonics at the boundary of $V_{ds} = 0$, guaranteeing a smooth AC derivative .

Through this mathematical linearization, the SPICE engine completely eradicates the complex, computationally expensive semiconductor physics equations from the active memory. A highly non-linear BSIM transistor, which typically requires tens of thousands of lines of C-code to evaluate its surface potentials and velocity saturation factors, is literally replaced by a static, linear equivalent circuit consisting solely of ideal linear resistors, linear voltage-controlled current sources (VCCS), and fixed linear capacitors . The physical device is effectively "frozen" at its DC tangent. This frozen linear network is what is passed forward to the AC solver, guaranteeing that the subsequent matrix equations will be purely linear and solvable in a single deterministic pass, entirely free from the necessity of iterative Newton-Raphson convergence loops .

## 9.4 REPLACING REACTANCE WITH COMPLEX PHASORS

Having linearized the active semiconductor devices into static resistive and transconductive equivalents, the SPICE engine must address the passive energy storage elements: capacitors and inductors. In the DC analysis, these elements were effectively removed from the matrix to find the quiescent state . In the AC analysis, they return to dominate the system's frequency-dependent behavior, but they do so not through differential calculus, but through complex phasor arithmetic.

The transition to phasors is fundamentally rooted in Euler's formula, which bridges real sinusoidal oscillations to the complex plane: $e^{j\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) + j\sin(\omega t)$. By operating exclusively in the complex plane, the simulator can elegantly track both the amplitude and the precise phase shift of a sinusoidal signal simultaneously, utilizing a single complex number composed of a real part (conductance) and an imaginary part (susceptance).

For a capacitor, the time-domain differential relation $i = C \frac{dv}{dt}$ is subjected to the Laplace substitution $d/dt \rightarrow j\omega$ . This yields the phasor domain algebraic equation $I = j\omega C \cdot V$. Rearranging this to find the equivalent AC conductance (admittance, $Y = I/V$), we find that the capacitor is stamped into the simulation matrix as a purely imaginary conductance:

$Y_C = j\omega C$

Similarly, the inductor's time-domain relation $v = L \frac{di}{dt}$ mathematically transforms into $V = j\omega L \cdot I$. In admittance form, this is expressed as $Y_L = \frac{1}{j\omega L}$, which standardizes to:

$Y_L = -j \frac{1}{\omega L}$

Mutual inductance between coupled magnetic elements ($M$) is also accommodated natively in this complex matrix framework. The induced voltage $V_1 = j\omega M \cdot I_2$ allows SPICE to construct dense coupling matrices that reflect the inductive crosstalk between physical printed circuit board traces, wire bonds, or on-chip transformer windings . Advanced Partial Element Equivalent Circuit (PEEC) models utilize these explicit mutual inductance matrices to capture far-off inductive couplings without explicitly running Maxwell field solvers, stamping the $j\omega M$ values directly into the imaginary domain of the circuit simulator . Furthermore, lossy transmission line structures (such as the Star-Hspice U-element) natively utilize these complex inductance matrices to model skin-effect losses and ground plane reference inductance as a function of the frequency variable $f$ .

The fundamental introduction of the imaginary operator $j$ is the exact mathematical mechanism by which SPICE tracks physical phase shifts. Multiplying a signal vector by $j$ mathematically rotates the phasor by exactly $90^{\circ}$ ($\pi/2$ radians) counter-clockwise in the complex plane. Therefore, the presence of $j\omega C$ in the matrix natively enforces the physical reality that the current through a capacitor leads the voltage by $90^{\circ}$. By substituting all time-domain reactances with their complex phasor equivalents, the SPICE engine successfully transforms the dynamic, time-varying physical circuit into a static algebraic network composed entirely of complex-valued conductances .

## 9.5 THE COMPLEX MNA MATRIX FORMULATION

With all non-linear components frozen into linear equivalents and all reactive components transformed into complex phasors, the SPICE algorithm must assemble these discrete mathematical elements into a unified, solvable system of linear equations. This assembly is accomplished using Complex Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA), the undisputed mathematical backbone of modern electronic circuit simulation .

In early simulation architectures, standard Nodal Analysis (NA) was utilized, which strictly enforced Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) at every node. However, standard NA fails completely when confronted with ideal voltage sources or inductors, because their branch currents are unknown variables that cannot be expressed purely in terms of nodal voltages . Modified Nodal Analysis resolves this limitation by elegantly appending the unknown branch currents of voltage-defined elements as additional state variables in the system matrix .

In a standard DC analysis, the simulator constructs a real-numbered matrix to satisfy the equation $G \cdot V = I$. However, the AC analysis requires the dramatic expansion of this system into the complex domain. The standard conductance matrix $G$ expands into the massive Complex Admittance Matrix $Y$ .

The overall Admittance Matrix $Y$ is formally defined as $Y = G + jB$, where $G$ represents the real conductances (derived from physical resistors and the frozen transistor parameters $g_m$ and $g_{ds}$), and $B$ represents the imaginary susceptances (derived from the reactive $j\omega C$ and $-j/(\omega L)$ components) . The fundamental AC MNA matrix equation is structured as follows:

$\begin{bmatrix} G + jB & B_v \\ C_v & D_v \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} V_n \\ I_v \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} J \\ E \end{bmatrix}$

In this expanded block matrix structure, the upper-left partition ($G + jB$) represents the traditional $N \times N$ nodal admittance matrix for a circuit containing $N$ non-reference nodes . Every component is programmatically "stamped" into this specific matrix block based on its topological terminal connections . The secondary matrices ($B_v$, $C_v$, and $D_v$) accommodate the voltage-defined elements. The $B_v$ matrix is an incidence matrix containing scalar values of $1$, $-1$, or $0$, which strictly dictates how the unknown branch currents enter or leave the structural nodes . The $C_v$ matrix (which is often the exact transpose of $B_v$ in passive networks) explicitly enforces the specific voltage constraints dictated by the sources (e.g., establishing that $V_1 - V_2 = V_{source}$) .

| Element Type | MNA Matrix Stamp Location | Real ($G$) or Imaginary ($jB$) Contribution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Resistor ($R$) | Upper Left $N \times N$ (Diagonal and Off-Diagonal) | Real: $+1/R$ and $-1/R$ |
| Capacitor ($C$) | Upper Left $N \times N$ (Diagonal and Off-Diagonal) | Imaginary: $+j\omega C$ and $-j\omega C$ |
| Transconductance ($g_m$) | Upper Left $N \times N$ (Asymmetric Off-Diagonal) | Real: $+g_m$ and $-g_m$ |
| Voltage Source ($V$) | Incidence Matrices $B_v$ and $C_v$ | Real: $+1$ and $-1$ constraints |
| Inductor ($L$) | Treated as Voltage Source; Branch Current added | Imaginary constraint in $D_v$: $-j\omega L$ |

The Right-Hand Side (RHS) of the MNA equation is the stimulus vector, containing the known values of the independent AC sources . If an AC voltage source is defined in the SPICE netlist as `V1 IN 0 AC 1 90`, the RHS vector $E$ will contain the complex excitation $1\angle 90^{\circ}$, which is mathematically represented internally as $0 + j1$ . Because the entire network has been linearized, the MNA matrix is populated entirely with constants at any given frequency, perfectly setting the stage for a direct linear algebraic solution .

## 9.6 SOLVING LINEAR SYSTEMS WITH COMPLEX NUMBERS

The formulation of the complex MNA matrix is a profound theoretical achievement, but its numerical resolution represents a massive computer science challenge. To find the unknown complex voltage vector $V_n$, the SPICE engine must rigorously solve the linear system $Y \cdot V = I$ . While mathematically this is equivalent to multiplying by the inverse ($V = Y^{-1} \cdot I$), explicit matrix inversion is practically never utilized in computational linear algebra due to its catastrophic $O(N^3)$ algorithmic time complexity and its total destruction of matrix sparsity . Instead, modern SPICE architectures rely exclusively on highly optimized sparse LU factorization algorithms .

The process of LU factorization decomposes the complex admittance matrix $Y$ into the product of a lower triangular matrix $L$ and an upper triangular matrix $U$, such that $P \cdot Y \cdot Q = L \cdot U$, where $P$ and $Q$ are permutation matrices utilized for dynamic pivot reordering . Once the matrix is factored, the system is solved using a rapid, two-step vector process of forward substitution ($L \cdot x = I$) and backward substitution ($U \cdot V = x$) . 

The primary computational burden of AC analysis arises from the fact that $Y$ is a matrix of complex floating-point numbers. In standard computer architecture, a single complex multiplication $(a + jb) \times (c + jd) = (ac - bd) + j(ad + bc)$ requires four distinct floating-point multiplications and two additions. This severely increases the processor cycles required compared to the purely real numbers utilized in the DC matrix solve.

Historically, classical circuit simulators such as SPICE3f5 utilized Kenneth Kundert's "Sparse 1.3" solver . The Sparse 1.3 engine employed a right-looking, Markowitz-style pivoting strategy designed to minimize fill-ins (the unwanted generation of non-zero elements during the factorization process) . The Sparse 1.3 solver represented the massive circuit matrix in memory as a linked list of nodes, each pointing to the next element in the column or row, effectively bypassing the zero-values that make up over 99% of a typical circuit matrix . 

However, as integrated circuit complexity grew from thousands to billions of components, Sparse 1.3 became a severe bottleneck. Modern simulation engines, such as Ngspice and Xyce, have universally transitioned to the KLU (KLU Sparse Direct Linear Solver) developed by Timothy Davis at the University of Florida . KLU is phenomenally faster than Sparse 1.3—often accelerating the solve time by a factor of 1,000 for highly sparse, scale-free MNA matrices . 

KLU achieves this exponential speedup by running an initial algorithm to identify the underlying Block Triangular Form (BTF) of the circuit matrix . Circuit matrices are uniquely characterized by having a roughly symmetric non-zero pattern that can be permuted into a BTF with a single large diagonal block surrounded by much smaller diagonal blocks . Once permuted, KLU utilizes a Gilbert-Peierls left-looking algorithm combined with Column Approximate Minimum Degree (COLAMD) reordering to factorize *only* the diagonal blocks of the BTF, completely skipping all off-diagonal zero-operations . Even with these advanced algorithmic optimizations, the dense complex arithmetic of the AC LU factorization remains one of the primary constraints in large-scale radio-frequency (RF) verification .

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Primary Matrix Structure | Linked lists of nodes | Block Triangular Form (BTF) |
| Core Factorization Method | Right-looking Markowitz-style | Left-looking Gilbert-Peierls |
| Reordering Strategy | Markowitz preordering | Column Approximate Minimum Degree (COLAMD) |

## 9.7 FREQUENCY SWEEPING ALGORITHMS

With the robust mathematical framework for solving a single complex frequency matrix firmly established, the simulator is prepared to execute the actual frequency sweep as requested by the user's `.AC` netlist directive. The AC analysis is not a chronological transient progression; it is a rigid, iterative looping algorithm that restamps and resolves the entire complex MNA matrix at a predefined sequence of independent frequency points .

The SPICE netlist directive explicitly defines the mathematical bounds and the stepping methodology of the sweep. For example, the command `.ac dec 10000 1 10k` instructs the simulation engine to sweep logarithmically over a decade scale, meticulously calculating 10,000 independent frequency points per decade starting from $1\ \text{Hz}$ and terminating at $10\ \text{kHz}$ . The simulator natively supports linear sweeps (`LIN`), octave sweeps (`OCT`), and decade sweeps (`DEC`). The choice of stepping purely dictates the algorithmic generation of the target frequency vector $f_k$ . 

At the initiation of the core AC loop, the simulator sets $\omega_k = 2\pi f_k$. Because the Laplace variable is mapped as $s = j\omega_k$, every single reactive element in the circuit has an absolute admittance that is directly proportional to the current evaluation frequency . Therefore, for every single step in the frequency sweep, the entire imaginary sub-matrix $B$ must be flushed from memory and recalculated. 

The algorithm executes the following sequence at every frequency point $f_k$:
1.  **Update Reactances:** Calculate the new imaginary admittance values based on the current frequency: $+j\omega_k C$ for all capacitors and $-j/(\omega_k L)$ for all inductors .
2.  **Matrix Restamping:** Inject these newly updated complex values into their specific geometric coordinate locations within the global Admittance matrix $Y$ . It is crucial to note that the real conductance matrix $G$ (composed of physical resistors and frozen transistor parameters) remains statically locked from the initial DC operating point and does not require recalculation .
3.  **Complex LU Factorization:** Pass the newly stamped, complete complex matrix $Y$ to the KLU or Sparse 1.3 solver. The matrix is factorized into its $L$ and $U$ components .
4.  **Forward/Backward Substitution:** Solve the linear system to compute the new complex node voltage vector $V_k$ corresponding exactly to the frequency $f_k$ .
5.  **Memory Storage:** Extract the raw complex floating-point arrays and append them to the SPICE output vector memory buffers.

This iterative computational loop continues uninterrupted until the final specified frequency point is evaluated. Because the network has been completely linearized, there are absolutely no convergence checks, iteration limits, or timestep rejections during the AC sweep. If the underlying DC operating point converged successfully, the AC analysis is virtually guaranteed to execute sequentially through every point without failure . 

## 9.8 EXTRACTING MAGNITUDE AND PHASE

Upon the culmination of the frequency sweep, the simulator's memory buffers are saturated with vast arrays of complex floating-point numbers, representing the absolute voltage at every node and the current through every branch at every simulated frequency. However, raw complex numbers stored in the Cartesian form $V_{re} + jV_{im}$ are rarely intuitive or useful to a circuit designer interpreting a Bode plot. Therefore, SPICE must execute a secondary post-processing algorithm to extract the physical parameters of interest: Magnitude, Phase, and Decibels .

The magnitude of the AC signal represents the absolute peak amplitude of the sinusoidal wave at that specific node. It is calculated algebraically using the Pythagorean modulus of the complex vector :

$|V| = \sqrt{V_{re}^2 + V_{im}^2}$

Because analog circuit behavior often spans several massive orders of magnitude (e.g., from nanovolts of noise to volts of output swing), linear amplitude is frequently compressed into a logarithmic scale known as the Decibel (dB). SPICE calculates the voltage magnitude in decibels strictly based on a base-10 logarithm scaled by a constant factor of 20 :

$V_{dB} = 20 \log_{10}(|V|)$

The phase angle represents the temporal delay or advance of the sinusoidal wave relative to the input stimulus (which is canonically assigned a reference phase of $0^{\circ}$). The raw phase is extracted using the arctangent of the imaginary part divided by the real part, converted from radians to degrees :

$\phi = \arctan\left(\frac{V_{im}}{V_{re}}\right) \times \left(\frac{180}{\pi}\right)$

The internal SPICE output engine incorporates four-quadrant arctangent functions (e.g., `atan2(Im, Re)`) to accurately track phase shifts across all $360^{\circ}$ of rotation and prevent mathematical sign ambiguities when $V_{re}$ becomes negative. Furthermore, phase-unwrapping algorithms are often applied during data plotting to prevent arbitrary aesthetic discontinuities (e.g., jumping from $+180^{\circ}$ to $-180^{\circ}$) on the visual graph.

A subtle but crucial architectural detail in SPICE is how it computationally handles the mathematical difference between two complex node voltages, dictated by the internal `ACOUT` option parameter . By default (`ACOUT=1`), SPICE calculates the difference of the individual magnitudes: $|V_1| - |V_2|$. However, if strict complex mathematical subtraction is required prior to magnitude extraction (e.g., $|V_1 - V_2|$), the legacy SPICE formulation (`ACOUT=0`) must be utilized, which resolves the complex differential vectors prior to executing the absolute value modulus .

### A Note on Small-Signal Noise Analysis
It is highly pertinent to emphasize that this precise complex post-processing methodology is also uniquely leveraged by SPICE's `.NOISE` analysis command . AC Noise analysis fundamentally utilizes the exact same frozen linear MNA matrix and frequency sweep engine as the standard `.AC` routine . However, instead of applying a deterministic sinusoidal stimulus vector $E$, the solver automatically applies stochastic, frequency-dependent noise current sources across all dissipative components. 

For example, a resistor's thermal (Johnson) noise is modeled internally as a parallel AC current source of $\overline{i_n^2} = 4kT\Delta f / R$, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is absolute temperature . Semiconductor shot noise is modeled as $\overline{i_{sn}^2} = 2qI_D \Delta f$, and semiconductor flicker noise is modeled using empirical SPICE parameters $AF$ and $KF$ . The complex MNA matrix evaluates the transfer function of these uncorrelated complex noise vectors to a specified output node . Because the noise vectors are uncorrelated, they are added as the sum of their power spectral densities (sum of squares), allowing designers to quantify the small-signal signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) without engaging in chaotic, time-consuming transient stochastic modeling .

## 9.9 POLE-ZERO ANALYSIS (`.PZ`)

While `.AC` sweeps explicitly solve the network's frequency response over a defined linear or logarithmic spectrum, an alternate architectural approach is to directly compute the inherent systemic resonances of the linear network without sweeping. This is achieved via the Pole-Zero Analysis (`.PZ`) directive. Rather than sweeping frequency and observing the resultant output magnitude, the Pole-Zero algorithm calculates the explicit complex roots of the system's overall transfer function $H(s) = N(s) / D(s)$ . This direct extraction yields the poles (roots of the denominator polynomial $D(s)$) and zeroes (roots of the numerator polynomial $N(s)$) .

In the complex $s$-plane, a pole represents a complex frequency at which the system's mathematical impedance approaches infinity, indicating a natural, unforced resonant mode. A zero represents a complex frequency where the network's output is entirely suppressed. The extraction of these roots is not achieved through iterative MNA sweeps, but rather by reframing the MNA matrix into a generalized eigenvalue problem . 

The complex MNA matrix is redefined explicitly in terms of the Laplace variable $s$, yielding $Y(s) = G + sC$, where $G$ contains the real conductances and $C$ contains the reactive capacitances and inductances . To find the poles of the network, the solver seeks the non-trivial solutions to the homogenous algebraic equation:

$(G + sC) \cdot V = 0$

This formulation perfectly mirrors the generalized eigenvalue problem $Ax = \lambda Bx$ . Historically, older variations of the SPICE engine, such as SPICE3f5, extracted the roots of this polynomial equation using Muller's method . First introduced by David E. Muller in 1956, Muller's method is a numerical root-finding algorithm that generalizes the Secant method by using a quadratic polynomial projection rather than a linear one . By utilizing three initial approximations of the root ($x_0, x_1, x_2$), the algorithm mathematically constructs a parabola and solves the quadratic formula to find the next complex root estimate $x_3$ . 

While Muller's method provides excellent convergence (with an order of convergence $\approx 1.84$) and elegantly identifies complex conjugate root pairs without requiring the calculation of analytic derivatives, it suffers from severe numerical instability when applied to the massive, highly sparse matrices of modern Very-Large-Scale Integration (VLSI) circuits . The algorithm can become trapped, generating spurious eigenvalues—numbers that appear in the eigenvalue list but are not actual solutions to the differential equations .

Consequently, modern pole-zero solvers in advanced SPICE iterations often abandon Muller's method in favor of the QZ algorithm (also known as the generalized Schur decomposition) . The QZ algorithm orthogonally transforms the $G$ and $C$ matrices into upper triangular forms without ever explicitly attempting to invert the potentially singular $C$ matrix . This mathematically robust eigenvalue extraction completely isolates the exact locations of the poles and zeros in the left- or right-hand complex planes, allowing filter designers and control-system engineers to explicitly guarantee the stability margins (Bode phase margins) of operational amplifiers and switching regulators without relying purely on subjective graphical AC sweeps .

## 9.10 AC ANALYSIS LIMITATIONS

The mathematical elegance of the frequency domain provides unparalleled simulation speed and explicit analytical depth; however, it is entirely restricted by a monolithic, fundamental assumption: perfect, absolute linearity . To conclude Volume 9, one must explicitly define the stringent theoretical limitations imposed by the Small-Signal Small-Perturbation theorem.

Because the SPICE engine rigorously freezes the active semiconductor devices at their specific DC Jacobian tangent (as detailed extensively in Chapter 3), the AC analysis matrix is completely and utterly blind to any dynamic shifts in the operating point . In physical reality, if a circuit designer applies a $10\ \text{V}$ peak sinusoidal stimulus to the gate of a low-voltage amplifier running on a $5\ \text{V}$ power supply, the physical transistor will violently saturate, clip harshly against the power supply rails, and exhibit severe non-linear harmonic distortion.

If this exact same $10\ \text{V}$ AC stimulus is applied in a SPICE `.AC` analysis, the simulator will blindly multiply the input stimulus vector by the linearized AC gain. If the static DC tangent gain ($g_m \cdot r_o$) is $100$, the AC analysis will confidently output a sinusoidal magnitude of $1,000\ \text{V}$ . The linear solver exhibits absolutely no mathematical "blushing" or error generation at producing output voltages that drastically exceed the physical power supply rails . This occurs because, within the frozen linear complex matrix, power supply rails do not exist—they are merely mathematical equivalents to AC ground. 

Consequently, AC analysis fundamentally fails to simulate large-signal non-linearities . It mathematically cannot model Total Harmonic Distortion (THD), Intermodulation Distortion (IMD), slew-rate limiting, hard clipping, or frequency mixing (heterodyning). Because the network is perfectly linear, an input signal at $1\ \text{kHz}$ will only ever produce an output signal at exactly $1\ \text{kHz}$. Frequency translation is mathematically impossible in a linear time-invariant (LTI) matrix .

When a designer requires the frequency spectrum of a non-linear large-signal operation (such as predicting the intermodulation spurs of a heavily driven RF mixer or the spectral harmonics of a clipped audio amplifier), they must entirely abandon the standard AC analysis. Instead, they must run a full non-linear Transient Analysis (`.TRAN`), recording the time-domain waveform, and subsequently execute a discrete Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) or utilize advanced Harmonic Balance algorithms to extract the large-signal frequency components .

Understanding these strict limitations separates the novice SPICE user from the masterful simulation architect. The frequency domain is an abstraction—a magnificent, computationally efficient, mathematically rigorous abstraction—but it remains a frozen snapshot of reality. The complex Modified Nodal Analysis matrix provides extreme precision regarding bandwidth, phase margins, and pole locations, but it explicitly relies on the engineer to ensure that the physical circuit remains well within the infinitesimal boundaries of the small-signal assumption.
