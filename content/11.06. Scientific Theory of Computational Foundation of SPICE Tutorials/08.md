# CHAPTER 8: THE SPICE TIMESTEP ENGINE AND LOCAL TRUNCATION ERROR CONTROL

## INTRODUCTION 

The transient analysis engine constitutes the most computationally demanding and mathematically complex subsystem within the Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis (SPICE). While direct current operating point analyses solve steady-state algebraic equations, and alternating current analyses solve linearized equations in the frequency domain, transient analysis must solve a coupled set of nonlinear first-order differential algebraic equations across the dimension of time . The fundamental challenge of this task lies in discretizing continuous time into finite steps, transforming dynamic differential equations into a series of time-independent nonlinear equations that can be solved iteratively via the Newton-Raphson method . 

However, time is not a uniform continuum in the context of electrical engineering. The dynamics of a circuit can shift instantaneously from absolute quiescence to violent nanosecond transitions. Consequently, the algorithms that govern how SPICE advances through time cannot rely on static increments. They require a highly responsive, predictive, and mathematically rigorous engine capable of expanding and contracting the temporal step size—often by orders of magnitude—from one moment to the next. The simulation of transient behavior demands the continuous resolution of state variables, specifically capacitor charges and inductor fluxes, which govern the temporal evolution of the system . The stability of this numerical integration is inextricably linked to the integration formulas utilized, the eigenvalues of the circuit's state matrix, and the precise control of the discretizing time step . 

This volume serves as an exhaustive deconstruction of the SPICE timestep control algorithms, tracing their origins from Laurence W. Nagel's foundational work on SPICE2 to modern commercial derivatives. It explores the predictive extrapolation of state variables via divided differences, the exact analytical formulation of Local Truncation Error, the deep interplay of convergence tolerances, and the contemporary architectural advancements required to prevent algorithmic collapse in complex, highly non-linear switching circuits.

## 8.1 THE INEFFICIENCY OF FIXED TIMESTEPS 

### THE NATURE OF DIFFERENTIAL ALGEBRAIC EQUATIONS
To understand the necessity of dynamic timestep control, one must first examine the fundamental mathematical representation of a circuit within the SPICE architecture. SPICE employs Modified Nodal Analysis to formulate the circuit equations, resulting in a coupled set of nonlinear first-order differential algebraic equations . These equations are generally expressed in the form:

$f(x(t), \dot{x}(t), t) = 0$

Here, the vector $x(t)$ represents the circuit variables, which include node voltages, branch currents, capacitor charges, and inductor fluxes, while $\dot{x}(t)$ represents the time derivatives of these vectors . The function $f$ encapsulates the nonlinear branch constitutive equations of the semiconductor devices. Because an analytical closed-form solution to this system is impossible for all but the most trivial linear circuits, SPICE must numerically integrate the system by replacing the continuous time derivative $\dot{x}(t)$ with a discrete finite difference approximation .

### THE DICHOTOMY OF CIRCUIT TIME SCALES
The fundamental necessity of a dynamic timestep engine is driven by the extreme stiffness of the differential equations that model semiconductor circuits. A stiff system of differential equations is one in which the desired solution varies slowly, but there are nearby solutions that vary rapidly, corresponding to widely separated eigenvalues in the complex plane . This mathematical stiffness directly translates to the physical dichotomy between steady-state operation and high-frequency transient switching in electrical networks.

Consider a modern switching power supply operating at a fundamental switching frequency of $100 \text{ kHz}$. To capture the macro-level behavior of this circuit, such as inductor current ripple or output capacitor voltage drift over a complete operational cycle, a simulator might intuitively step through time in increments of $1 \text{ \mu s}$. However, the power field-effect transistors within this supply do not transition between their conducting and blocking states over microseconds; they switch in single-digit nanoseconds or even picoseconds. If the simulator were strictly bound to a fixed step of $1 \text{ \mu s}$, it would completely bypass the switching transient. The engine would sample the gate voltage at $0 \text{ V}$, and one step later sample it at $10 \text{ V}$, treating the transition as an instantaneous mathematical discontinuity. This artificially high derivative would inject massive, erroneous displacement currents into parasitic capacitances according to the physical law of $i = C \frac{dv}{dt}$, preventing the Newton-Raphson solver from converging and ultimately crashing the simulation .

### THE COMPUTATIONAL DISASTER OF A CONSTANT MINIMUM TIMESTEP
To resolve the discontinuity, a naive architectural approach might be to force the simulator to use a constant timestep small enough to resolve the fastest possible event in the circuit. If the transistor switches in $1 \text{ ns}$, one might mandate a fixed $\Delta t = 10 \text{ ps}$ to ensure sufficient resolution of the rising edge and guarantee that the Newton-Raphson algorithm achieves convergence.

While this prevents convergence failure during the highly nonlinear switching event, it introduces a computationally disastrous penalty during the circuit's steady-state intervals. If a transient simulation spans $10 \text{ ms}$, which is a typical timeframe to observe the startup and voltage regulation of a $100 \text{ kHz}$ converter, forcing a constant $\Delta t = 10 \text{ ps}$ would require $10^9$ discrete integration steps. Because each time point requires the assembly of a sparse Jacobian matrix, the loading of semiconductor device models, matrix factorization, and multiple iterative loops to achieve convergence, evaluating a billion time points would take hours or days for a relatively simple circuit . 

Therefore, a fixed timestep is computationally inviable. The timestep engine must operate elastically. During periods of relative inactivity, where nodal voltages and branch currents are stable, the engine must stretch $\Delta t$ into the microsecond domain to leap forward in time, thereby minimizing matrix factorizations and maximizing simulation speed . Conversely, the precise moment an active device begins to conduct, or an independent source begins to slew, the engine must violently shrink $\Delta t$ down to the picosecond domain to accurately resolve the nonlinear transition . This requirement mandates a continuous, real-time assessment of integration error, determining exactly how large of a step the simulator can take without violating the fundamental physics of the circuit.

## 8.2 DEFINING LOCAL TRUNCATION ERROR (LTE) 

### THE ORIGIN OF NUMERICAL INTEGRATION ERROR
To dynamically scale the timestep, the SPICE engine must possess a rigorous, quantifiable metric of accuracy for the current step. This metric is the Local Truncation Error. Local Truncation Error is defined mathematically as the exact error introduced into the simulation strictly by the numerical integration algorithm during the current timestep, under the theoretical assumption that all previous time points up to that moment were calculated with absolute perfection .

When the SPICE engine employs a numerical integration formula, it truncates the infinite Taylor series expansion of the state variable. The discarded higher-order terms of this Taylor series represent the truncation error . This truncation allows the continuous differential equation to be mapped onto a discrete time domain, but it intrinsically limits the fidelity of the solution based on the step size $\Delta t$ and the order of the integration method chosen.

### MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION OF LTE
The state of a circuit variable at a future time $t_{n+1}$ can be expressed using an infinite Taylor series expansion around the current time $t_n$:

$x(t_{n+1}) = x(t_n) + \dot{x}(t_n)\Delta t + \frac{1}{2!}\ddot{x}(t_n)\Delta t^2 + \frac{1}{3!}\dddot{x}(t_n)\Delta t^3 + \dots$

Numerical integration methods approximate this infinite series by retaining only the lowest-order terms. When using the Trapezoidal integration method, a second-order implicit technique, the state at time $t_{n+1}$ is approximated by integrating the derivative over the interval $[t_n, t_{n+1}]$ using a linear interpolation of the derivatives:

$x_{n+1} = x_n + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \left( \dot{x}_{n+1} + \dot{x}_n \right)$

By expanding the exact analytical expression $x(t_{n+1})$ into a Taylor series and comparing it to the Trapezoidal approximation, the exact mathematical difference between the true continuous solution and the discrete numerical solution emerges. This difference constitutes the Local Truncation Error. For the Trapezoidal method, the principal term of the local truncation error is proportional to the third derivative of the state variable :

$LTE_{Trap} = -\frac{1}{12} \Delta t^3 \frac{d^3 x}{dt^3}(\xi)$

where $\xi$ is some point in the interval $[t_n, t_{n+1}]$.

Similarly, if the simulator utilizes the Backward Euler integration algorithm, which is a first-order implicit method, the local truncation error is proportional to the second derivative:

$LTE_{BE} = -\frac{1}{2} \Delta t^2 \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}(\xi)$

### THE IMPLICATIONS OF THE ERROR MAGNITUDE
The magnitude of the Local Truncation Error strictly dictates the validity of the numerical solution . If the circuit is experiencing a high-speed transient, the higher-order derivatives such as the acceleration and the jerk of the state variable become extremely large. If $\Delta t$ is not simultaneously reduced to compensate for these massive derivatives, the Local Truncation Error will mathematically explode, resulting in a computed state vector that severely deviates from physical reality.

Because numerical stability relies on eigenvalues remaining within specific stable regions of the complex plane, integration error can accumulate and cause absolute divergence . Computing the local truncation error at every converged time point is therefore the mandatory prerequisite for determining whether the current time step is acceptable, and what the optimal magnitude of the next time step should be to maintain both speed and numerical stability .

## 8.3 THE DIVIDED DIFFERENCE PREDICTOR 

### THE NECESSITY OF PREDICTING THE FUTURE
A fundamental paradox arises when attempting to calculate the Local Truncation Error using the theoretical formulas defined in the previous chapter: the exact analytical derivatives are strictly unknown during a discrete simulation. SPICE only possesses discrete values of voltages and currents at specific timestamps, and therefore cannot directly evaluate a continuous third derivative . 

Furthermore, before the implicit Newton-Raphson solver can iterate toward a converged solution for the future time $t_{n+1}$, it requires a highly accurate initial guess for all state variables. Providing a poor initial guess to the Newton-Raphson solver will result in an excessive number of iterations, or far worse, convergence to a false local minimum, which manifests as a non-physical mathematical artifact . 

To solve the dual problem of estimating uncomputable derivatives and providing an optimal seed for the iterative solver, SPICE relies on polynomial extrapolation via the Divided Difference algorithm . Before any matrix assembly occurs for the next time step, the timestep engine mathematically predicts the future state of every node voltage, capacitor charge, and inductor flux in the circuit based strictly on their historical trajectories .

### THE DIVIDED DIFFERENCE ALGORITHM
The divided difference method constructs an explicit interpolating polynomial through the most recently converged time points, namely $t_n, t_{n-1}, t_{n-2}$, and evaluates this polynomial at the future target time $t_{n+1}$ . This technique approximates the required derivatives without requiring analytical differentiation of the complex circuit models.

Let $x_n$ be the converged value of a state variable at time $t_n$. The SPICE engine recursively calculates the divided differences of order $k$, denoted as $DD_k$ . The zero-order divided difference is simply the current value of the state variable:

$DD_0(t_n) = x_n$

The first-order divided difference represents the approximate first derivative, or the slope of the signal over the last timestep:

$DD_1(t_n) = \frac{DD_0(t_n) - DD_0(t_{n-1})}{t_n - t_{n-1}}$

The second-order divided difference represents the approximate second derivative, characterizing the curvature of the signal trajectory:

$DD_2(t_n) = \frac{DD_1(t_n) - DD_1(t_{n-1})}{t_n - t_{n-2}}$

The third-order divided difference represents the approximate third derivative, describing the rate of change of the curvature:

$DD_3(t_n) = \frac{DD_2(t_n) - DD_2(t_{n-1})}{t_n - t_{n-3}}$

Using these computed coefficients, the engine formulates the predictor polynomial for the future time $t_{n+1}$. For a second-order integration method such as the Trapezoidal rule or second-order Gear, a second-order predictor polynomial is utilized :

$x_{n+1}^P = x_n + DD_1(t_n)(t_{n+1} - t_n) + DD_2(t_n)(t_{n+1} - t_n)(t_{n+1} - t_{n-1})$

This extrapolated value, $x_{n+1}^P$, serves as the explicit predictor . It is a highly educated guess of the circuit's future state, assuming the momentum and acceleration of the voltages and currents remain consistent with their immediate history. The predictor is immediately injected into the semiconductor device equations to seed the Jacobian matrix for the very first Newton-Raphson iteration of the new time step .

## 8.4 THE CORRECTOR MECHANISM 

### THE PREDICTOR-CORRECTOR LOOP
Once the predicted state $x_{n+1}^P$ is established, the SPICE engine shifts from the explicit prediction phase into the implicit corrector phase. The corrector is comprised of the chosen numerical integration method coupled tightly with the Newton-Raphson iterative solver . 

The explicit polynomial predictor completely ignores the actual physical structure of the circuit; it merely extrapolates numerical trends. The corrector, conversely, forces the state variables to adhere to the physical laws of the universe. The Newton-Raphson solver iteratively linearizes the nonlinear differential algebraic equations, assembling a Jacobian matrix and solving the resulting system of linear equations $Ax = b$ until the voltages and currents satisfy Kirchhoff's Current Law and Kirchhoff's Voltage Law within a strictly defined tolerance . 

When the iterative updates fall below the required relative and absolute thresholds, the engine declares that the time point has converged . This final, converged value is defined as the corrected state, denoted as $x_{n+1}^C$.

### MEASURING THE EXACT LTE
The architectural brilliance of the SPICE timestep engine, as originally formulated by Laurence W. Nagel in SPICE2, lies in how it seamlessly links the explicit predictor and the implicit corrector to extract a highly accurate measurement of the Local Truncation Error . 

The predicted value $x^P$ was calculated using polynomial extrapolation, an entirely mathematical pathway. The corrected value $x^C$ was calculated using numerical integration constrained by nonlinear physics. Because these two values were arrived at via entirely independent algorithms, their difference represents a quantifiable measurement of the numerical integration error introduced during the step .

By expanding both the predictor formula and the corrector formula into Taylor series, matching the lowest-order terms, and subtracting them, the SPICE algorithm isolates the exact highest-order derivative term responsible for the truncation error. For the Trapezoidal method, the estimated local truncation error at the newly converged time point is calculated directly from this difference:

$LTE_{n+1} = \frac{x_{n+1}^C - x_{n+1}^P}{\alpha}$

Here, $\alpha$ is a dynamic scaling coefficient derived from the ratios of the current and previous discrete timesteps. For a constant step size $\Delta t$, the scaling factor for the Trapezoidal method simplifies to $\alpha = -3$, yielding the classical estimation formula:

$LTE_{n+1} \approx \frac{x_{n+1}^C - x_{n+1}^P}{-3}$

This calculation is performed individually for every energy-storing element in the entire circuit . The engine assesses the error on every capacitor charge and every inductor flux. It then scans the entire array of computed values and selects the absolute worst-case error. This singular, maximum local truncation error across the entire topology dictates the ultimate fate of the timestep engine .

## 8.5 CALCULATING THE OPTIMAL TIMESTEP 

### THE LTE-BASED TIMESTEP FORMULA
With the maximum local truncation error successfully quantified, the SPICE engine must execute its core directive: determining the optimal step size for the subsequent jump into the future, designated as $\Delta t_{new}$. 

The optimization goal of the engine is to maximize simulation speed by making $\Delta t_{new}$ as large as mathematically permissible, while strictly guaranteeing that the local truncation error of the next step will not exceed an acceptable tolerance limit, denoted as $TOL$. 

Because the local truncation error for an integration method of order $k$ scales proportionally with $\Delta t^{k+1}$, the engine can algebraically invert this relationship to predict the maximum allowable step size. The exact formula SPICE uses to dynamically calculate the next timestep is:

$\Delta t_{new} = \Delta t_{old} \left( \frac{TRTOL \cdot TOL}{LTE_{max}} \right)^{\frac{1}{k+1}}$

The parameters of this equation are strictly defined:
*   $\Delta t_{old}$ is the magnitude of the timestep that was just successfully evaluated and converged.
*   $k$ is the mathematical order of the integration method, where $k=1$ for Backward Euler, and $k=2$ for Trapezoidal and Gear-2 .
*   $TOL$ is the absolute upper limit of acceptable error for the specific node or branch being evaluated .
*   $TRTOL$ is the Transient Error Tolerance, a user-defined scaling factor that multiplies the acceptable error bound .

### THE CRITICAL ROLE OF TRTOL
The parameter `TRTOL` is arguably one of the most critical yet misunderstood options within the SPICE architecture. It stands for Transient Error Tolerance and acts as a deliberate overestimation factor for the allowable local truncation error . 

If `TRTOL` were strictly set to $1.0$, the simulator would demand that the predicted step exactly matches the theoretical error boundary without any margin. Because nonlinear circuits exhibit highly erratic derivative fluctuations that are impossible for polynomials to perfectly extrapolate, attempting to ride the exact edge of the error boundary causes the simulator to constantly overshoot the tolerance. This overshoot results in an endless cycle of rejected time points and extreme computational thrashing. 

By setting the parameter to an empirical default of $7.0$, the simulator introduces a necessary algorithmic hysteresis . It instructs the engine that it may take a timestep that theoretically produces an error seven times larger than the strict mathematical tolerance, acknowledging that the divided difference polynomial predictor is inherently pessimistic. This mechanism allows the engine to aggressively expand $\Delta t$ during steady-state conditions without suffering from constant, highly punitive timestep rejections. Increasing the value of `TRTOL` will uniformly increase the simulation speed by permitting larger timesteps, but this comes at the direct expense of transient accuracy, potentially missing subtle high-frequency ringing on a fast switching node .

## 8.6 ITERATION-COUNT TIMESTEP CONTROL 

### THE NECESSITY OF A SECONDARY CONTROL ALGORITHM
While the LTE-based control algorithm is mathematically rigorous, it is not infallible. In electrical circuits plagued by extreme, non-physical nonlinearities—such as macro-models containing idealized behavioral switches, un-damped arbitrary voltage sources, or highly complex semiconductor models lacking smooth derivative transitions—the physical derivatives can approach infinity. In such scenarios, the divided difference polynomials fail entirely to produce a meaningful estimate of the truncation error, rendering the LTE equation mathematically useless .

To ensure the simulator does not hang infinitely when attempting to resolve a non-differentiable edge, SPICE implements a secondary, purely heuristic timestep control mechanism based entirely on the Newton-Raphson iteration count . This algorithm can operate in tandem with the local truncation error method as a fail-safe ceiling, or it can override it completely if the user manually configures the simulation with `LVLTIM=1` .

### THE ITL3 AND ITL4 HEURISTICS
The iteration-count algorithm abandons the measurement of integration error and focuses solely on the computational struggle of the Jacobian matrix solver. It is governed primarily by the parameter `ITL4`, which defines the absolute upper transient iteration limit, and `ITL3`, which defines the lower transient iteration limit . 

The operational logic of this heuristic is remarkably straightforward but highly effective for maintaining forward momentum in stiff simulations:

1.  **The Penalty Phase:** If the Newton-Raphson solver requires more than `ITL4` iterations to converge on a solution (the default limit being 10 iterations), it indicates that the initial predictor guess was extremely poor. This failure is almost exclusively due to a massive, unforeseen transient event occurring between the time points. The engine forcibly rejects the time point, discards the matrix data, and aggressively halves the timestep:
$\Delta t_{new} = \frac{\Delta t_{old}}{2}$
(In legacy SPICE2 architectures, this division was even more punitive, scaling by a factor of 8 ).
2.  **The Reward Phase:** If the Newton-Raphson solver converges almost instantly—typically in fewer iterations than the `ITL3` limit—it indicates the circuit is highly linear or resting in a quiescent state. The engine rewards this algorithmic stability by doubling the timestep for the next evaluation:
$\Delta t_{new} = \Delta t_{old} \times 2$

While iteration-count control is exceptionally robust against derivative explosions, it is blind to integration artifacts. Because it does not measure local truncation error, it will gladly stretch the timestep to maximum length across an oscillating high-frequency sine wave if the iterative solver converges easily. This results in an output plot that resembles a jagged, undersampled triangle wave rather than a smooth sinusoid. Consequently, in modern simulation engines, the `ITL4` heuristic is utilized strictly as a mandatory fail-safe to cut time when the LTE algorithm requests a step that is mathematically permissible but physically impossible to solve .

## 8.7 THE IMPACT OF TOLERANCES (`TRTOL`, `RELTOL`) 

### DEFINING THE TOLERANCE BOUNDARY
In the optimal timestep formula defined in Chapter 5, the engine continuously attempts to keep the local truncation error strictly below a dynamic limit known as $TOL$. This tolerance boundary is not a static scalar value; it is recalculated continuously for every single node and branch at every time step, based on the absolute magnitude of the specific signals currently present in the network .

The calculation of $TOL$ represents the deep, inextricably linked interplay between relative and absolute error parameters in the SPICE architecture. The engine computes $TOL$ for the local truncation error control using the following standard equation:

$TOL = RELTOL \cdot \max(|x_{n+1}^C|, |x_n|) + \text{Absolute Tolerance}$

If the state variable under evaluation is a node voltage, the absolute tolerance utilized is `VNTOL`. If the state variable is a branch current, the absolute tolerance utilized is `ABSTOL`. If the state variable is a capacitor charge or inductor flux evaluated specifically during the truncation error phase, it is replaced by `CHGTOL` or flux tolerance, respectively .

### THE INTERPLAY OF TOLERANCE PARAMETERS
The parameters governing this dynamic boundary dictate the foundational accuracy and speed of the entire simulation.

| Tolerance Parameter | SPICE3 Default | Definition & Impact on Timestep Engine |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **RELTOL** | $0.001$ ($0.1\%$) | The relative error tolerance. This is the primary dial for overall simulation accuracy. Tightening `RELTOL` (e.g., to $10^{-5}$) drastically shrinks the $TOL$ boundary, forcing the engine to take significantly smaller timesteps to suppress local truncation error.  |
| **ABSTOL** | $1 \text{ pA}$ | The absolute current tolerance. Establishes the mathematical noise floor for currents. It prevents the required $TOL$ from becoming zero when a current waveform crosses zero, avoiding infinitesimally small timesteps.  |
| **VNTOL** | $1 \text{ \mu V}$ | The absolute voltage tolerance. Establishes the noise floor for voltages. Prevents math errors and zero-division when measuring microvolt-level node fluctuations or zero crossings.  |
| **CHGTOL** | $10 \text{ fC}$ | The absolute charge tolerance. Scales the acceptable error bound specifically for capacitive and inductive state variables during the local truncation error calculation phase.  |

The dependence on `RELTOL` is the defining characteristic of SPICE simulation fidelity. If a specific node voltage is swinging rapidly between $0 \text{ V}$ and $100 \text{ V}$, the $100 \text{ V}$ peak dominates the equation. With the default `RELTOL` set to $0.001$, the acceptable error margin is approximately $100 \text{ V} \times 0.001 = 0.1 \text{ V}$. The engine will automatically scale the timestep to ensure the numerical integration algorithm introduces no more than $100 \text{ mV}$ of synthetic error per step .

However, the architecture relies heavily on absolute tolerances to prevent failure. If the voltage crosses zero, the product of $RELTOL \cdot 0$ yields zero. If $TOL$ were allowed to reach absolute zero, the $\Delta t$ formula would attempt to divide by zero, or force a timestep of zero picoseconds, immediately triggering a catastrophic "timestep too small" engine failure. The parameters `ABSTOL` and `VNTOL` exist entirely to provide a physical floor. When the signal is massive, `RELTOL` dominates the equation; when the signal approaches zero, the absolute tolerances dominate, allowing the engine to safely and smoothly step through zero-crossing events without collapsing the time continuum .

## 8.8 HANDLING ABRUPT DISCONTINUITIES 

### THE BREAKPOINT ALGORITHM
Electrical circuits are heavily driven by independent stimulus sources, such as clock signals, pulse-width modulation generators, and rapidly slewing pulsed inputs. When an engineer defines a Piecewise Linear (PWL) or PULSE voltage source in a SPICE netlist, they inject a severe, predefined mathematical discontinuity directly into the time continuum . 

Consider a pulsed voltage source defined to transition from $0 \text{ V}$ to $5 \text{ V}$ with a rise time of exactly $1 \text{ ns}$, initiating precisely at $t = 10 \text{ \mu s}$. As the simulation approaches the $10 \text{ \mu s}$ mark, the local truncation error algorithm is likely dictating an exceptionally large timestep, potentially on the order of $100 \text{ ns}$, because the circuit is currently sitting in a steady-state condition at $0 \text{ V}$. If the engine blindly takes a $100 \text{ ns}$ step, it will completely overshoot the initiation of the pulse, sample the source mid-transition or after the transition has completed, and trigger an immediate convergence failure due to the massive discrepancy between the predicted state and the physical state.

To prevent this architectural blindness, SPICE parses all independent transient sources prior to the initialization of the matrix solver and constructs a Breakpoint Table . The breakpoint table is a hardcoded array of critical timestamps indicating exactly when any independent source will change its derivative—specifically, the exact coordinate of a rising edge initiation, and the exact coordinate of a rising edge termination . 

### VIOLENT CONTRACTION AND BACKWARD EULER
As the dynamic timestep engine marches forward through the analysis, it constantly queries the breakpoint table. If the formulaically calculated $\Delta t_{new}$ would cause the simulation time to step past an upcoming breakpoint, the engine forcibly overrides the local truncation error algorithm . It artificially truncates the timestep so that the next evaluated time point lands *exactly* on the breakpoint coordinate .

Landing precisely on a breakpoint poses a severe numerical hazard for the solver. At the exact microscopic moment a pulse source begins to rise, its second and third derivatives become mathematically undefined, approaching infinity. Higher-order integration methods, particularly the default second-order Trapezoidal rule, are highly susceptible to numerical ringing when integrating across non-differentiable discontinuities .

To circumvent this mathematical instability, when SPICE hits a breakpoint, it violently alters its internal algorithmic structure. It temporarily abandons the second-order predictors and unconditionally forces the integration method to first-order Backward Euler strictly for that specific time step . Backward Euler, where the integration order $k=1$, is both A-stable and L-stable . This L-stability provides heavy numerical damping, allowing the engine to absorb the shock of the infinite derivative without suffering from non-physical ringing artifacts . Once the breakpoint is successfully cleared and the discontinuity resolved, the engine smoothly transitions back to the highly accurate second-order integration method and hands control back to the standard local truncation error algorithm .

## 8.9 TIME-STEP REJECTION (REWINDING TIME) 

### THE ANATOMY OF A REJECTED STEP
The efficiency of the timestep engine relies entirely on the assumption that its polynomial divided differences can accurately predict the future. However, physical circuits frequently behave unpredictably. An internal node might abruptly collapse due to a semiconductor device reaching its threshold voltage, such as a rapid avalanche breakdown or a snap-off reverse recovery in a power diode . These events occur completely independently of the Breakpoint Table because they are internally generated by the circuit's dynamic state, not by a pre-programmed user-defined source.

If the engine takes a large step into the future, and an internal device violently switches during that temporal leap, one of two catastrophic things will happen:
1. The Newton-Raphson solver will fail to converge within the absolute maximum `ITL4` iterations, caught in an endless loop of oscillating matrix updates.
2. The solver will successfully converge, but upon calculating the Local Truncation Error, the engine discovers that the error vastly exceeds the boundary, evaluating to $LTE_{max} > TRTOL \cdot TOL$.

In either scenario, the evaluated time point is deemed physically invalid. The SPICE engine must reject the step and initiate a complex rewind of time . 

### THE COMPUTATIONAL PENALTY
Time-step rejection is the single most computationally expensive penalty in a SPICE simulation . When a step is rejected, the engine must perform a complete state rollback to preserve mathematical integrity. 

First, the memory vectors containing the newly computed node voltages, branch currents, and charge states are completely discarded, vaporizing the results of the calculation . Second, the Jacobian matrix, which required thousands of floating-point operations to construct, populate with device conductance values, and factorize using Lower-Upper (LU) decomposition, is entirely flushed . Third, the internal simulation clock is rewound back to the exact coordinate of the last successfully converged time point.

Once the state is restored, the SPICE engine invokes the timestep reduction formula, drastically scaling back $\Delta t$—often dropping it by an order of magnitude or utilizing the exact calculated LTE ratio to create a micro-step. It then begins the entire process again: predicting the state, assembling the matrix, and solving the linear equations. If the circuit is highly unstable, such as an under-damped switching regulator exhibiting parasitic ringing, the engine may reject a step, cut the time, step forward by picoseconds, and immediately reject the new step. This results in a fatal cascade of failures known as a "timestep too small" error, where $\Delta t$ is repeatedly crushed down to $10^{-18}$ seconds until the engine determines progress is impossible and aborts the simulation entirely . Mastering the prevention of rejected steps is the foundational goal of all modern SPICE engine architecture.

## 8.10 MODERN ADVANCEMENTS IN TIMESTEP OPTIMIZATION 

### THE PROBLEM WITH CLASSIC ALGORITHMS: RINGING VS. DAMPENING
The classic Berkeley SPICE algorithms formulated in SPICE2 and SPICE3 forced software architects and integrated circuit designers into a rigid, unavoidable dichotomy when dealing with timestep control and numerical integration stability. 

The default Trapezoidal integration method is highly accurate and computationally fast. However, it is not L-stable. When subjected to the abrupt switching of inductive currents or the stiff nonlinearities common in modern power electronics, Trapezoidal integration exhibits severe numerical ringing . This simulation artifact manifests as a high-frequency, non-physical oscillation around the true solution. This artificial ringing causes the local truncation error algorithm to falsely detect massive state deviations, prompting it to dramatically slash the timestep and triggering the exact chain reaction of timestep rejections that grinds the simulation to a halt .

Alternatively, a user could manually configure the engine to utilize Gear integration (`METHOD=GEAR`). Gear's backward differentiation formulas are stiffly stable and heavily damped, granting them immunity to numerical ringing . However, this immunity extracts a steep cost: Gear integration artificially dampens the actual physics of the circuit . It will erroneously suppress real, physical oscillations in high-Q LC circuits and oscillators, leading the engineer to falsely believe an unstable circuit is perfectly stable. Furthermore, higher-order Gear methods require more historical data points, increasing the complexity of the divided difference predictor and elevating matrix assembly time .

### VOLT-SECOND ERROR CONTROL AND MODIFIED TRAP
As the electronics industry shifted aggressively toward complex, high-frequency switching power supplies, the limitations of classic timestep control became critical bottlenecks. Various commercial simulators introduced proprietary solutions to prevent algorithmic collapse. 

One such advancement was the introduction of Volt-Second Error Control (`VSECTOL`). Instead of relying solely on the local truncation error of individual state variables, the engine calculates the maximum volt-second error for any node . If the product of the proposed timestep and the absolute value of the difference between the iterated value and the predicted value exceeds the `VSECTOL` limit, the timestep is proactively scaled back . This allows the simulator to backtrack and capture extremely fast rise times from behavioral switching without forcing the global minimum timestep down to unmanageable levels, effectively preventing millions of unnecessary integration steps .

The most paradigm-shifting advancement in modern timestep optimization is the Modified Trap algorithm, introduced in the LTspice derivative engine . Modified Trap was architected to possess the computational speed and high-order accuracy of standard Trapezoidal integration, while completely neutralizing the numerical ringing artifact without relying on Gear's artificial dampening . 

While the exact mechanics of Modified Trap remain closed-source proprietary intellectual property, it operates by injecting a highly sophisticated post-processing step into the corrector loop . The algorithm mathematically detects the specific high-frequency oscillation characteristic of Trap-ringing—an error that oscillates strictly between minimum and maximum bounds at the Nyquist frequency of the timestep—and mathematically cancels it before the local truncation error algorithm is allowed to evaluate the node . 

Because the synthetic ringing is suppressed prior to the LTE calculation, the engine's dynamic timestep algorithm is no longer fooled into assessing the circuit as wildly unstable. Consequently, the Modified Trap algorithm allows the engine to maintain significantly larger $\Delta t$ steps through harsh inductive switching environments, entirely bypassing the cascade of rejected timesteps that routinely paralyze legacy Berkeley SPICE simulators . This advancement represents the current pinnacle of timestep optimization, allowing engineers to simulate highly non-linear switching topologies with both the speed of a non-dissipative solver and the absolute stability of a damped architecture .
