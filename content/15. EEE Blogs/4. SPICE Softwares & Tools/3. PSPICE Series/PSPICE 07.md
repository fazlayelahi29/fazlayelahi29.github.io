# Co-Simulation of Continuous and Discrete Systems: An Exhaustive Analysis of the SLPS Interface Architecture

## Introduction

The modern engineering landscape is defined by the inescapable convergence of complex digital control algorithms and highly non-linear analog physical systems. In the pursuit of higher efficiency, increased power density, and deterministic control, disciplines that once operated in isolation are now inextricably linked. The development of these multi-domain systems—ranging from multi-megawatt traction inverters in electric vehicles to high-frequency digitally controlled power supplies—is traditionally hampered by an inherent system-level design bottleneck. The SLPS (Simulink-PSpice) interface emerges as the definitive solution to this bottleneck, bridging the theoretical, mathematical domains of MATLAB/Simulink with the component-level, physical realities of Cadence OrCAD PSpice. This report provides an exhaustive, expert-level examination of the SLPS architecture, detailing the synchronization of multi-rate solvers, the intricate configuration of mixed-signal interfaces, and the profound implications of co-simulation on the future of cyber-physical system verification.

## Chapter 1: The System-Level Design Bottleneck

The fundamental challenge in mixed-signal, system-level design originates from the strict segregation of engineering disciplines and their respective simulation environments. Control and software engineers predominantly operate within the mathematical, idealized realms of MATLAB and Simulink, constructing algorithms based on continuous-time Laplace domains or discrete-time z-domains . These tools are optimized for block-based, data-flow graphical programming and numerical computing, allowing engineers to design sophisticated control laws, state machines, and digital filters assuming mathematically perfect plant representations . Conversely, hardware and power electronics engineers rely on SPICE-based simulators to analyze the non-linear, transient behaviors of physical components. PSpice, commercialized in 1984 as the first PC-based version of U.C. Berkeley SPICE, excels at modeling parasitic capacitances, reverse recovery times, thermal dependencies, and electromagnetic interference down to the semiconductor junction level . 

The dichotomy between these two simulation universes creates a perilous gap. A control engineer may design a perfect digital algorithm that mathematically stabilizes a simulated motor, only to find that the physical prototype fails catastrophically due to unaccounted high-frequency ringing in the inverter's MOSFETs. Hardware engineers might overdesign a power stage, unaware of how the quantization constraints and sampling delays of the digital controller will affect the output ripple. Traditional methodologies attempt to bridge this gap through the sequential construction of physical prototypes, a process that is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and prone to destructive failures. 

The SLPS interface was engineered specifically to bridge this gap, offering a unified co-simulation architecture that tightly couples the algorithmic processing of Simulink with the physical fidelity of PSpice . By utilizing this interface, software engineers and hardware engineers can collaborate simultaneously on a singular virtual prototype . This environment ensures that the digital controller in Simulink reacts dynamically to the non-linear analog physics generated by PSpice, revealing integration failures, thermal limits, and parasitic interactions long before the first printed circuit board (PCB) is fabricated . The SLPS integration allows designers to perform system-level simulations that include realistic electrical stress, propagation delays, and component yield variations, fundamentally shifting the verification paradigm from post-fabrication debugging to pre-fabrication optimization.

## Chapter 2: The SLPS Synchronization Architecture

Bridging Simulink and PSpice requires harmonizing two fundamentally disparate mathematical solver architectures. Simulink generally relies on time-marching numerical integration solvers, such as the explicit Dormand-Prince (`ode45`) or implicit solvers for stiff systems (`ode15s`), which advance time based on the error tolerances of ordinary differential equations (ODEs). PSpice, however, models circuits using Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) . The MNA approach formulates the electrical circuit into a massive matrix of differential and algebraic equations (DAEs) based on Kirchhoff's Current and Voltage Laws. 

The standard MNA formulation can be represented mathematically as:

$[G]\mathbf{x}(t) + [C]\frac{d\mathbf{x}(t)}{dt} = \mathbf{b}(t)$

Here, $[G]$ represents the conductance matrix, $[C]$ represents the capacitance and inductance matrix, $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is the vector of unknown node voltages and branch currents, and $\mathbf{b}(t)$ is the vector of independent excitations. To solve this non-linear MNA system in the time domain, PSpice employs implicit numerical integration (such as Trapezoidal or Gear methods) to discretize the derivative term, and then utilizes the iterative Newton-Raphson algorithm to converge on a solution for the non-linear components at each discrete time step .

The SLPS interface operates as an intricate synchronization engine that orchestrates data coherency between the explicit ODE solvers of Simulink and the implicit MNA solver of PSpice. In this co-simulation topology, Simulink acts as the master execution engine, while PSpice operates as a subordinate solver. At the beginning of a simulation macro-step, Simulink calculates the states of the digital algorithms and passes the resulting control signals across the SLPS boundary. PSpice receives these values as forced inputs, typically mapped to Voltage-Controlled Voltage Sources (VCVS) or Voltage-Controlled Current Sources (VCCS) within the netlist . PSpice then attempts to solve the non-linear transient response of the physical circuit up to the target time requested by Simulink. 

This synchronization is governed by a lock-step protocol equipped with a dynamic rollback mechanism. If the PSpice Newton-Raphson solver encounters extreme non-linearities—such as an Insulated-Gate Bipolar Transistor (IGBT) snapping off during a hard-switching event—it must autonomously reduce its internal time-step to achieve convergence . The SLPS architecture manages this by allowing PSpice to process multiple micro-steps within a single Simulink master step. If PSpice fails to converge, the SLPS interface initiates a rollback, forcing both simulators to regress in time, negotiate a smaller mutual time-step, and re-evaluate the transition. This bi-directional handshake ensures absolute data coherency, guaranteeing that the mathematical algorithms in MATLAB are continuously subjected to the exact physical delays and switching dynamics synthesized by the PSpice physics engine.

## Chapter 3: Configuring the SLPS Block

The practical implementation of this co-simulation architecture relies on the rigorous configuration of the SLPS block within the Simulink graphical user interface. This block serves as the portal between the block-diagram mathematics and the physical circuit schematic. Configuring the SLPS block requires a profound understanding of signal mapping, interface scaling, and port designation. The process begins in the OrCAD Capture environment, where the analog hardware schematic is designed and the SLPS interface ports are explicitly defined . Input ports in the schematic are represented by specialized hierarchical pins that receive data from Simulink, while output ports are tied to specific circuit nodes to transmit voltage or current measurements back to the digital domain .

Once the PSpice schematic is prepared, the SLPS block is instantiated within the Simulink workspace and linked to the target PSpice project file. The interface automatically parses the schematic netlist and populates the Simulink block with corresponding input and output terminals. A critical configuration phase involves mapping the dimensionless mathematical signals of Simulink into physical electrical units. 

| Simulink Domain (Math) | PSpice Domain (Physics) | Interface Configuration Requirement |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Digital Logic (0 or 1) | Gate Drive Voltage | Scale boolean output to physical voltage (e.g., 0V to 15V) mapped to a VCVS. |
| Continuous Control Effort | Analog Current Source | Map floating-point values to a VCCS; requires impedance matching in PSpice. |
| Node Voltage Feedback | Sampled ADC Value | Route PSpice output through a Zero-Order Hold (ZOH) and quantization block. |
| High-Frequency PWM | Switching Excitation | Ensure Simulink step size is fine enough to capture duty cycle resolution accurately. |

Furthermore, the engineer must configure the execution rates within the block mask. While continuous states can be passed, digital control algorithms usually execute at discrete sample times. The SLPS block must be configured to apply zero-order hold (ZOH) sampling to the inputs feeding PSpice, ensuring that the analog circuit experiences the exact discrete staircase waveforms characteristic of Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs). Reading signals back from PSpice requires analogous attention; node voltages exported by PSpice must be routed through appropriate scaling factors and quantization blocks in Simulink to accurately model the finite resolution, sampling delay, and input impedance of a physical Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) .

## Chapter 4: Co-Simulating Motor Drives

To demonstrate the profound capability of the SLPS architecture, one must examine the co-simulation of an advanced motor drive system, specifically the Field Oriented Control (FOC) of a Brushless DC (BLDC) motor or Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motor (PMSM). Motor drives represent the ultimate intersection of complex control mathematics and volatile power electronics. In this architecture, the FOC algorithm, which requires intensive matrix transformations and discrete-time proportional-integral (PI) regulators, is constructed entirely within Simulink . The physical plant—comprising the three-phase inverter, the gate drivers, and the electrical equivalent model of the BLDC motor—is constructed in PSpice .

The fundamental premise of the FOC algorithm relies on the Clarke and Park transformations to decouple the three-phase time-varying stator currents ($I_a, I_b, I_c$) into a synchronous, two-coordinate rotational reference frame ($I_d, I_q$) . This mathematical decoupling allows the controller to regulate torque and magnetic flux independently. The Clarke transformation converts the three-phase system into a two-coordinate orthogonal stationary frame ($\alpha, \beta$):

$\begin{bmatrix} I_\alpha \\ I_\beta \end{bmatrix} = \frac{2}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\frac{1}{2} & -\frac{1}{2} \\ 0 & \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} & -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I_a \\ I_b \\ I_c \end{bmatrix}$

Subsequently, the Park transformation converts the stationary orthogonal frame into a rotating reference frame ($d, q$) synchronized with the rotor position $\theta_e$:

$\begin{bmatrix} I_d \\ I_q \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_e) & \sin(\theta_e) \\ -\sin(\theta_e) & \cos(\theta_e) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I_\alpha \\ I_\beta \end{bmatrix}$

In Simulink, these equations calculate the exact duty cycles required for the Space Vector Pulse Width Modulation (SVPWM) block. These duty cycles are sent via the SLPS block to PSpice. Inside PSpice, the duty cycles drive a physical three-phase inverter constructed from nonlinear MOSFET models . Unlike ideal switches in a purely mathematical environment, the PSpice MOSFETs exhibit Miller capacitance, non-linear $R_{ds(on)}$, diode reverse recovery, and parasitic gate inductance . When the MOSFETs switch, they generate high-frequency ringing and severe $dv/dt$ transients. These physical artifacts heavily distort the phase currents. Because of the SLPS interface, these distorted, real-world currents are fed back into the Simulink FOC algorithm. The engineer can instantly observe how the digital PI controllers react to the analog switching noise, enabling the precise tuning of digital filters and dead-time compensation algorithms long before a physical motor drive is manufactured and spun .

## Chapter 5: Multi-Rate Time Domain Challenges

While the SLPS interface seamlessly links domains, the co-simulation of high-speed switching circuits and mechanical systems introduces profound mathematical challenges rooted in multi-rate time domain integration . Power electronics, such as the aforementioned traction inverter, operate at switching frequencies ranging from 10 kHz to over 1 MHz for wide-bandgap Silicon Carbide (SiC) or Gallium Nitride (GaN) devices. Accurately resolving the transient physics of a MOSFET turn-on event requires the PSpice solver to reduce its internal time-step to the picosecond or nanosecond range . Conversely, the mechanical dynamics of the motor—characterized by inertia, viscous friction, and load torque—possess time constants in the tens or hundreds of milliseconds. 

This extreme temporal disparity creates a severely "stiff" system. If the entire co-simulation were forced to execute at the nanosecond resolution required by the analog switches, simulating one second of mechanical motor operation would require trillions of calculations, bringing the simulation to a computational standstill. The SLPS architecture overcomes this by exploiting multi-rate solver decoupling . Simulink's solver dictates a macro-step based on the mechanical system's dynamics and the digital controller's sample rate. When control signals are held constant between discrete sample times, PSpice is unleashed to independently manage its micro-steps. 

PSpice aggressively scales its time-step down during switching transitions to capture the nonlinear semiconductor physics, and rapidly expands its time-step during the steady-state conduction phases. The mathematical challenge lies in ensuring that the interpolation of data at the macro-step boundaries does not introduce artificial energy or phase delays into the feedback loops. The interface must rigorously maintain conservation laws at the boundary layer, ensuring that the disparate temporal resolutions do not destabilize the closed-loop control system or artificially damp the resonance of the physical circuit.

## Chapter 6: Digital Control of SMPS

The indispensable nature of SLPS is further illustrated in the design of digitally controlled Switch Mode Power Supplies (SMPS), such as high-frequency DC-DC Buck converters. In modern power electronics, analog controllers are increasingly replaced by digital signal controllers executing discrete PID algorithms . A fundamental pathology in digitally controlled SMPS is the emergence of Limit Cycle Oscillations (LCO)—steady-state oscillations in the output voltage caused by the quantization effects of the ADC and the Digital Pulse Width Modulator (DPWM) . 

In a digital control loop, the output voltage is sampled by an ADC with a finite resolution $q_{ADC}$, defined by the reference voltage and the number of bits. The digital PID calculates a control effort, which is translated into a discrete duty cycle by the DPWM with a resolution $q_{DPWM}$ . The DPWM resolution per switching period is strictly defined by the clock frequency and the number of bits $N_{DPWM}$:

$\Delta D = \frac{1}{2^{N_{DPWM}}}$

If the minimum change in the output voltage caused by the smallest possible DPWM step is greater than the voltage window of a single ADC bin, the controller cannot reach a stable equilibrium. It will continuously toggle between adjacent duty cycles, generating a sustained limit cycle oscillation that manifests as an unacceptable sub-harmonic ripple on the output . Mathematically, to prevent LCO, the static condition requires that the effective resolution of the DPWM must be finer than the ADC resolution scaled by the plant gain:

$q_{DPWM} \le \frac{q_{ADC}}{K_{dc}}$

where $K_{dc}$ is the DC gain of the power stage (e.g., $K_{dc} = V_{in}$ for a buck converter). Furthermore, dynamic conditions involving the integral gain $K_i$ of the compensator must be satisfied to prevent the amplification of quantization noise .

Using MATLAB alone, modeling these quantization effects requires abstract approximations that miss the true physical ripple of the inductor current and the Equivalent Series Resistance (ESR) of the output capacitor. With SLPS, the Buck converter's LC filter, ESR, and switching devices reside in PSpice, generating the exact analog ripple. This ripple is fed into a Simulink ADC block that enforces strict quantization and processing delays. By co-simulating, engineers can visually observe the onset of LCOs, dynamically adjust the DPWM dithering algorithms, and optimize the fixed-point compensator coefficients in Simulink until the PSpice analog output perfectly stabilizes at the target voltage without oscillation .

## Chapter 7: Optimizing Co-Simulation Performance

The sheer computational weight of passing data between two highly complex solvers mandates a rigorous strategy for optimizing co-simulation performance. Unoptimized SLPS models are prone to simulation freezing, numerical stalling, and exponentially long convergence times. The primary vector for optimization lies in relaxing the default PSpice numerical tolerances, which are traditionally set for ultra-high-precision integrated circuit design—a level of fidelity often unnecessary for system-level control verification .

| SPICE Parameter | Definition | Default Value | Recommended SLPS Value | Impact on Co-Simulation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `RELTOL` | Relative error tolerance for voltages and currents. | 0.001 (0.1%) | 0.01 to 0.05 (1% to 5%) | Drastically reduces Newton-Raphson iterations on noisy power buses . |
| `VNTOL` | Absolute voltage tolerance threshold. | 1.0 $\mu$V | 10 $\mu$V to 100 $\mu$V | Prevents stalling on insignificant parasitic voltage floors . |
| `ABSTOL` | Absolute current tolerance threshold. | 1.0 pA | 1.0 nA to 1.0 $\mu$A | Prevents the solver from chasing picoampere leakage currents in power devices . |
| `ITL4` | Transient analysis iteration limit per time-step. | 10 iterations | 40 to 100 iterations | Gives the solver computational "breathing room" during hard-switching transients . |
| `VSECTOL` | Maximum volt-second error for any node. | OFF | ON (e.g., 50nV-sec) | Allows the simulator to backtrack and resolve behavioral switching events accurately . |

To accelerate the simulation, the relative tolerance (`RELTOL`) is typically relaxed from 0.001 to 0.01 or higher . This prevents the Newton-Raphson solver from wasting thousands of iterations attempting to resolve microvolt-level fluctuations. Similarly, `VNTOL` and `ABSTOL` are increased to prevent the solver from stalling on insignificant parasitic noise floors . 

Crucially, when co-simulating high-frequency switching circuits, the `ITL4` parameter must be drastically increased . `ITL4` defines the maximum number of iterations PSpice will attempt at a specific time point before giving up, rejecting the time point, cutting the time-step in half, and trying again. In power electronics featuring hard-switching events, the equations are highly non-linear. By increasing `ITL4` from its default of 10 up to 40 or 100, the solver is given enough iterations to converge on a solution during a difficult switching transient without unnecessarily shrinking the time-step and grinding the co-simulation to a halt . Additionally, selecting Gear integration over Trapezoidal integration in PSpice prevents numerical oscillation artifacts when simulating circuits with rapidly switched inductors or reverse-recovery diodes . On the Simulink side, fixing the maximum step size to coincide with the digital controller's sample rate prevents the ODE solver from taking arbitrarily large steps that could violate the Nyquist criteria of the feedback loop.

## Chapter 8: Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) Preparation

The overarching objective of Model-Based System Design (MBSD) is to eliminate handwritten code errors and accelerate deployment to embedded microcontrollers. The SLPS co-simulation framework acts as the definitive, mandatory verification checkpoint before entering the Hardware-in-the-Loop (HIL) phase of the V-model development cycle . In standard workflows, a digital controller designed in Simulink is first subjected to Software-in-the-Loop (SIL) testing against a highly idealized mathematical plant. However, SIL cannot validate how the controller logic will handle physical phenomena like sensor noise, ground bounce, or component parameter drift.

By routing the Simulink controller through the SLPS interface to a high-fidelity PSpice analog model, the system undergoes a "Virtual HIL" verification. The digital algorithms are subjected to the true electrical stress of the physical plant. Once the controller proves robust against the PSpice physics engine, the Simulink model is locked. Engineers then utilize tools like Embedded Coder or Simulink Coder to automatically generate optimized, production-ready C or C++ code directly from the block diagrams . 

Because the control logic was verified against the PSpice component-level models, the generated C-code can be flashed directly onto a digital signal processor (DSP) or microcontroller with an exceptionally high degree of confidence. This code is then connected to a physical HIL simulator (such as a dSPACE system), replacing the PSpice virtual plant with real-time hardware emulation . The SLPS step ensures that the transition from pure software simulation to physical iron is seamless, having preemptively solved quantization errors, scaling issues, and fixed-point mathematical overflows that traditionally plague the latter stages of embedded deployment.

## Chapter 9: Simulating Automotive Systems

Beyond power converters and motor drives, the SLPS interface is uniquely critical for the development of modern automotive electronic control units (ECUs). In an automotive network, the logical state machines governing vehicle behavior reside in microcontrollers, communicating via robust protocols such as the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus . A major cause of failure in automotive systems is not logical errors in the software, but physical layer signal integrity violations on the CAN bus due to poor topology, excessive stub lengths, or improper termination .

Using SLPS, an engineer models the intricate logic of the ECU in Simulink (e.g., prioritizing messages, handling frame collisions, executing diagnostic state machines). Simulink generates the logical TX and RX bitstreams. These bitstreams cross the SLPS boundary into PSpice, where the physical layer of the CAN bus is rigorously modeled . PSpice treats the CAN bus not as a theoretical data pipe, but as a lossy transmission line governed by Telegrapher's equations . 

To prevent signal reflections that could corrupt the digital bits, the CAN bus requires strict impedance matching. PSpice simulates the physical split termination network—typically a 120-ohm termination split into two 60-ohm resistors (or standard available values like 59.7 $\Omega$ or 62 $\Omega$) with a capacitor tied to ground at the center point . This split termination is vital for filtering out high-frequency common-mode noise in complex industrial environments . The capacitance choice is dependent on the baud rate cutoff frequency:

$C = \frac{1}{2\pi R F_c}$

For a 500 kHz baud rate and a 62 $\Omega$ resistor, a capacitor of approximately 4.7 nF to 5.13 nF is utilized . The SLPS co-simulation allows engineers to observe how the Simulink logic behaves when the PSpice transmission line suffers from electromagnetic interference, degraded differential voltages, or signal reflections bouncing off mismatched nodes . The result is the ability to generate virtual eye-diagrams, verifying that the physical layer will sustain reliable communication before the physical wiring harness is integrated into the vehicle chassis .

## Chapter 10: The Future of System Verification

The integration of disparate domain solvers via the SLPS architecture represents the vanguard of a broader paradigm shift in engineering: the rise of the true Digital Twin and Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) . Historically, simulation was a discrete, static phase in the product development lifecycle, largely abandoned once the physical prototype was manufactured. However, the future of system verification demands continuous integration and continuous deployment (CI/CD) applied directly to physical hardware design.

In this rapidly approaching future, full-vehicle or factory-scale Digital Twins will operate continuously alongside their physical counterparts . The SLPS architecture will evolve from a desktop engineering tool into a real-time, cloud-based predictive engine. As physical sensors on a deployed EV motor drive or a smart factory robotics arm stream telemetry data back to the server, the SLPS co-simulation will run in parallel. Simulink will process the algorithmic logic and machine learning models, while PSpice will continuously solve the degraded state of the physical components (e.g., tracking the thermal breakdown of an IGBT's gate oxide or the shifting ESR of a capacitor). By maintaining this synchronized, bi-directional coupling of the ideal algorithmic intent (MATLAB) with the harsh, entropic reality of the physical world (PSpice), engineers will be able to predict catastrophic system failures, optimize performance via over-the-air updates, and realize the ultimate goal of holistic, closed-loop lifecycle management.
