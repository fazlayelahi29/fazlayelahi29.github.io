# CHAPTER 9: TRANSLATING SEMICONDUCTOR PHYSICS INTO C-CODE SUBROUTINES

## SECTION 1: THE GAP BETWEEN PHYSICS AND CODE

At the foundational level of solid-state physics, the behavior of charge carriers within a semiconductor crystal lattice is governed by coupled partial differential equations (PDEs) spanning quantum mechanics and classical electrodynamics . A rigorous first-principles evaluation of a single submicron transistor requires solving the time-dependent Schrödinger wave equation to determine the quantum-mechanical carrier probability density across energy bands:

$i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi(\mathbf{r}, t)}{\partial t} = \left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*} \nabla^2 + V(\mathbf{r}, t) \right) \Psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$

Simultaneously, the macroscopic electromagnetic field distribution across the device geometry is dictated by Maxwell's equations, which in the electrostatic and quasi-static semiconductor regime reduce to Poisson's equation coupling the electrostatic potential $\psi$ to the space-charge density $\rho$, alongside the continuity equations for electrons ($n$) and holes ($p$) derived from the Boltzmann Transport Equation (BTE):

$\nabla \cdot (\epsilon_s \nabla \psi) = -q \left( p - n + N_D^+ - N_A^- \right)$

$\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{q} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_n + G_n - R_n$

$\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = -\frac{1}{q} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_p + G_p - R_p$

In Technology Computer-Aided Design (TCAD) device simulators, these equations are discretized over a two-dimensional or three-dimensional finite-element mesh consisting of tens of thousands of spatial nodes . Solving the coupled Poisson and drift-diffusion equations for a single bias point of a single transistor requires inverting large sparse matrices iteratively over several seconds or minutes of CPU time . When this computational burden is extrapolated to modern integrated circuits containing millions or billions of transistors and interconnect parasitics across seven to twelve metal layers, evaluating spatial PDEs inside a time-domain transient simulation running from kilohertz to gigahertz frequencies over millions of picosecond time steps becomes mathematically intractable . 

To bridge the chasm between continuous physical reality and practical Electronic Design Automation (EDA) throughput, circuit simulators such as Synopsys HSPICE abandon spatial mesh discretization in favor of macroscopic, analytical representations known as Compact Models . Compact modeling collapses the three-dimensional distributed physics of the semiconductor device into a zero-dimensional, lumped network of nonlinear algebraic and ordinary differential equations (ODEs) expressed purely in terms of terminal branch voltages and state variables . By integrating Poisson's equation and the drift-diffusion equations analytically under regional or surface-potential approximations, and augmenting those analytical cores with empirical fitting parameters to capture higher-order physical effects, a compact model reduces a ten-thousand-node spatial PDE system into a deterministic C subroutine that executes in microseconds .

This architectural compromise is governed by the fundamental accuracy-speed-capacity trilemma of circuit simulation: increasing physical fidelity directly degrades simulation throughput and capacity, whereas higher abstraction expands capacity at the expense of signoff precision . Within the SPICE paradigm, execution runtime is strictly partitioned into two primary computational phases: Device Evaluation, which calculates the physical branch currents, charges, and their Jacobian derivatives for every device instance, and Matrix Solution, which solves the resulting sparse linear system via LU factorization . Because the matrix solver scales super-linearly ($O(N^{1.2})$ to $O(N^{1.5})$) with node count while device evaluation scales linearly ($O(N)$) with the number of nonlinear components, device evaluation typically consumes between 50% and 80% of total CPU time in smaller and medium-sized circuits, making the efficiency of the underlying C-code subroutines the decisive factor in simulator performance .

## SECTION 2: THE ARCHITECTURE OF SPICE DEVICE ROUTINES

When SPICE transitioned from the monolithic FORTRAN codebase of SPICE2 to the modular C architecture of SPICE3 in 1985—an architectural lineage shared and heavily extended by commercial engines like HSPICE—the simulator core was decoupled from individual semiconductor physics implementations through an object-oriented C dispatch interface . The core numerical engine, which manages the Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix, the Newton-Raphson convergence loop, and the multi-step time-domain integrator, has zero hard-coded knowledge of whether it is simulating a linear resistor, a Shockley diode, a Gummel-Poon BJT, or a submicron MOSFET . Instead, every device family is encapsulated as a self-contained module consisting of header definitions and specialized C source files (such as `dio.c`, `dioload.c`, `diotemp.c`, `bjt.c`, and `bjtload.c`) that expose a standardized table of function pointers via the `SPICEdev` master descriptor structure .

At initialization, the simulator populates a global registry array, `SPICEdev *devList[]`, where each index corresponds to a unique device type identifier . Within each device directory, the maintainer defines a static instance of the `SPICEdev` struct that binds the abstract engine hooks—such as `DEVsetup` for node binding and sparse matrix pointer allocation, `DEVtemperature` for temperature-dependent parameter pre-computation, `DEVload` for per-iteration physics evaluation and Jacobian stamping, `DEVacLoad` for small-signal complex admittance loading, and `DEVtrunc` for local truncation error timestep control—to the concrete C functions implemented for that specific device .

To minimize memory overhead when instantiating millions of identical components, the data architecture enforces a strict two-tier hierarchy consisting of Models (`GENmodel`) and Instances (`GENinstance`) . Shared process parameters specified in a netlist `.MODEL` card—such as saturation current density, oxide thickness, or grading coefficients—are stored once inside a device-specific model structure such as `DIOmodel` . Individual circuit elements (`D1`, `D2`, `Q1`) are allocated as lightweight instance structures such as `DIOinstance` containing only geometry modifiers like `area` or multiplicity `m`, external and internal node indices, pre-allocated pointers into the sparse matrix, and indices into the state vector tables . Models of the same device type are chained together in a singly linked list, and each model structure anchors a child linked list of all instances referencing that `.MODEL` card .

During the setup phase (`DEVsetup`), the simulator performs crucial topological optimizations such as Node Collapse . For instance, a physical junction diode includes an extrinsic parasitic series resistance $R_s$, which necessitates allocating an internal anode prime node (`DIOposPrimeNode`) between the external terminal and the intrinsic junction . However, if the user specifies $R_s = 0$ in the `.MODEL` statement, or if the resistance falls below a minimum threshold, adding an extra row and column to the MNA matrix for an ideal short circuit is both computationally wasteful and numerically hazardous . Inside `DIOsetup`, the C code inspects `model->DIOresistGiven`; if $R_s = 0$, it maps `here->DIOposPrimeNode = here->DIOposNode`, collapsing the internal node directly onto the external terminal before requesting matrix element pointers via `TSTALLOC` .

## SECTION 3: THE `DEVLOAD` SUBROUTINE

If the `SPICEdev` table is the structural skeleton of the simulator, the `DEVload` subroutine is its computational heart . During every single Newton-Raphson (NR) iteration of a DC operating point, DC sweep, or transient time point, the core solver loops over the active `devList[]` array and invokes `(*(devList[i]->DEVload))(ckt->CKThead[i], ckt)` . Because semiconductor devices are governed by nonlinear current-voltage relationships $I = f(\mathbf{V})$, the global circuit equations cannot be solved directly by linear algebra . Instead, the Newton-Raphson algorithm iteratively linearizes every nonlinear branch around the present trial voltage vector $\mathbf{V}^{(k)}$ using a first-order Taylor series expansion .

Consider a two-terminal nonlinear element connected between node $p$ (positive/anode) and node $n$ (negative/cathode), with branch voltage $v_d = v_p - v_n$ and current $i_d = f(v_d)$ . At Newton-Raphson iteration $k+1$, the linearized branch current is approximated from the previous iterate $v_d^{(k)}$ as:

$i_d^{(k+1)} \approx f\left(v_d^{(k)}\right) + \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_d}\right|_{v_d^{(k)}} \left( v_d^{(k+1)} - v_d^{(k)} \right)$

By defining the equivalent linearized conductance (the Jacobian derivative) $g_{eq}^{(k)}$ and the equivalent Norton companion current source $I_{eq}^{(k)}$ as:

$g_{eq}^{(k)} = \left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial v_d}\right|_{v_d^{(k)}}$

$I_{eq}^{(k)} = f\left(v_d^{(k)}\right) - g_{eq}^{(k)} v_d^{(k)}$

the nonlinear branch equation reduces to a linear Norton equivalent circuit valid specifically for iteration $k+1$ :

$i_d^{(k+1)} = g_{eq}^{(k)} \left( v_p^{(k+1)} - v_n^{(k+1)} \right) + I_{eq}^{(k)}$

It is the exclusive responsibility of `DEVload` to execute this linearization for every instance of a device model and stamp the resulting $g_{eq}^{(k)}$ terms into the sparse Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) Jacobian matrix $\mathbf{Y}$ while subtracting $I_{eq}^{(k)}$ from the Right-Hand Side (RHS) excitation vector $\mathbf{J}$ . Specifically, for a branch oriented from node $p$ to node $n$, Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) requires stamping the $2 \times 2$ admittance template into the matrix and the companion current into the RHS vector according to:

$\begin{bmatrix} \ddots & \vdots & \vdots & \reflectbox{$\ddots$} \\ \cdots & +g_{eq}^{(k)} & -g_{eq}^{(k)} & \cdots \\ \cdots & -g_{eq}^{(k)} & +g_{eq}^{(k)} & \cdots \\ \reflectbox{$\ddots$} & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ v_p^{(k+1)} \\ v_n^{(k+1)} \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vdots \\ -I_{eq}^{(k)} \\ +I_{eq}^{(k)} \\ \vdots \end{bmatrix}$

To achieve maximum execution speed, `DEVload` never searches the sparse matrix by row and column indices `(p, n)` during the simulation loop . During `DEVsetup`, the macro `TSTALLOC(ptr, row, col)` asks the sparse matrix package (`SMPmakeElt`) to allocate the non-zero matrix entry once and stores its direct `double *` memory address inside the device instance struct (`here->DIOposPrimePosPrimePtr`) . Inside `DEVload`, stamping the matrix requires only a single pointer dereference and floating-point addition (`*(here->DIOposPrimePosPrimePtr) += gdeq;`), eliminating hash-table or linked-list traversal overhead inside the critical loop.

```c
/* Structural anatomy of the outer loops and MNA stamping inside DIOload (dioload.c) */
int DIOload(GENmodel *inModel, CKTcircuit *ckt)
{
    DIOmodel *model = (DIOmodel *)inModel;
    DIOinstance *here;
    double vd, vdtemp, delvd, cd, gd, cdeq;
    double gspr;
    int Check;

    /* Outer Loop: Iterate over all .MODEL definitions of type Diode */
    for ( ; model != NULL; model = model->DIOnextModel ) {
        
        /* Inner Loop: Iterate over all circuit instances sharing this .MODEL */
        for ( here = model->DIOinstances; here != NULL; here = here->DIOnextInstance ) {
            
            /* 1. Compute scaled series conductance Gspr = (1/Rs) * Area * M */
            gspr = model->DIOtConductance * here->DIOarea * here->DIOm;

            /* 2. Extract trial branch voltage from the previous NR solution vector */
            if (ckt->CKTmode & MODEINITJCT) {
                vd = here->DIOtVcrit;
            } else {
                vd = *(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->DIOposPrimeNode) -
                     *(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->DIOnegNode);
            }

            /* 3. Apply voltage limiting (pnjlim) & evaluate device physics (cd, gd) */
            /*    [Detailed in Sections 4, 5, and 6]                                 */
            /* ...                                                                   */

            /* 4. Form the Norton equivalent current: Ieq = Id - gd * Vd             */
            cdeq = cd - gd * vd;

            /* 5. Persist state variables for convergence checks and time integration */
            *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOvoltage) = vd;
            *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcurrent) = cd;
            *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOconduct) = gd;

            /* 6. Stamp the RHS Vector (KCL current contributions)                   */
            *(ckt->CKTrhs + here->DIOnegNode)      += cdeq;
            *(ckt->CKTrhs + here->DIOposPrimeNode) -= cdeq;

            /* 7. Stamp the Sparse MNA Jacobian Matrix via pre-bound double pointers */
            *(here->DIOposPosPtr)           += gspr;
            *(here->DIOnegNegPtr)           += gd;
            *(here->DIOposPrimePosPrimePtr) += (gd + gspr);
            *(here->DIOposPosPrimePtr)      -= gspr;
            *(here->DIOposPrimePosPtr)      -= gspr;
            *(here->DIOnegPosPrimePtr)      -= gd;
            *(here->DIOposPrimeNegPtr)      -= gd;
        }
    }
    return(OK);
}
```

The state machine inside `DEVload` is controlled by the bitmask integer `ckt->CKTmode`, which informs the device routine of the exact operational state of the solver . Flags such as `MODEDC` (DC operating point or sweep), `MODETRAN` (transient analysis), `MODEINITJCT` (initial junction voltage seeding at iteration zero), `MODEINITFIX` (fixed initial conditions via `.IC` or `OFF`), `MODEINITFLOAT` (standard floating Newton-Raphson iteration), and `MODEINITPRED` (first iteration of a new transient time step using polynomial extrapolation from historical state vectors `CKTstate1` and `CKTstate2`) dictate how `DEVload` obtains its trial voltage `vd` and whether capacitive state integration must be executed .

## SECTION 4: CODING THE SHOCKLEY DIODE EQUATION

Within the central physics evaluation block of `DIOload`, the simulator must compute the DC conduction current $I_d$ and its partial derivative $g_d = \frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_d}$ across four distinct operating regimes: forward bias, normal reverse bias, reverse breakdown, and deep reverse saturation . The baseline Shockley diode law models the diffusion and recombination of minority carriers across a $pn$ depletion region as:

$I_d(V_d) = I_s \left( \exp\left(\frac{V_d}{N \cdot V_t}\right) - 1 \right) + V_d \cdot G_{\text{min}}$

where $I_s$ is the geometry- and temperature-scaled saturation current (`here->DIOtSatCur`), $N$ is the emission or ideality coefficient (`model->DIOemissionCoeff`), $V_t = \frac{k_B T}{q}$ is the thermal voltage, and $G_{\text{min}}$ (`ckt->CKTgmin`, defaulting to $10^{-12}\text{ }\Omega^{-1}$) is a parallel minimum conductance injected across every nonlinear junction . Differentiating with respect to $V_d$ yields the analytic small-signal and Jacobian conductance :

$g_d(V_d) = \frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_d} = \frac{I_s}{N \cdot V_t} \exp\left(\frac{V_d}{N \cdot V_t}\right) + G_{\text{min}}$

While these two equations appear mathematically straightforward on paper, their direct translation into production C code exposes severe performance and numerical stability bottlenecks . First, the standard C math library function `exp(double x)` (defined in `<math.h>`) is one of the most computationally expensive scalar instructions in scientific computing . While an IEEE-754 double-precision addition or multiplication executes in $1$ to $4$ CPU clock cycles on modern superscalar pipelines, a transcendental `exp()` call requires range reduction, polynomial approximation, and IEEE exponent bit reconstruction, consuming $20$ to $60$ clock cycles depending on hardware microarchitecture . In a circuit with $100,000$ junctions running $10,000$ transient time steps at $4$ Newton-Raphson iterations per step, `exp()` is executed $4 \times 10^8$ times . 

As noted in classic compact modeling literature, a naive or automatically generated implementation that evaluates `Id = Is * (exp(vd / vte) - 1.0)` and subsequently evaluates `gd = (Is / vte) * exp(vd / vte)` commits a cardinal error of SPICE programming: calling `exp()` twice for the exact same argument . Hand-coded production C routines always assign the result of `exp(vd / vte)` to a local register variable `evd` once, deriving both `cd` and `gd` via fast floating-point multiplications .

Second, in reverse bias where $V_d < -3 N V_t$, the exponential term $\exp(V_d / N V_t)$ decays rapidly toward zero, and for large negative voltages it risks floating-point underflow . More importantly, if the diode were left purely governed by $I_d = -I_s$, the derivative $\frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_d}$ would become identically zero (or approach zero below IEEE-754 machine epsilon $\approx 2.22 \times 10^{-16}$) . A zero conductance on the diagonal of the MNA matrix creates a structurally singular Jacobian matrix when two reverse-biased junctions meet at a floating node, causing the LU solver to abort with a zero-pivot error . In SPICE3 and HSPICE, when $V_d$ falls into the reverse regime ($V_d < -3 N V_t$), the code bypasses the `exp()` call entirely and switches to a cubic polynomial continuation that smoothly matches the value and derivative at $-3 N V_t$, while adding `ckt->CKTgmin` to guarantee positive-definite diagonal dominance .

Third, if a reverse breakdown voltage $B_V$ (`model->DIObreakdownVoltage`) is specified and $V_d$ drops below $-B_V$, the junction enters avalanche breakdown, modeled by a reverse exponential curve shifted to match the breakdown knee at $V_{brk}$ . Furthermore, HSPICE introduces the control option `EXPMAX` to cap the maximum argument passed to exponential functions well below the IEEE-754 double-precision overflow ceiling of $\ln(1.797 \times 10^{308}) \approx 709.78$ . Above the threshold argument where $\frac{V_d}{N V_t} > \text{EXPMAX}$, HSPICE replaces the true exponential with a continuous linear tangent extrapolation (`limexp`), preventing `Inf` and `NaN` propagation even if voltage limiting is bypassed .

```c
/* Production C-code implementation of the 4-regime Shockley Diode & Safe Exponential */
#include <math.h>

/* Safe linear-extrapolated exponential (limexp) governed by EXPMAX */
static inline void hspice_safe_exp(double arg, double expmax, double *val, double *dval)
{
    double max_exp_val;
    if (arg > expmax) {
        /* Linearly extrapolate tangent above EXPMAX to prevent IEEE-754 overflow */
        max_exp_val = exp(expmax);
        *val  = max_exp_val * (1.0 + (arg - expmax));
        *dval = max_exp_val;
    } else if (arg < -expmax) {
        *val  = 0.0;
        *dval = 0.0;
    } else {
        *val  = exp(arg);
        *dval = *val;
    }
}

/* Inside DIOload: Multi-regime DC current (cd) and conductance (gd) evaluation */
void evaluate_shockley_diode(
    double vd,          /* Limited branch voltage V(posPrime, neg) */
    double csat,        /* Scaled saturation current Is(T) * Area * M */
    double vte,         /* Scaled thermal voltage N * k * T / q */
    int    brk_given,   /* Flag: Breakdown voltage BV specified in .MODEL */
    double vbrk,        /* Effective breakdown knee voltage */
    double gmin,        /* Simulator minimum conductance (CKTgmin) */
    double expmax,      /* Maximum exponential argument (EXPMAX option) */
    double *cd_out,     /* Output: DC branch current */
    double *gd_out      /* Output: Linearized Jacobian conductance dId/dVd */
)
{
    double evd, devd, arg, evrev, devrev;
    double cd, gd;

    if (vd >= -3.0 * vte) {
        /* REGIME 1: Forward Bias and Shallow Reverse Bias (vd >= -3*N*Vt) */
        arg = vd / vte;
        hspice_safe_exp(arg, expmax, &evd, &devd);
        cd = csat * (evd - 1.0) + gmin * vd;
        gd = csat * devd / vte + gmin;
    } 
    else if (!brk_given || vd >= -vbrk) {
        /* REGIME 2: Normal Reverse Bias (-vbrk <= vd < -3*N*Vt)                */
        /* Avoid expensive exp() call; use cubic continuation for C1 smoothness */
        arg = 3.0 * vte / (vd * M_E);
        arg = arg * arg * arg;
        cd = -csat * (1.0 + arg) + gmin * vd;
        gd = csat * 3.0 * arg / vd + gmin;
    } 
    else {
        /* REGIME 3: Reverse Avalanche Breakdown (vd < -vbrk)                   */
        arg = -(vbrk + vd) / vte;
        hspice_safe_exp(arg, expmax, &evrev, &devrev);
        cd = -csat * evrev + gmin * vd;
        gd = csat * devrev / vte + gmin;
    }

    *cd_out = cd;
    *gd_out = gd;
}
```

## SECTION 5: THE PNJLIMIT ALGORITHM

Even with a safe exponential wrapper, applying raw, unmodified Newton-Raphson iterations to a circuit containing $pn$ junctions almost universally results in catastrophic convergence failure or extreme iteration counts . The root cause is the fundamental asymmetry between the linear tangent approximation of the Newton-Raphson method and the exponential curvature of the Shockley equation .

Consider a simple series diode-resistor circuit driven by a $5.0\text{ V}$ voltage source (`Vsrc = 5.0`, `R = 1e3`, `Is = 1.0e-14`, `Vt = 0.02585`) . Suppose at iteration $k=0$, the initial voltage across the diode is $V_d^{(0)} = 0\text{ V}$. At $V_d^{(0)} = 0\text{ V}$, the diode current is $0\text{ A}$ and its small-signal conductance is $g_d^{(0)} = \frac{I_s}{V_t} + G_{\text{min}} \approx 1.38 \times 10^{-12}\text{ S}$ . Because the diode looks like an open circuit compared to the $1\text{ k}\Omega$ resistor, the linear MNA solver places virtually the entire $5.0\text{ V}$ supply voltage directly across the diode for iteration $k=1$: $V_d^{(1)} \approx 5.0\text{ V}$ . When the next `DEVload` pass attempts to evaluate $I_d(5.0\text{ V})$ at room temperature ($V_t \approx 25.853\text{ mV}$) :

$I_d(5.0\text{ V}) = 10^{-14} \left( \exp\left(\frac{5.0}{0.025853}\right) - 1 \right) = 10^{-14} \cdot e^{193.4} \approx 9.8 \times 10^{69}\text{ A}$

Had the supply voltage been $30\text{ V}$ (giving an exponent of $30 / 0.025853 \approx 1160.4$), $\exp(1160.4)$ would immediately exceed the IEEE-754 double-precision maximum of $1.797 \times 10^{308}$ ($e^{709.78}$), generating a floating-point `Inf` overflow that corrupts the entire MNA matrix . Even at $5.0\text{ V}$, where $e^{193.4}$ fits in double precision, the reverse problem occurs on the subsequent steps: the conductance at $5.0\text{ V}$ is so colossal ($\approx 3.8 \times 10^{71}\text{ S}$) that each subsequent Newton-Raphson step can only reduce the junction voltage by approximately one thermal voltage ($\Delta V_d \approx -V_t \approx -25.85\text{ mV}$) per iteration :

$V_d^{(k+1)} = V_d^{(k)} - \frac{I_d\left(V_d^{(k)}\right)}{g_d\left(V_d^{(k)}\right)} \approx V_d^{(k)} - \frac{I_s e^{V_d^{(k)}/V_t}}{\frac{I_s}{V_t} e^{V_d^{(k)}/V_t}} = V_d^{(k)} - V_t$

Consequently, an unconstrained Newton-Raphson solver suffers from millivolt-creep, requiring roughly two hundred iterations merely to walk down the exponential wall from $5.0\text{ V}$ back to the physical operating point near $0.7\text{ V}$ .

To solve both overflow and millivolt-creep simultaneously, Laurence Nagel introduced the `pnjlim` (`DEVpnjlim`) routine in SPICE2, which acts as a nonlinear postcondition corrector between iterations . Rather than modifying the residual equations directly, `DEVpnjlim` intercepts the proposed trial voltage $V_{\text{new}}$ coming out of the linear solver and compares it against the previously accepted voltage $V_{\text{old}}$ (`*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOvoltage)`) and a device-specific critical voltage $V_{\text{crit}}$ .

The critical voltage $V_{\text{crit}}$ is defined mathematically as the forward junction voltage at which the theoretical radius of curvature of the Shockley diode curve is minimized, corresponding to the knee of maximum nonlinearity :

$V_{\text{crit}} = N V_t \ln \left( \frac{N V_t}{\sqrt{2} I_s} \right)$

For a typical silicon diode at $300\text{ K}$ with $I_s = 10^{-14}\text{ A}$ and $N = 1$, $V_{\text{crit}} \approx 0.71\text{ V}$ . Because $V_{\text{crit}}$ depends only on model or instance parameters and temperature, it is pre-calculated once inside `DEVtemp` and cached in the instance structure (`here->DIOtVcrit`). Inside `DEVload`, `DEVpnjlim` enforces a strict physical heuristic: in high forward bias ($V_{\text{new}} > V_{\text{crit}}$), the junction current—rather than the junction voltage—is forced to step linearly between iterations, meaning the junction voltage may only increase logarithmically . Specifically, if $V_{\text{new}} > V_{\text{crit}}$ and the proposed voltage step $|V_{\text{new}} - V_{\text{old}}|$ exceeds $2 N V_t$ ($\approx 51.7\text{ mV}$), `DEVpnjlim` compresses the voltage update using a natural logarithm (`log()`) transformation and sets a non-convergence flag (`*icheck = 1`) that prohibits the Newton-Raphson loop from declaring convergence on that iteration :

$V_{\text{new, damped}} = \begin{cases} V_{\text{old}} + N V_t \ln \left( 1 + \dfrac{V_{\text{new}} - V_{\text{old}}}{N V_t} \right) & \text{if } V_{\text{old}} > 0 \text{ and } 1 + \dfrac{V_{\text{new}} - V_{\text{old}}}{N V_t} > 0 \\[12pt] V_{\text{crit}} & \text{if } V_{\text{old}} > 0 \text{ and } 1 + \dfrac{V_{\text{new}} - V_{\text{old}}}{N V_t} \le 0 \\[12pt] N V_t \ln \left( \dfrac{V_{\text{new}}}{N V_t} \right) & \text{if } V_{\text{old}} \le 0 \end{cases}$

When the $5.0\text{ V}$ initial guess is passed into `DEVpnjlim` from $V_{\text{old}} = 0\text{ V}$, the third branch executes: $V_{\text{new, damped}} = 0.025853 \ln(5.0 / 0.025853) \approx 0.136\text{ V}$ . On the second iteration, if the solver again proposes $5.0\text{ V}$ from $V_{\text{old}} = 0.136\text{ V}$, the first branch executes: $V_{\text{new, damped}} = 0.136 + 0.025853 \ln(1 + (5.0 - 0.136)/0.025853) \approx 0.272\text{ V}$ . Instead of leaping to $5.0\text{ V}$ and requiring two hundred millivolt-creep iterations to descend, `DEVpnjlim` smoothly guides the iterate up to the $0.7\text{ V}$ operating point in roughly a dozen iterations .

```c
/* Authentic SPICE3 / HSPICE C implementation of DEVpnjlim (devsup.c) */
#include <math.h>

double DEVpnjlim(
    double vnew,    /* Proposed junction voltage from current MNA solution */
    double vold,    /* Accepted junction voltage from previous NR iteration */
    double vt,      /* Scaled thermal voltage (N * k * T / q)               */
    double vcrit,   /* Critical knee voltage Vcrit                          */
    int *icheck     /* Output pointer: set to 1 if voltage was clamped      */
)
{
    double arg;

    /* Trigger limiting only above vcrit when step size exceeds 2 * vt */
    if ((vnew > vcrit) && (fabs(vnew - vold) > (vt + vt))) {
        if (vold > 0.0) {
            arg = 1.0 + (vnew - vold) / vt;
            if (arg > 0.0) {
                vnew = vold + vt * log(arg);
            } else {
                vnew = vcrit;
            }
        } else {
            vnew = vt * log(vnew / vt);
        }
        *icheck = 1; /* Signal to CKTnoncon that limiting modified the iterate */
    } else {
        *icheck = 0; /* Step accepted without damping */
    }
    return(vnew);
}
```

A critical subtlety arises when coding complementary $P$-type devices such as PNP bipolar transistors or PMOS bulk junctions . Compact models are universally written in terms of $N$-type polarity by multiplying terminal voltages by a polarity integer `type` ($\text{NPN/NMOS} = +1$, $\text{PNP/PMOS} = -1$) . If a maintainer mistakenly writes `vbe = type * DEVpnjlim(vbe_raw, ...)` without passing the properly oriented positive-forward junction drop into `DEVpnjlim`, the limiter compares a negative voltage against a positive `vcrit` and fails to limit altogether . Thus, production C code, or the `typedpnjlim` wrapper, always normalizes the branch voltage by `type` before invoking `DEVpnjlim` . Furthermore, whenever `DEVpnjlim` clamps $V_{\text{new}}$ to $V_{\text{damped}}$, the Norton companion current source stamped into the RHS vector must be adjusted for the voltage discrepancy $\Delta V_{\text{lim}} = V_{\text{damped}} - V_{\text{new, raw}}$ so that the linear system solves self-consistently around the damped operating point $V_{\text{damped}}$ rather than the raw trial point .

## SECTION 6: DYNAMIC JUNCTION CAPACITANCE

During transient analysis (`ckt->CKTmode & MODETRAN`) or small-signal AC analysis, a semiconductor junction exhibits nonlinear charge storage $Q_d(V_d)$ arising from two distinct physical mechanisms: depletion capacitance (space-charge region widening and narrowing) and diffusion capacitance (minority carrier transit-time charge storage) . The total incremental junction capacitance $C_d(V_d) = \frac{\partial Q_d}{\partial V_d}$ is the sum of both components:

$C_d(V_d) = C_{\text{diff}}(V_d) + C_{\text{depl}}(V_d) = \tau_T \frac{\partial I_d}{\partial V_d} + C_{\text{depl}}(V_d)$

where $\tau_T$ (`model->DIOtransitTime`, parameter `TT`) is the mean minority carrier transit time . For the depletion component, solving Poisson's equation across a one-dimensional $pn$ step or linearly graded junction yields the classic reverse-bias depletion capacitance formula:

$C_{\text{depl}}(V_d) = \frac{C_{J0} \cdot A}{\left( 1 - \dfrac{V_d}{V_J} \right)^M} \quad \text{for } V_d < F_C \cdot V_J$

where $C_{J0}$ is the zero-bias junction capacitance per unit area (`CJO`), $A$ is the junction area (`area`), $V_J$ is the built-in contact potential (`VJ` or `PHI`), and $M$ is the junction grading coefficient (`M`, typically $0.5$ for abrupt junctions and $0.33$ for linearly graded junctions) . 

Here again, theoretical physics collides with floating-point numerical reality . As the forward bias $V_d$ approaches the built-in potential $V_J$, the denominator $\left(1 - \frac{V_d}{V_J}\right)^M$ approaches zero, causing $C_{\text{depl}}(V_d) \to \infty$, and for $V_d > V_J$ the base of the fractional exponent becomes negative, producing a complex number (`NaN` in C's `pow()` function) . To eliminate this pole, SPICE defines a forward-bias depletion capacitance coefficient $F_C$ (`model->DIOdepletionCapCoeff`, defaulting to $0.5$). Below $V_d = F_C V_J$, the exact power-law formula is integrated analytically to yield charge $Q_{\text{depl}}(V_d)$. Above $V_d = F_C V_J$, `DEVload` switches to a linear tangent extrapolation of $C_{\text{depl}}(V_d)$ and a quadratic polynomial for $Q_{\text{depl}}(V_d)$ using three pre-computed constants $F_1$, $F_2$, and $F_3$ cached during `DEVtemp`:

$C_{\text{depl}}(V_d) = \frac{C_{J0} \cdot A}{F_2} \left( F_3 + \frac{M \cdot V_d}{V_J} \right) \quad \text{for } V_d \ge F_C \cdot V_J$

$Q_{\text{depl}}(V_d) = C_{J0} \cdot A \cdot F_1 + \frac{C_{J0} \cdot A}{F_2} \left[ F_3 \left( V_d - F_C V_J \right) + \frac{M}{2 V_J} \left( V_d^2 - (F_C V_J)^2 \right) \right]$

where $F_1 = \frac{V_J}{1 - M} \left[ 1 - (1 - F_C)^{1 - M} \right]$, $F_2 = (1 - F_C)^{1 + M}$, and $F_3 = 1 - F_C (1 + M)$.

Once the total charge $Q_d(t_n) = \tau_T I_d(t_n) + Q_{\text{depl}}(t_n)$ and total capacitance $C_d(t_n) = \frac{\partial Q_d}{\partial V_d}$ are evaluated and stored in the current state vector (`CKTstate0`), the simulator must convert the time derivative $i_{\text{cap}}(t_n) = \frac{d Q_d}{dt}$ into a discrete-time algebraic Norton companion circuit using a Linear Multi-Step (LMS) numerical integration method .

http://googleusercontent.com/visual_element_2

As governed by the Dahlquist stability barrier, no linear multi-step integration method of order greater than $2$ can be unconditionally A-stable . Consequently, SPICE restricts its core integration engines to first-order Backward Euler (BE), second-order Trapezoidal (TR), and higher-order Gear (BDF) methods, which are controlled in HSPICE via `.OPTION METHOD` and `.OPTION MAXORD` . Regardless of which integration order is active at the current time point, the integration subroutine `NIintegrate(ckt, &geq, &ceq, cap, qcap_idx)` abstracts the difference equation into a universal linear form utilizing two pre-computed integration coefficients stored in the `ckt->CKTag[]` array:

$i_{\text{cap}}\left(t_{n+1}\right) = \left.\frac{d Q_d}{dt}\right|_{t_{n+1}} = \text{ag}[0] \cdot Q_d\left(t_{n+1}\right) + \text{cceq}$

where $\text{cceq}$ is a linear combination of historical charges $Q_d(t_n), Q_d(t_{n-1}), \dots$ and historical capacitor currents $i_{\text{cap}}(t_n)$ stored in `CKTstate1`, `CKTstate2`, and deeper history tables. For Backward Euler with step size $h$, $\text{ag}[0] = \frac{1}{h}$ and $\text{ag}[1] = -\frac{1}{h}$ . For the Trapezoidal rule, $\text{ag}[0] = \frac{2}{h}$ and $\text{ag}[1] = -\frac{2}{h}$ . Differentiating $i_{\text{cap}}(t_{n+1})$ with respect to $V_d(t_{n+1})$ via the chain rule yields the transient equivalent capacitor conductance $g_{\text{eq,cap}}$ and companion current $I_{\text{eq,cap}}$:

$g_{\text{eq,cap}} = \frac{\partial i_{\text{cap}}}{\partial V_d} = \text{ag}[0] \cdot \frac{\partial Q_d}{\partial V_d} = \text{ag}[0] \cdot C_d\left(V_d^{(k)}\right)$

$I_{\text{eq,cap}} = i_{\text{cap}}^{(k)} - g_{\text{eq,cap}} \cdot V_d^{(k)}$

Inside `DIOload`, `geq` and `ceq` returned by `NIintegrate` are added directly to the DC conductance `gd` and DC current `cd` before stamping the MNA matrix, seamlessly unifying static and dynamic device physics into a single linear matrix solve .

```c
/* C-code implementation of Dynamic Junction Capacitance & NIintegrate in DIOload */
int evaluate_diode_capacitance_and_integrate(
    CKTcircuit *ckt,
    DIOmodel *model,
    DIOinstance *here,
    double vd,          /* Current trial junction voltage */
    double cd,          /* Evaluated DC conduction current */
    double gd,          /* Evaluated DC conductance dId/dVd */
    double *gd_total,   /* In/Out: Total conductance (DC + Transient Cap) */
    double *cd_total    /* In/Out: Total current (DC + Transient Cap) */
)
{
    double czero, czof2, arg, sarg;
    double capd, qd;
    double geq, ceq;
    int error;

    /* Scale zero-bias depletion capacitance by instance Area and Multiplicity */
    czero = here->DIOtJctCap * here->DIOarea * here->DIOm;

    if (vd < here->DIOtDepCap) {
        /* Normal Depletion Region: vd < FC * VJ */
        arg  = 1.0 - vd / here->DIOtJctPot;
        sarg = exp(-model->DIOgradingCoeff * log(arg)); /* arg^(-M) */
        qd   = here->DIOtJctPot * czero * (1.0 - arg * sarg) / (1.0 - model->DIOgradingCoeff);
        capd = czero * sarg;
    } else {
        /* Forward-Biased Extrapolation Region: vd >= FC * VJ */
        czof2 = czero / model->DIOtF2;
        qd    = czero * here->DIOtF1 + czof2 * (
                    model->DIOtF3 * (vd - here->DIOtDepCap) +
                    (model->DIOgradingCoeff / (2.0 * here->DIOtJctPot)) *
                    (vd * vd - here->DIOtDepCap * here->DIOtDepCap)
                );
        capd  = czof2 * (model->DIOtF3 + model->DIOgradingCoeff * vd / here->DIOtJctPot);
    }

    /* Add Transit-Time Diffusion Charge (TT * Id) and Capacitance (TT * gd) */
    qd   += model->DIOtransitTime * cd;
    capd += model->DIOtransitTime * gd;

    /* Store total charge and incremental capacitance into Current State Vector */
    *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcapCharge) = qd;
    *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcapCurrent) = 0.0;

    /* If not in transient mode, return pure DC values */
    if (!(ckt->CKTmode & (MODETRAN | MODETRANOP))) {
        return(OK);
    }

    /* Seed historical state vector on initial transient pass */
    if (ckt->CKTmode & MODEINITTRAN) {
        *(ckt->CKTstate1 + here->DIOcapCharge) = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcapCharge);
    }

    /* Apply LMS Numerical Integration (Backward Euler / Trapezoidal / Gear) */
    error = NIintegrate(ckt, &geq, &ceq, capd, here->DIOcapCharge);
    if (error) return(error);

    if (ckt->CKTmode & MODEINITTRAN) {
        *(ckt->CKTstate1 + here->DIOcapCurrent) = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcapCurrent);
    }

    /* Augment DC conductance and current with transient capacitive Norton equivalent */
    *gd_total = gd + geq;
    *cd_total = cd + *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->DIOcapCurrent);

    return(OK);
}
```

## SECTION 7: THE GUMMEL-POON BJT MODEL IN C

Moving from a two-terminal diode to a three-terminal (or four-terminal, with substrate) Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) modeled via the integral-charge Gummel-Poon equations in `bjtload.c` illustrates how cross-coupled terminal physics expands into an intricate web of partial derivatives and dependent current sources . Unlike the simple Ebers-Moll model, the Gummel-Poon formulation incorporates high-level injection (the Webster effect), base-width modulation (the Early effect via forward and reverse Early voltages $V_{AF}$ and $V_{AR}$), and low-current base-emitter space-charge recombination via the normalized majority base charge factor $q_b$:

$q_1 = \frac{1}{1 - \dfrac{V_{BC}}{V_{AR}} - \dfrac{V_{BE}}{V_{AF}}}$

$q_2 = \frac{I_{SE\text{-fwd}}}{I_{KF}} + \frac{I_{SC\text{-rev}}}{I_{KR}}$

$q_b = \frac{q_1}{2} \left( 1 + \sqrt{1 + 4 q_2} \right)$

$I_{CC} = \frac{I_S}{q_b} \left[ \exp\left(\frac{V_{BE}}{N_F V_t}\right) - \exp\left(\frac{V_{BC}}{N_R V_t}\right) \right]$

Because $q_b$ is simultaneously a nonlinear function of both the intrinsic base-emitter voltage $V_{BE} = V_{B'} - V_{E'}$ and the intrinsic base-collector voltage $V_{BC} = V_{B'} - V_{C'}$, the collector transport current $I_{CC}$ depends non-locally on two distinct junction voltages . Furthermore, the intrinsic base current $I_B$ combines four distinct diode components—ideal forward diffusion $I_{BE}/\beta_F$, non-ideal base-emitter space-charge recombination $I_{BEN}$, ideal reverse diffusion $I_{BC}/\beta_R$, and non-ideal base-collector recombination $I_{BCN}$—while the extrinsic base resistance $R_B$ varies dynamically with base current crowding according to $R_{BM} + (R_B - R_{BM})/q_b$.

When `BJTload` linearizes the intrinsic three-terminal BJT core for the Newton-Raphson solver, a single conductance $g_d$ no longer suffices. Instead, the C code must apply the multivariable chain rule to compute a coupled matrix of conductances and transconductances with respect to $V_{BE}$ and $V_{BC}$. The base-emitter input conductance $g_\pi$ captures the sensitivity of total base current to $V_{BE}$, while the base-collector feedback conductance $g_\mu$ captures its sensitivity to $V_{BC}$. Simultaneously, differentiating the collector transport current $I_{CC}$ with respect to $V_{BE}$ and $V_{BC}$ through the quotient rule on $1/q_b(V_{BE}, V_{BC})$ yields the forward transconductance $g_m$ and the collector-emitter output conductance $g_o$:

$g_\pi = \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial V_{BE}} = \frac{1}{\beta_F} \frac{\partial I_{BF}}{\partial V_{BE}} + \frac{\partial I_{LE}}{\partial V_{BE}}, \qquad g_\mu = \frac{\partial I_B}{\partial V_{BC}} = \frac{1}{\beta_R} \frac{\partial I_{BR}}{\partial V_{BC}} + \frac{\partial I_{LC}}{\partial V_{BC}}$

$g_m = \frac{\partial I_{CC}}{\partial V_{BE}} - g_o = \frac{1}{q_b} \frac{\partial I_{BF}}{\partial V_{BE}} - \frac{I_{CC}}{q_b} \frac{\partial q_b}{\partial V_{BE}} - g_o, \qquad g_o = -\frac{\partial I_{CC}}{\partial V_{BC}} = \frac{1}{q_b} \frac{\partial I_{BR}}{\partial V_{BC}} + \frac{I_{CC}}{q_b} \frac{\partial q_b}{\partial V_{BC}}$

| Linearized Jacobian Term | C Variable in `BJTload` | Governing Partial Derivative | Physical Mechanism Captured | MNA Matrix Stamp Symmetry |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Base-Emitter Conductance ($g_\pi$)** | `gpi` | $\partial I_B / \partial V_{BE}$ | Forward base diffusion and low-level emitter-base recombination | Symmetric across $(B', E')$ |
| **Base-Collector Conductance ($g_\mu$)** | `gmu` | $\partial I_B / \partial V_{BC}$ | Reverse base diffusion and collector-base leakage | Symmetric across $(B', C')$ |
| **Forward Transconductance ($g_m$)** | `gm` | $\partial I_{CC} / \partial V_{BE} - g_o$ | Collector current modulation by base-emitter bias and Webster high-injection roll-off | Asymmetric VCCS coupling $(C', E')$ to $(B', E')$ |
| **Output Conductance ($g_o$)** | `go` | $-\partial I_{CC} / \partial V_{BC}$ | Forward Early effect base-width modulation ($V_{AF}$) and reverse transport | Symmetric across $(C', E')$ with cross-coupling |
| **Modulated Base Conductance ($g_x$)** | `gx` | $\partial I_{B\text{-ext}} / \partial V_{BB'}$ | Dynamic base resistance crowding modulated by majority base charge $q_b$ | Coupled between external $B$ and internal $B'$ |

When transient charge storage ($Q_{BE}$, $Q_{BC}$, $Q_{BX}$, $Q_{CS}$) and excess phase-shift network parameters (`TF`, `XTF`, `VTF`, `ITF`, `PTF`) are included, $q_b$ also modulates the effective base transit time $\tau_F(V_{BE}, V_{BC})$, introducing capacitive transconductances (`geqcb`) that augment `gm` and `go`. Consequently, the intrinsic BJT stamps an asymmetric $3 \times 3$ Jacobian sub-matrix across the internal nodes $(C', B', E')$—expanding to $6 \times 6$ when including the external terminals $(C, B, E)$ via parasitic conductances $(g_c, g_x, g_e)$ and the substrate node $S$.

```c
/* Excerpt from BJTload (bjtload.c): Chain-rule linearization of Gummel-Poon BJT */
void evaluate_gummel_poon_core(
    BJTmodel *model, BJTinstance *here,
    double vbe, double vbc, double vt,
    double *cc_out, double *cb_out,
    double *gpi_out, double *gmu_out, double *gm_out, double *go_out, double *gx_out
)
{
    double csat, c2, c4, vte, vtc, vtn, vtr;
    double evbe, cbe, gbe, evben, cben, gben;
    double evbc, cbc, gbc, evbcn, cbcn, gbcn;
    double q1, dq1dvbe, dq1dvbc, q2, dq2dvbe, dq2dvbc;
    double sqarg, qb, dqbdve, dqbdvc;
    double cc, cb, gpi, gmu, gm, go, gx, arg, rbpr, rbpi;

    csat = here->BJTtSatCur * here->BJTarea;
    c2   = here->BJTtBEleakCur * here->BJTarea;
    c4   = here->BJTtBCleakCur * here->BJTarea;
    vte  = model->BJTemissionCoeffF * vt;
    vtc  = model->BJTemissionCoeffR * vt;
    vtn  = model->BJTleakBEemissionCoeff * vt;
    vtr  = model->BJTleakBCemissionCoeff * vt;

    /* 1. Evaluate 4 fundamental junction currents and their derivatives */
    evbe  = exp(vbe / vte);
    cbe   = csat * (evbe - 1.0);
    gbe   = csat * evbe / vte;

    evben = exp(vbe / vtn);
    cben  = c2 * (evben - 1.0);
    gben  = c2 * evben / vtn;

    evbc  = exp(vbc / vtc);
    cbc   = csat * (evbc - 1.0);
    gbc   = csat * evbc / vtc;

    evbcn = exp(vbc / vtr);
    cbcn  = c4 * (evbcn - 1.0);
    gbcn  = c4 * evbcn / vtr;

    /* 2. Evaluate Early-effect factor q1 and high-injection factor q2 */
    q1      = 1.0 / (1.0 - model->BJTinvEarlyVoltF * vbc - model->BJTinvEarlyVoltR * vbe);
    dq1dvbe = q1 * q1 * model->BJTinvEarlyVoltR;
    dq1dvbc = q1 * q1 * model->BJTinvEarlyVoltF;

    q2      = cbe * model->BJTinvRollOffF + cbc * model->BJTinvRollOffR;
    dq2dvbe = gbe * model->BJTinvRollOffF;
    dq2dvbc = gbc * model->BJTinvRollOffR;

    /* 3. Compute normalized majority base charge qb and partial derivatives */
    sqarg  = sqrt(1.0 + 4.0 * q2);
    qb     = 0.5 * q1 * (1.0 + sqarg);
    dqbdve = 0.5 * (dq1dvbe * (1.0 + sqarg) + q1 * (2.0 * dq2dvbe / sqarg));
    dqbdvc = 0.5 * (dq1dvbc * (1.0 + sqarg) + q1 * (2.0 * dq2dvbc / sqarg));

    /* 4. Form collector transport current (cc) and base current (cb) */
    cc = (cbe - cbc) / qb - cbc / here->BJTtBetaR - cbcn;
    cb = cbe / here->BJTtBetaF + cben + cbc / here->BJTtBetaR + cbcn;

    /* 5. Derive linearized Jacobian conductances and transconductances */
    gpi = gbe / here->BJTtBetaF + gben;
    gmu = gbc / here->BJTtBetaR + gbcn;
    go  = (gbc + (cbe - cbc) * dqbdvc / qb) / qb;
    gm  = (gbe - (cbe - cbc) * dqbdve / qb) / qb - go;

    /* 6. Compute base-charge-modulated base conductance gx */
    rbpr = model->BJTminBaseResist / here->BJTarea;
    rbpi = model->BJTbaseResist / here->BJTarea - rbpr;
    gx   = 1.0 / (rbpr + rbpi / qb);

    *cc_out = cc; *cb_out = cb;
    *gpi_out = gpi; *gmu_out = gmu; *gm_out = gm; *go_out = go; *gx_out = gx;
}
```

Once `gpi`, `gmu`, `gm`, `go`, and `gx` are computed, `BJTload` stamps $22$ distinct pointer entries into the MNA matrix for each BJT instance. Because `gm` represents a Voltage-Controlled Current Source (VCCS) where collector current depends on base-emitter voltage, it stamps off-diagonally without symmetry ($\mathbf{Y}[C', B'] \neq \mathbf{Y}[B', C']$), breaking the structural symmetry of the MNA matrix and requiring an asymmetric sparse LU solver .

## SECTION 8: HANDLING TEMPERATURE DEPENDENCE

Semiconductor device physics is acutely sensitive to lattice temperature $T$ . If every call to `DEVload` had to recalculate the silicon bandgap energy $E_g(T)$, carrier mobility $\mu(T)$, intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i(T)$, temperature-scaled saturation current $I_s(T)$, built-in junction potential $V_J(T)$, and critical limiting voltage $V_{\text{crit}}(T)$ from scratch, the computational overhead of the additional `exp()`, `log()`, and `pow()` calls would double the runtime of every Newton-Raphson iteration .

To prevent this redundant computation, the SPICE architecture isolates all temperature-scaling algebra into a dedicated subroutine, `DEVtemperature` (such as `DIOtemp` in `diotemp.c` or `BJTtemp` in `bjttemp.c`) . This routine is invoked once during initial circuit setup prior to the DC operating point analysis, and is dynamically re-invoked only when the user sweeps or steps the circuit temperature via `.TEMP`, `.STEP TEMP`, or an `.ALTER` block . Inside `DEVtemp`, the C code reads the nominal parameter extraction temperature $T_{\text{nom}}$ (`ckt->CKTnomTemp`, specified in `.OPTION TNOM`) and the operating instance temperature $T$ (`here->DIOtemp`, which includes local self-heating or `.OPTION DTEMP` offsets), converting Celsius to Kelvin using `P_CELSIUS0 = 273.15` .

The fundamental physical drivers of temperature scaling in silicon are the narrowing of the energy bandgap $E_g(T)$ due to thermal lattice expansion and the degradation of carrier mobility $\mu(T)$ due to acoustic phonon scattering . In SPICE and HSPICE, the temperature dependence of the silicon bandgap energy is modeled by the empirical Varshni equation, while effective carrier mobility (`XMU`) and parasitic resistances scale according to power-law and polynomial thermal coefficients (`CTE`, `TC1`, `TC2`) :

$E_g(T) = E_g(0) - \frac{\alpha T^2}{T + \beta}, \qquad \mu(T) = \mu(T_{\text{nom}}) \left( \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}} \right)^{-\text{XMU}}$

where for silicon, $E_g(0) = 1.16\text{ eV}$ (or $1.17\text{ eV}$ depending on the HSPICE `LEVEL` selector), $\alpha = 7.02 \times 10^{-4}\text{ eV/K}$, $\beta = 1108\text{ K}$, and the mobility temperature exponent $\text{XMU} \approx 1.5$ . Using the temperature ratio $r_T = \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}}$ and thermal voltage $V_t(T) = \frac{k_B T}{q}$, the saturation current $I_s(T)$ scales exponentially with the bandgap energy activation exponent `XTI` ($p_t$, typically $3.0$ for $pn$ junctions and $2.0$ for Schottky barrier diodes) and ideality factor $N$:

$I_s(T) = I_s(T_{\text{nom}}) \cdot \left( \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}} \right)^{\frac{\text{XTI}}{N}} \exp \left[ \frac{q E_g(T_{\text{nom}})}{N k_B T}\left( \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}} - 1 \right) \right]$

Simultaneously, the built-in junction potential $V_J(T)$ decreases almost linearly with rising temperature as the Fermi levels shift toward the intrinsic mid-gap, which in turn causes the zero-bias junction capacitance $C_{J0}(T)$ to increase:

$V_J(T) = \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}} V_J(T_{\text{nom}}) - 3 V_t(T) \ln\left(\frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}}\right) - \left[ \frac{T}{T_{\text{nom}}} E_g(T_{\text{nom}}) - E_g(T) \right]$

$C_{J0}(T) = C_{J0}(T_{\text{nom}}) \cdot \left[ 1 + M \left( 4.0 \times 10^{-4} (T - T_{\text{nom}}) - \frac{V_J(T) - V_J(T_{\text{nom}})}{V_J(T_{\text{nom}})} \right) \right]$

All of these pre-computed, temperature-adjusted parameters are written directly into dedicated `DIOt...` fields inside the `DIOinstance` structure (`here->DIOtSatCur`, `here->DIOtJctPot`, `here->DIOtJctCap`, `here->DIOtConductance`, and `here->DIOtVcrit`), allowing `DIOload` to read them as simple scalar constants during the Newton-Raphson loop .

```c
/* Authentic C-code implementation of DIOtemp (diotemp.c) for .STEP TEMP updates */
#include <math.h>
#include "spice.h"
#include "const.h"
#include "cktdefs.h"
#include "diodefs.h"

int DIOtemp(GENmodel *inModel, CKTcircuit *ckt)
{
    DIOmodel *model = (DIOmodel *)inModel;
    DIOinstance *here;
    double vt, vtnom, egfet, egfet1, arg, arg1, fact1, fact2, pbfact, pbfact1;
    double gmanew, gmaold, xfc, dt, xmu_factor;

    /* Outer loop over all Diode .MODEL definitions */
    for ( ; model != NULL; model = model->DIOnextModel ) {
        
        /* Nominal reference temperature evaluations (Tnom) */
        vtnom   = CONSTKoverQ * model->DIOtnom;
        fact1   = model->DIOtnom / REFTEMP;
        egfet1  = 1.16 - (7.02e-4 * model->DIOtnom * model->DIOtnom) /
                         (model->DIOtnom + 1108.0);
        arg1    = -egfet1 / (2.0 * CONSTboltz * model->DIOtnom) +
                  1.1150877 / (CONSTboltz * (REFTEMP + REFTEMP));
        pbfact1 = -2.0 * vtnom * (1.5 * log(fact1) + CHARGE * arg1);

        /* Pre-compute depletion capacitance extrapolation factors F2 and F3 */
        xfc = log(1.0 - model->DIOdepletionCapCoeff);
        model->DIOtF2 = exp((1.0 + model->DIOgradingCoeff) * xfc);
        model->DIOtF3 = 1.0 - model->DIOdepletionCapCoeff * (1.0 + model->DIOgradingCoeff);

        /* Inner loop over all Diode instances */
        for ( here = model->DIOinstances; here != NULL; here = here->DIOnextInstance ) {
            if (!here->DIOtempGiven) {
                here->DIOtemp = ckt->CKTtemp + here->DIOdtemp;
            }
            dt    = here->DIOtemp - model->DIOtnom;
            vt    = CONSTKoverQ * here->DIOtemp;
            fact2 = here->DIOtemp / REFTEMP;

            /* 1. Varshni Bandgap Energy Eg(T) at operating instance temperature */
            egfet = 1.16 - (7.02e-4 * here->DIOtemp * here->DIOtemp) /
                           (here->DIOtemp + 1108.0);
            arg   = -egfet / (2.0 * CONSTboltz * here->DIOtemp) +
                    1.1150877 / (CONSTboltz * (REFTEMP + REFTEMP));
            pbfact = -2.0 * vt * (1.5 * log(fact2) + CHARGE * arg);

            /* 2. Scale Saturation Current Is(T) */
            here->DIOtSatCur = model->DIOsatCur * exp(
                ((here->DIOtemp / model->DIOtnom) - 1.0) *
                model->DIOactivationEnergy / (model->DIOemissionCoeff * vt) +
                model->DIOsaturationCurrentExp / model->DIOemissionCoeff *
                log(here->DIOtemp / model->DIOtnom)
            );

            /* 3. Scale Built-in Junction Potential VJ(T) and Capacitance CJO(T) */
            gmaold = (model->DIOjunctionPot - pbfact1) / fact1;
            here->DIOtJctPot = pbfact + fact2 * gmaold;
            gmanew = (here->DIOtJctPot - pbfact) / fact2;

            here->DIOtJctCap = model->DIOjunctionCap * (
                1.0 + model->DIOgradingCoeff *
                (4.0e-4 * (here->DIOtemp - REFTEMP) - gmanew + gmaold)
            );

            /* 4. Scale Carrier Mobility & Series Conductance Gspr(T) via TC1, TC2 */
            xmu_factor = 1.0 + model->DIOtempCoeff1 * dt + model->DIOtempCoeff2 * dt * dt;
            here->DIOtConductance = model->DIOconductance / xmu_factor;

            /* 5. Cache Forward-Bias Depletion Thresholds (F1, FC*VJ) and Vcrit(T) */
            here->DIOtDepCap = model->DIOdepletionCapCoeff * here->DIOtJctPot;
            here->DIOtF1 = here->DIOtJctPot * (1.0 - exp((1.0 - model->DIOgradingCoeff) * xfc)) /
                           (1.0 - model->DIOgradingCoeff);

            here->DIOtVcrit = model->DIOemissionCoeff * vt * log(
                model->DIOemissionCoeff * vt / (CONSTroot2 * here->DIOtSatCur)
            );
        }
    }
    return(OK);
}
```

## SECTION 9: BYPASSING EVALUATION FOR LATENT DEVICES

In any large-scale digital, mixed-signal, or memory integrated circuit, temporally localized switching is the norm: at any given picosecond of transient simulation, fewer than 5% to 15% of the transistors are actively transitioning between logic states, while the remaining 85% to 95% sit quiescently in static cutoff or saturation, exhibiting temporal latency . If `DEVload` blindly executes the full Gummel-Poon or BSIM physics equations for every single latent transistor on every Newton-Raphson iteration, up to 90% of all device evaluation CPU cycles are wasted recalculating the exact same currents, charges, and conductances that were already computed on iteration $k-1$ .

To exploit temporal latency without sacrificing full MNA matrix integrity, SPICE3 and HSPICE implement an algorithmic optimization inside `DEVload` known as Model Evaluation Bypass, governed by the netlist control options `.OPTION BYPASS`, `.OPTION MBYPASS`, and `.OPTION BYTOL` . Before evaluating any exponential or power-law physics for a device instance on iteration $k > 1$, `DEVload` inspects the trial terminal voltages extracted from `ckt->CKTrhsOld` and computes the voltage differences ($\Delta V_d = V_d^{(k)} - V_d^{(k-1)}$) relative to the values stored in `ckt->CKTstate0` from the previous iteration .

For an instance to qualify as latent and trigger the `bypass` jump, every controlling branch voltage $V_j$ and linearly predicted branch current $\hat{I}_j = I_j^{(k-1)} + g_j^{(k-1)} \Delta V_j$ must satisfy the strict two-part relative-plus-absolute tolerance inequalities :

$\left| V_j^{(k)} - V_j^{(k-1)} \right| < \text{BYTOL}_V = \text{MBYPASS} \cdot \left( \text{RELTOL} \cdot \max\left(\left|V_j^{(k)}\right|, \left|V_j^{(k-1)}\right|\right) + \text{VNTOL} \right)$

$\left| \hat{I}_j^{(k)} - I_j^{(k-1)} \right| < \text{BYTOL}_I = \text{MBYPASS} \cdot \left( \text{RELTOL} \cdot \max\left(\left|\hat{I}_j^{(k)}\right|, \left|I_j^{(k-1)}\right|\right) + \text{ABSTOL} \right)$

| HSPICE Control Option | Internal Engine Variable | Default Value | Algorithmic Role in Device Evaluation & Latency Bypass |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **`.OPTION BYPASS`** | `ckt->CKTbypass` | `1` (or `2` for advanced BSIM models) | Master enable/disable switch for latent device bypass (`BYPASS=0` forces full physics evaluation on every iteration)  |
| **`.OPTION MBYPASS`** | `ckt->CKTmbypass` | `1.0` (standard) / tighter in `ACCURATE` | Multiplier scaling the default bypass voltage tolerance `BYTOL` relative to convergence tolerances  |
| **`.OPTION BYTOL`** | `ckt->CKTbytol` | Derived from `VNTOL * MBYPASS` | Absolute voltage change ceiling below which a device terminal is considered latent  |
| **`.OPTION RELTOL` / `RELV`** | `ckt->CKTreltol` | `1.0e-3` ($0.1\%$) | Relative voltage and current convergence tolerance applied to both NR termination and bypass checks  |
| **`.OPTION ABSV` / `VNTOL`** | `ckt->CKTvoltTol` | `5.0e-5 V` ($50\text{ }\mu\text{V}$) | Absolute minimum voltage floor preventing division-by-zero tolerances near zero-crossing nodes  |
| **`.OPTION CHGTOL` / `RELQ`** | `ckt->CKTchgTol` | `1.0e-14 C` / `0.01` | Charge conservation tolerance checked during dynamic capacitive integration before allowing transient bypass  |

When all branch voltage and current deltas fall strictly beneath these thresholds, the C code executes a `goto load;` (or `goto done;`) jump statement that skips `DEVpnjlim`, skips the Shockley or Gummel-Poon DC equations, skips the nonlinear depletion capacitance power laws, and skips `NIintegrate` . Instead, it retrieves the cached `vd`, `cd`, and `gd` directly from `*(ckt->CKTstate0 + ...)` and proceeds straight to stamping them into the MNA matrix and RHS vector .

The bypassed device must still stamp its cached values into the MNA matrix because at the start of every Newton-Raphson iteration (`NIiter`), the core solver zeroes out the entire sparse MNA matrix (`SMPclear(ckt->CKTmatrix)`) and RHS vector before calling `DEVload` . Therefore, skipping the matrix stamp would erase the device from the circuit topology; `BYPASS` skips the expensive physics evaluation while still stamping the cached linear companion model in a few nanoseconds .

```c
/* Authentic C-code implementation of the Latency Bypass check inside BJTload */
            /* Extract raw terminal voltage deltas from previous NR iteration */
            vbe = model->BJTtype * (*(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->BJTbasePrimeNode) -
                                    *(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->BJTemitPrimeNode));
            vbc = model->BJTtype * (*(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->BJTbasePrimeNode) -
                                    *(ckt->CKTrhsOld + here->BJTcolPrimeNode));

            delvbe = vbe - *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbe);
            delvbc = vbc - *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbc);

            /* Predict new currents linearly using cached Jacobians from state0 */
            cchat = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcc) +
                    (*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgm) + *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgo)) * delvbe -
                    (*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgo) + *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgmu)) * delvbc;

            cbhat = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcb) +
                    *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgpi) * delvbe +
                    *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgmu) * delvbc;

            /* LATENCY BYPASS CHECK: Skip evaluation if voltages & currents are quiescent */
            if ((!(ckt->CKTmode & (MODEINITFIX | MODEINITJCT | MODEINITPRED))) &&
                (ckt->CKTbypass)) 
            {
                /* 1. Check Base-Emitter voltage change against BYTOL / RELTOL */
                if (fabs(delvbe) < (ckt->CKTreltol * MAX(fabs(vbe),
                    fabs(*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbe))) + ckt->CKTvoltTol)) 
                {
                    /* 2. Check Base-Collector voltage change */
                    if (fabs(delvbc) < (ckt->CKTreltol * MAX(fabs(vbc),
                        fabs(*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbc))) + ckt->CKTvoltTol)) 
                    {
                        /* 3. Check linearly predicted Collector Current change */
                        if (fabs(cchat - *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcc)) <
                            (ckt->CKTreltol * MAX(fabs(cchat),
                            fabs(*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcc))) + ckt->CKTabstol)) 
                        {
                            /* 4. Check linearly predicted Base Current change */
                            if (fabs(cbhat - *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcb)) <
                                (ckt->CKTreltol * MAX(fabs(cbhat),
                                fabs(*(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcb))) + ckt->CKTabstol)) 
                            {
                                /* Device is LATENT! Restore cached state0 variables */
                                vbe = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbe);
                                vbc = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTvbc);
                                cc  = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcc);
                                cb  = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTcb);
                                gpi = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgpi);
                                gmu = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgmu);
                                gm  = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgm);
                                go  = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgo);
                                gx  = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgx);
                                geqcb = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTgeqcb);
                                ceqbe = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTceqbe);
                                ceqbc = *(ckt->CKTstate0 + here->BJTceqbc);

                                /* Jump directly over physics & integration to MNA stamping! */
                                goto load;
                            }
                        }
                    }
                }
            }
```

While `.OPTION BYPASS=1` provides a substantial $2\times$ to $3\times$ speedup in digital-heavy transient runs, seasoned analog designers must understand its numerical trade-off . Because a bypassed transistor freezes its Jacobian derivatives at the values from a slightly different voltage $V^{(k-1)}$, the global Jacobian matrix ceases to be the exact Fréchet derivative of the residual vector. In high-precision analog blocks—such as sigma-delta modulators, low-phase-noise LC oscillators, or sub-millivolt bandgap references—this slight mismatch between successive bypassed steps can inject artificial numerical noise ("bypass chatter") or cause premature timestep rejection . Consequently, invoking `.OPTION ACCURATE` in HSPICE tightens `MBYPASS` and `BYTOL` or disables model bypass altogether (`BYPASS=0`), forcing full C-code evaluation on every iteration to guarantee golden-reference signoff fidelity .

## SECTION 10: CONCLUSION AND TRANSITION

The journey from Maxwell's equations and quantum statistics to a compiled binary executing `DIOload` and `BJTload` reveals the extraordinary engineering discipline embedded within the SPICE source code . Every physical equation entered into a compact model must satisfy three uncompromising numerical requirements inside the C engine . First, every branch current $I(\mathbf{V})$ and charge $Q(\mathbf{V})$ must maintain strict $C^1$ analytical continuity—possessing smooth, analytically derived first partial derivatives ($g_m, g_{ds}, C_{ij}$) across all operating regimes—so that the Newton-Raphson Jacobian matrix never encounters a step discontinuity in slope . Second, unbounded exponential and power-law singularities must be conditioned by predictor-corrector damping (`DEVpnjlim`, `DEVfetlim`, `DEVlimvds`), tangent linearizations above `EXPMAX` (`limexp`) and $F_C V_J$, and diagonal regularization (`GMIN`) to prevent IEEE-754 floating-point exceptions and singular matrices . Third, computational cost must be amortized across the simulation lifecycle by hoisting invariant thermal physics out of the iterative loop into `DEVtemp`, pre-binding sparse matrix element pointers in `DEVsetup`, and short-circuiting temporally dormant components via latency `BYPASS` checks .

While the Shockley diode and Gummel-Poon BJT subroutines examined in this chapter require several hundred lines of carefully structured C code, they represent only the foundational era of compact modeling . As CMOS fabrication scaled below one micron into the deep-submicron, nanometer, and FinFET/Gate-All-Around (GAA) eras, basic regional equations collapsed under the weight of short-channel effects, velocity saturation, drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), quantum-mechanical gate tunneling, non-quasi-static (NQS) channel transit delays, and layout-dependent proximity effects . 

In Chapter 10 (Deep-Submicron MOSFET Modeling: BSIM3, BSIM4, and BSIM-CMG), this architectural foundation scales by two orders of magnitude . The single-page C functions of `dioload.c` give way to twenty-thousand-line C modules and automated Verilog-A / ADMS compiler pipelines where single-equation regional continuity is achieved through intricate mathematical smoothing functions, charge-conserving Ward-Dutton partitioning, and GPU-accelerated vector evaluation .
