# The Paradigm Shift in Semiconductor Device Modeling: Artificial Intelligence and Neural Network-Based Compact Models in 2026 and Beyond

## 1. Introduction to the Modeling Crisis and the Artificial Intelligence Imperative

The trajectory of the semiconductor industry has been defined by an unrelenting drive toward dimensional scaling and architectural complexity. Historically, technology computer-aided design (TCAD) methodology and physics-driven compact models have served as the foundational bedrock for circuit computer-aided design (CAD) and electronic design automation (EDA). From the inception of the Ebers-Moll model for bipolar transistors in 1954 to the Gummel-Poon charge-control relations introduced in 1970, and ultimately the highly sophisticated Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model (BSIM) and surface-potential-based PSP models, analytical equations have successfully bridged the gap between fundamental physics and the Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis (SPICE).

  

However, as the industry transitions deeply into the Angstrom era—deploying sub-3-nanometer and sub-2-nanometer nodes by 2026—the fundamental physics governing charge carrier transport have grown exponentially convoluted. The adoption of three-dimensional, highly confined transistor architectures, including Gate-All-Around (GAA) Nanosheet Field-Effect Transistors (NSFETs) and vertically stacked Complementary FETs (CFETs), introduces severe quantum mechanical confinement, non-quasi-static (NQS) phenomena, and extreme electrothermal self-heating effects. Traditional equation-based modeling frameworks face near-insurmountable challenges in balancing physical accuracy with acceptable development time and computational execution speed.

  

Modern industry-standard analytical compact models, such as BSIM-CMG (Common Multi-Gate), frequently mandate the precise calibration of over one thousand semi-empirical parameters to adequately reflect these complex structural variables and secondary physical effects. This massive parameter overhead severely degrades SPICE simulation speeds, complicates the parameter extraction process, and restricts fitting accuracy to exceptionally narrow design spaces. In the context of Design-Technology Co-Optimization (DTCO)—where the evaluation of Power, Performance, Area, and Cost (PPAC) metrics requires highly iterative feedback loops between manufacturing realities and circuit design—the computational bottleneck of traditional compact modeling has become a critical vulnerability.

  

To circumvent the inherent limitations of rigid mathematical approximations, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) into semiconductor compact modeling has transitioned from exploratory academic research to an urgent industrial mandate. Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have emerged as highly flexible, data-driven surrogate models capable of directly mapping process variables to electrical output characteristics without the overhead of complex, hand-derived analytical formulations. By 2026, the application of Machine Learning-Assisted Compact Modeling (MLCM) represents a fundamental paradigm shift, replacing pure analytical equations with physics-constrained neural architectures that rapidly accelerate DTCO methodologies while preserving the rigorous physical integrity required for advanced integrated circuit (IC) synthesis and verification.

  

## 2. The Evolution of Neural Compact Models: Transcending the Black Box

The initial implementation of data-driven device modeling heavily utilized basic Look-Up Tables (LUT) and unconstrained, shallow Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). While LUTs facilitate the rapid implementation of measured device data into circuit simulations, they are notoriously sensitive to experimental noise, inherently lack physical explainability, and possess virtually zero predictive capability beyond their immediate dataset. Early Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) neural networks offered superior scalability and mathematical smoothness compared to interpolation schemes, but their application to rigorous semiconductor modeling revealed critical functional vulnerabilities.

  

The primary deficiency of purely data-driven ANNs is their function as opaque black-box solvers. Because their training objectives rely exclusively on minimizing the mean squared error against massive datasets generated by computationally expensive TCAD runs, standard ANNs frequently fail to internalize the underlying semiconductor physics. Consequently, these models routinely exhibit unphysical behaviors that are catastrophic for sensitive analog and mixed-signal SPICE simulations. Such anomalies include the prediction of non-zero drain currents at zero bias conditions, abrupt mathematical discontinuities, and non-monotonic variations in capacitance-voltage behaviors. Furthermore, unconstrained ANNs suffer from severe generalization failures; they are notoriously poor at extrapolation and routinely produce wildly inaccurate predictions when operating even slightly outside their defined training boundaries.

  

### 2.1 The Integration of Physical Constraints

To resolve these non-physical behaviors and eliminate convergence failures in standard EDA tool flows, the industry has aggressively pivoted toward Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Physics-Constrained Neural Networks (PCNNs). These architectures are fundamentally designed to solve supervised learning tasks while strictly respecting the universal laws of physics, which are embedded directly into the neural architecture or enforced through custom loss functions representing nonlinear partial differential equations.

  

|**Architectural Paradigm**|**Key Characteristics**|**Advantages**|**Disadvantages**|
|---|---|---|---|
|Traditional Analytical|Equation-based, semi-empirical parameters (e.g., BSIM4, PSP)|Deep physical meaning, excellent SPICE convergence|Extremely slow to execute, complex parameter extraction|
|Look-Up Table (LUT)|Direct data mapping, interpolation arrays|Fast implementation of raw measured data|High noise sensitivity, zero extrapolation capability|
|Standard ANN (MLP)|Purely data-driven, black-box optimization|High fitting flexibility, fast forward execution|Prone to unphysical behaviors, requires massive data|
|PINN / PCNN|Neural structures embedded with PDE constraints and invariants|Certified monotonicity, excellent generalization, robust physics|High upfront complexity in network architecture design|

A highly illustrative application of PINN methodology involves the modeling of complex electrostatic potentials within an n-type silicon nanowire. In benchmark studies utilizing commercial TCAD solvers on a nanowire defined by a 4-nanometer radius, an 18-nanometer gate length, and a 1-nanometer oxide thickness, purely data-driven models require exhaustive domain expertise to train effectively. Conversely, a properly structured PINN utilizes a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture to predict spatial electron density profiles, feeding this output into a physics-informing linear regression module that strictly enforces the Poisson equation to derive the electrostatic potential.

  

By ramping the gate voltage from 0 to 0.75 volts and capturing 101 spatial snapshots across a 2,193-point mesh, the PINN enforces boundary conditions by continually comparing the predicted gate voltage against the actual applied voltage. Furthermore, a dedicated physics-informing module calculates carrier densities based on Fermi-Dirac statistics. This rigorous balancing of equations allows the PINN to deeply learn the physics of the device, yielding maximum errors of less than 0.3% for the electrostatic potential profile and less than 0.6% for the electron density profile on a logarithmic scale. Crucially, this mastery of the underlying physics allows the PINN to execute highly accurate out-of-training-range predictions, effectively modeling the complex transition from the depletion regime to the inversion regime based on physical invariants rather than simple statistical extrapolation.

  

### 2.2 Mathematical Certification of Monotonicity and Symmetry

Ensuring absolute monotonicity across specific input subsets is a non-negotiable requirement for addressing fairness, interpretability, and convergence stability in SPICE-compatible compact models. Innovative approaches have successfully certified the monotonicity of general piece-wise linear neural networks by formulating the training constraint as a mixed-integer linear programming problem. By applying heuristic monotonicity regularizations during the learning process and iteratively amplifying the regularization magnitude, the final learned model achieves mathematically guaranteed monotonicity regardless of arbitrary model structures.

  

The PCNN framework advances this concept by introducing a self-adaptive data preprocessing strategy that entirely eliminates the need for manual data manipulation. PCNNs utilize a highly specialized weight-generation neural architecture that embeds physics-inspired constraints to enforce both monotonicity and symmetry natively. These constraints are rigorously validated using the Gummel Symmetry Test—the foundational benchmark for assessing mathematical symmetry in compact models. In practice, PCNNs demonstrate perfect overlap between conditions such as a gate-source voltage of 0 volts and 0.15 volts.

  

During extensive circuit-level evaluations, PCNN methodologies have demonstrated a 3.2-fold reduction in Mean Relative Error (MRE) for modeling I-V and C-V characteristics across advanced devices when compared to standard ANN implementations. In dynamic circuit simulations, PCNNs have reduced the power-delay product error in 17-stage ring oscillators from an unacceptable 50% down to a highly precise 7%, while concurrently lowering common-source amplifier gain errors from 5.7% to a negligible 0.4%. Furthermore, by replacing convoluted analytical evaluations with optimized neural forward passes, these models routinely reduce transient simulation times by up to 70%, paving a scalable pathway for AI-assisted compact model integration.

  

## 3. High-Fidelity Data Optimization and Advanced Architectures

While PINNs and PCNNs resolve the physical validity crisis, the development of these models is heavily bottlenecked by the staggering computational cost of acquiring high-quality training data. Traditional ANN-based compact models require immense datasets to maintain accuracy across multi-dimensional input spaces encompassing process variables and intricate device geometries. Generating this volume of data via conventional TCAD software is frequently cost-prohibitive in industrial settings.

  

### 3.1 Multi-Fidelity Neural Networks and Active Learning

To systematically dismantle the data acquisition bottleneck, the industry has aggressively adopted Multi-Fidelity Neural Networks (MFNN) coupled with active learning-based sampling algorithms. The MFNN framework leverages transfer learning, initiating the modeling process by training a Low-Fidelity Neural Network (LFNN) on an extremely sparse dataset. The knowledge encoded within the LFNN provides a highly informed weight initialization for the subsequent High-Fidelity model, which results in faster and significantly deeper test loss decay compared to networks trained without prior fidelity mapping.

  

The integration of active learning fundamentally optimizes how the design space is explored. In studies targeting sub-3-nanometer NSFET process coverage, researchers define an initial design space of 1,000 potential device parameter combinations. Utilizing empirical formulas where the initial dataset size is strictly bound to ten times the input dimension (`$N_P \times 10$`), the algorithm requires an initial dataset of merely 80 devices simulated via TCAD. Instead of relying on randomized Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS), the active learning algorithm evaluates the model's predictive uncertainty and selectively targets the most informative parameter combinations for subsequent sequential simulation.

  

|**Sampling Methodology**|**Network Architecture**|**TCAD Simulation Requirement**|**Error Margin**|**Simulation Speedup**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Latin Hypercube (LHS)|Standard ANN|Extremely High|Moderate to High|Baseline|
|Active Learning|Standard ANN|High|Moderate|Baseline|
|Latin Hypercube (LHS)|Multi-Fidelity NN|Moderate|Low|2x|
|Active Learning|Multi-Fidelity NN|> 50% Reduction|< 1.5%|> 3x faster than ANN|

By sequentially adding targeted data points, the proposed MFNN active learning approach vastly outperforms standard ANN models and conventional LHS methodologies. This algorithmic efficiency reduces the total number of required TCAD simulations by more than half, drastically lowering computational resource consumption while maintaining a prediction error of less than 1.5% for both global and local electrical characteristic variations in advanced NSFETs. Furthermore, the efficiency of the training data allows for the construction of a much simpler neural network, reducing the overall size of the compact model by more than three times and accelerating subsequent SPICE simulations proportionally.

  

### 3.2 Knowledge-Based Artificial Neural Networks (KBANN)

An alternative trajectory for embedding physical rigor into AI models is the Knowledge-Based Artificial Neural Network (KBANN) paradigm, which strategically fuses deterministic analytical equations alongside neural layers. A prominent implementation is the Neural Network–Virtual Source (NN-VS) model, an end-to-end trainable hybrid that combines the exact physical integrity of the virtual source framework with the adaptive flexibility of deep learning.

  

Within the NN-VS architecture, foundational analytical constructs—such as the Grove-Frohman formulations for I-V characteristics and the Meyer expressions for C-V behaviors—are physically embedded within the neural network. The system dynamically enforces a shared temperature-dependent threshold voltage, embedding vital temperature effects seamlessly across both the learned ANN parameters and the deterministic analytical expressions. By embedding this prior knowledge directly into the input architecture—resulting in frameworks such as the Prior Knowledge Input with Difference ANN (PKID-ANN)—the model exhibits the fastest learning rate and the fastest runtime among KBANN variants. Crucially, the PKID-ANN framework natively guarantees zero current at absolute zero bias and matches the strict accuracy of rigorous TCAD solvers while entirely bypassing the painstaking manual parameter extraction required by conventional analytical virtual source models.

  

### 3.3 Symbolic Interpretability via Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN)

Despite the physical validity introduced by KBANNs and PINNs, the latent layers of the networks remain mathematically opaque to human engineers. This lack of interpretability prevents device physicists from extracting meaningful, actionable insights regarding novel transport phenomena. To breach this barrier, the introduction of the Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) to transistor modeling constitutes a profound methodological breakthrough.

  

Unlike traditional Multi-Layer Perceptrons that assign static non-linear activation functions to fixed nodes, KAN architectures place highly flexible, learnable activation functions directly along the network edges. When the KAN framework is augmented with symmetric Fourier features—creating the Fourier KAN (FKAN)—the network overcomes spectral bias and achieves unprecedented prediction accuracy. In rigorous benchmarking against TCAD-simulated FinFETs, an FKAN augmented with just eight Fourier features achieved phenomenal 3-sigma error margins of 0.15% for I-V characteristics, 0.33% for transconductance, and 4.14% for output conductance.

  

However, the most transformative capability of KAN is its mathematical transparency. KAN possesses the unique ability to derive highly accurate, human-readable symbolic formulas directly from the learned data patterns. By explicitly outputting the symbolic relationships governing phenomena like gate current, drain charge, and source charge, KAN effectively dismantles the black-box paradigm, providing engineers with robust, physically interpretable mathematical expressions that facilitate in-depth transistor analysis, optimization, and scaling research.

  

## 4. The BSIM-NN Framework: Redefining Industry Standards

For decades, the Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model (BSIM) suite, developed by the University of California, Berkeley, has dominated the commercial EDA landscape. As multi-gate technologies scale into the extreme limits, UC Berkeley has actively spearheaded the development of the BSIM-NN (Berkeley Neural Network) framework. Officially positioned as a direct Machine Learning successor and enhancement to the industry-standard BSIM-CMG model, BSIM-NN is meticulously engineered to provide SPICE-compatible, hyper-accurate IC simulation at extraordinary speeds.

  

### 4.1 Latent Space Compression and Core Architecture

The foundational architecture of BSIM-NN diverges sharply from classical single-network designs by strategically segmenting the modeling task. The framework employs distinct neural pathways: one highly optimized neural network exclusively models the drain, source, and gate currents, while a completely separate neural network is dedicated to modeling the respective terminal charges.

  

A critical innovation enabling the extreme efficiency of BSIM-NN is its autoencoder-based latent space compression. The workflow initiates by utilizing a fully calibrated BSIM-CMG model to generate an exhaustive dataset of current-voltage characteristics across advanced FinFET geometries. This high-dimensional data is fed into an encoder comprised of two fully connected hidden layers, each featuring 50 neurons utilizing Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation functions. The encoder progressively compresses the physical data into a microscopic latent space bottleneck consisting of merely three neurons.

  

This compact latent space intrinsically encodes the most essential, fundamental factors of device variation. A mirrored decoder subsequently reconstructs the full I-V curves directly from these three variables, autonomously extracting critical device metrics including the precise threshold voltage, subthreshold slope, and peak transconductance. By applying specific, highly optimized transfer and loss functions to the decoder output, BSIM-NN enforces absolute mathematical smoothness, providing a data-driven compact model that rivals the accuracy of BSIM-CMG but operates with a dramatically reduced computational footprint.

  

### 4.2 Non-Quasi-Static (NQS) Modeling for High-Frequency Applications

In the domain of high-frequency AC and radio frequency (RF) circuit simulation, the fundamental Quasi-Static (QS) assumption—which posits that channel charges react instantaneously to terminal voltage variations—completely breaks down. To accurately capture these high-frequency behaviors, standard analytical models deploy elaborate Non-Quasi-Static (NQS) formulations that rely on extensive channel segmentation, severely inflating simulation times.

  

The BSIM-NN framework introduces a revolutionary charge deficit-based NQS model that perfectly integrates with its neural architecture. Instead of complex physical segmentation, the model mathematically calculates the deficient or surplus charge within the channel to dynamically reflect the NQS effect. The framework introduces mathematically rigorous, physics-based parasitic charges designed specifically to extract the intrinsic channel charges directly from the pre-trained QS NN models.

  

To overcome the inherent difficulties of training charge models, researchers implemented an improved, highly specialized loss function capable of obtaining perfectly physical charge values utilizing only capacitance training data. By routing these outputs through a dedicated charge deficit subcircuit to calculate the actual NQS currents, the model successfully captures precise high-frequency behavior without sacrificing neural network efficiency. Validated across complex transient, AC, S-parameter, and RF mixed-signal simulations, the BSIM-NN NQS integration demonstrates an astounding execution speedup of more than 14X compared to standard analytical compact models.

  

### 4.3 Advanced Electrothermal and Self-Heating Capabilities

Spatial confinement in modern 3D transistors exacerbates Self-Heating Effects (SHE), where locally generated thermal energy is unable to dissipate efficiently, leading to severe mobility degradation and performance loss. Conventional analytical compact models require the highly iterative, semi-empirical extraction of multiple temperature-dependent parameters to accurately capture SHE, a process that is both labor-intensive and prone to severe inaccuracies.

  

BSIM-NN circumvents this entirely by introducing an ANN-based electrothermal architecture that completely abolishes semi-empirical temperature equations. The model utilizes a novel temperature relaxation framework that permits the network to be trained exclusively on ambient temperature data, removing the necessity to iteratively extract dynamic self-heating parameters. The trained electrothermal model successfully captures intricate nanosheet geometry-dependent thermal behaviors, achieving a remarkable test accuracy of 98.5% for drain current prediction, 99.91% for direct thermal profile prediction, and 96.88% for thermal profile extrapolation across diverse supply voltages. This implementation allows IC designers to seamlessly sweep massive ranges of dynamic operating temperatures without the constant need for model recalibration, granting a 12X speed advantage over traditional BSIM-CMG thermal simulations.

  

### 4.4 Standard Cell Library Characterization Benchmarking

The ultimate validation for any novel compact model is its performance within the rigorous confines of commercial EDA standard cell library characterization. Standard cell characterization demands the execution of many thousands of SPICE simulations, encompassing complex Process-Voltage-Temperature (PVT) corners and local device mismatch Monte Carlo evaluations. Models that lack absolute mathematical stability will inevitably trigger catastrophic convergence failures during these massive procedural loops.

  

Rigorous testing of BSIM-NN utilizing a 3-nanometer nanosheet node established its unparalleled viability as a production-grade replacement for conventional models. During full-scale standard cell library characterizations, the neural network models demonstrated absolute consistency, achieving staggering sub-0.11% errors for logic delay evaluation and sub-0.76% errors for total power estimation across a vast spectrum of complex digital and analog circuits. Furthermore, because the neural models bypass the convoluted evaluation of thousands of analytical parameters, BSIM-NN completed the full standard cell library characterization up to 8 times faster than the conventional Verilog-A-based BSIM-CMG implementation, permanently solidifying its role as the vanguard of high-speed IC simulation.

  

## 5. Modeling Next-Generation Architectures: CFETs and Beyond

As the semiconductor industry pushes physical scaling beyond the capabilities of standard FinFET and planar technologies, vertically integrated three-dimensional architectures have become strictly necessary. Complementary Field-Effect Transistors (CFETs) represent the pinnacle of this evolution, achieving immense active area savings by vertically stacking a pFET directly on top of an nFET, enabling simplified terminal access via internal shorting of the p/n drain contacts.

  

### 5.1 The Extreme Dimensionality of CFET Geometries

CFET technologies are broadly categorized into sequential-CFET and monolithic-CFET architectures based on their fabrication sequence, with monolithic structures offering superior N/P gate integration and cost-efficiency per wafer. The structural classifications are further divided by their specific channel morphologies, encompassing fin-on-rectangular-fin (FORF), fin-on-trapezoidal-fin (FOTF), and fin-on-GAA-fin (FOGF) designs.

  

The electrical performance of CFETs is dictated by an overwhelming array of highly interactive physical parameters. These include the precise competencies of the dielectric and semiconductor materials, the exact vertical separation thickness between the N and P FETs, the underlap length, the specific fin height, the width of the fin, the inter-bridge width, and the inter-bridge height.

  

Analytical modeling of these variables reveals intense structural trade-offs. For instance, as the inter-bridge height increases, the ON-current for both N-Tree and P-Tree CFETs improves significantly due to the enlarged channel area, while the OFF-current remains stable, yielding an improved ON/OFF ratio. Conversely, modifying the inter-bridge width yields asymmetrical results: the ON-current in the N-Tree CFET initially rises before falling due to structural limitations, while the P-Tree CFET ON-current rises continuously but at a reduced rate. Furthermore, expanding the inter-bridge width directly decreases the ON/OFF ratio, signaling a decline in switching performance due to increased carrier density and leakage currents, while simultaneously exacerbating Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL) and Subthreshold Swing (SS) degradation due to reduced gate control at wider interfaces.

  

|**CFET Parameter**|**Impact on N-Tree Device**|**Impact on P-Tree Device**|**Overall Device Consequence**|
|---|---|---|---|
|Increased Inter-bridge Height|Higher ON-current, stable SS, increased intrinsic delay|Higher ON-current, decreasing SS, decreased intrinsic delay|Improved ON/OFF ratio, higher overall oxide capacitance|
|Increased Inter-bridge Width|ON-current rises then falls, higher leakage|ON-current rises continuously (reduced rate), higher leakage|Decreased ON/OFF ratio, higher DIBL, reduced gate control|
|Increased Fin Overlap|Higher parasitic capacitance|Higher parasitic capacitance|Slower global switching speed, higher thermal density|

### 5.2 Deep Neural Network Surrogate Models and Reliability

The sheer complexity of these highly correlated, 3D spatial variables renders traditional parameter extraction and equation formulation nearly impossible. To effectively navigate the CFET and next-generation nanosheet design space, researchers have deployed massive Deep Neural Network (DNN) surrogate models. To train these DNNs without incurring decades of computational debt, engineers generate physics-inspired synthetic datasets containing roughly 150,000 discrete samples, constrained entirely by first-order device relationships to reflect realistic forksheet and CFET behavior.

  

The surrogate DNN architecture is meticulously structured for hierarchical feature extraction. The network utilizes a feature vector comprising raw and engineered parameters: channel length, channel width, supply voltage, temperature, threshold voltage, normalized width/length, voltage overdrive, and logarithmic transformations. These inputs are processed through two initial hidden layers of 256 neurons utilizing Batch Normalization to prevent internal covariate shift and ReLU activation for high non-linearity. A dropout layer set to a 0.2 rate prevents overfitting, followed by successive compression layers of 128 and 64 neurons to form a dense latent representation. This sophisticated DNN surrogate achieves formidable predictive performance, yielding a coefficient of determination (`$R^2$`) of approximately 0.91, with highly minimized Mean Absolute Errors of approximately 0.024 and Root Mean Square Errors of approximately 0.031.

  

Furthermore, extending these models to assess long-term reliability against extrinsic variability sources—such as Metal Gate Work Function Fluctuation and Random Dopant Fluctuation—requires advanced mapping. Modern frameworks achieve this by losslessly converting unstructured 3D device meshes into attributed graphs, constructing a 45-dimensional device encoding scheme. This encoding scheme flawlessly integrates macroscopic electro-thermal stresses and localized interface trap distributions directly into the neural topology. By deploying a dual-end normalization strategy and a logarithmic-scale loss function, the model accurately predicts leakage currents across multiple orders of magnitude with a microscopic mean error of 1.27%, achieving an unprecedented acceleration of 17,000 times compared to traditional TCAD reliability assessments.

  

## 6. AI-Driven Parameter Extraction: Eradicating the Manual Tuning Bottleneck

Generating a robust model architecture, whether analytical or neural, is futile without the ability to rapidly and accurately fit that model to empirical silicon data. This process, known as parameter extraction, is historically the most laborious bottleneck in technology node development. Advanced analytical frameworks like BSIM4, BSIM-CMG, and ASM-HEMT are governed by dozens of highly correlated, mutually dependent parameters regulating mobility degradation, velocity saturation, and series resistance.

  

### 6.1 The Failure of Conventional Optimizers

Traditionally, device engineers relied on local gradient-based optimization algorithms, such as the Levenberg-Marquardt method. However, these traditional optimizers possess critical flaws when applied to multi-variate high-dimensional spaces. They require incredibly precise initial guesses; if the starting parameters are even slightly misaligned, the optimizer rapidly becomes trapped in local minima. Because parameters are highly correlated, adjusting one variable frequently destabilizes several others, resulting in lengthy, error-prone, sequential extraction flows that mandate constant manual intervention and iterative tuning.

  

While derivative-free Genetic Algorithms offer a broader search capacity and are less dependent on precise initial values, their performance across continuous convex variables is sluggish, and they struggle to achieve exact precision in high-dimensional domains.

  

### 6.2 The Era of ML-Based Automated Extraction

To obliterate these limitations, leading EDA vendors have aggressively integrated Machine Learning-based optimization solutions directly into their extraction platforms. A premier example is Keysight Technologies' ML Optimizer, fully integrated into the IC-CAP 2025 and MBP 2026 software suites.

  

The ML Optimizer operates on gradient-free optimization algorithms heavily augmented by adaptive learning routines. Unlike static algorithms, the ML Optimizer continuously learns from every extraction iteration, dynamically refining its internal strategy to perfectly balance broad exploration of the global parameter space with highly targeted exploitation of local minimums.

  

The impact on commercial modeling flows is profound. The ML Optimizer is capable of performing global optimization across high-dimensional spaces, routinely optimizing more than 40 complex parameters simultaneously in a single, automated step without requiring precise initial boundary conditions. During advanced Gallium Nitride (GaN) HEMT characterization utilizing the standard ASM-HEMT model, the ML Optimizer successfully optimized the complete DC module for a 4 x 50 micrometers device in under 6,000 automated trials, achieving a near-perfect drain current error margin of just 0.00125. Furthermore, as semiconductor foundries iterate their process specifications, the ML Optimizer can perform automatic re-centering of legacy BSIM4 models, instantly adapting thousands of parameters to the evolving specs with zero manual tweaking.

  

Concurrently, academia has pioneered deep-learning methodologies utilizing cascaded forward and inverse ANN architectures to extract physical parameters autonomously. Utilizing a floating normalization scheme that accommodates user-defined parameter ranges, these models absorb raw experimental gate capacitance vs. gate voltage and drain current vs. gate voltage measurements directly from 12-nanometer FinFETs. The inverse network seamlessly outputs the corresponding precise BSIM-CMG parameters, creating a fully autonomous, closed-loop extraction pipeline that entirely removes the human engineer from the optimization loop.

  

## 7. Industry Standardization, Verilog-A, and the Open Model Interface (OMI)

For artificial intelligence and neural compact models to be commercially viable, they must be universally standardized and natively supported by the entire EDA software ecosystem—ranging from Cadence Spectre and Synopsys HSPICE to Siemens Analog FastSPICE (AFS) and open-source simulators like ngspice.

  

### 7.1 The Compact Model Coalition (CMC) and VAMPyRE

The supreme governing body for device modeling standardization is the Silicon Integration Initiative’s (Si2) Compact Model Coalition (CMC). The CMC is a collaborative working group composed of the world's leading foundries, Integrated Device Manufacturers (IDMs), and EDA tool suppliers. Its primary function is to choose, evaluate, fund, and maintain industry-standard semiconductor device models to prevent the catastrophic fragmentation of proprietary formats. Standard models explicitly managed by the CMC encompass BSIM-BULK, BSIM-CMG, PSP, HiSIM, HICUM/L2, L-UTSOI, MVSG, and ASM-HEMT. As novel AI models such as BSIM-NN mature, they are subjected to rigorous four-phase standardization processes by CMC workgroups before being officially designated as industry standards.

  

The universal language utilized to code these standard models is Verilog-A, a specialized analog hardware description language. Historically, complex compact models were hard-coded in C and tightly bound to specific proprietary simulators, severely restricting portability. While Verilog-A decoupled the mathematical models from the simulator engine, poorly formulated code routinely causes fatal convergence errors, especially when integrating highly convoluted neural network mathematics.

  

To strictly enforce coding quality, the CMC introduced the Verilog-A Model Pythonic Rule Enforcer (VAMPyRE), a highly advanced standalone compact model parser and linter written in Python. VAMPyRE acts as an automated quality gate, meticulously screening massive Verilog-A neural implementations for problematic formulations such as hidden-state variables, unconstrained bias-dependent switch branches, floating unused parameters, and catastrophic mathematical violations like zero-by-zero or divide-by-zero cases. By automatically suggesting indentation and enforcing physical parameter limits, VAMPyRE ensures that computationally dense AI models maintain absolute mathematical robustness before they are released into commercial production environments.

  

### 7.2 The Open Model Interface (OMI) Architecture

Compiling vast Verilog-A neural models dynamically into natively executable code via circuit simulators incurs severe compilation time penalties that scale linearly with library complexity. To resolve this and facilitate the seamless integration of AI-enhanced extensions, the CMC released the Open Model Interface (OMI). Originating from the TSMC Model Interface (TMI2) and donated to the Si2 framework, OMI is a standardized C-language Application Programming Interface (API) that functions as a highly secure, simulator-agnostic middleware layer.

  

The OMI framework empowers circuit designers to extend, customize, and simulate major CMC standard models (including BSIM4, BSIM-CMG, and HiSIM2) without ever touching or modifying the native, compiled Verilog-A source code. This structural innovation enables two critical capabilities:

  

1. **IP Encapsulation and Security:** Semiconductor foundries frequently develop highly sensitive, proprietary ML-based modeling enhancements or aging parameter sets. Utilizing OMI, the foundry compiles these advanced algorithms into a unified OMI shared library. The foundry provides this file to the customer, completely protecting their internal modeling Intellectual Property (IP), while allowing the fabless designer to execute the model identically in any OMI-compliant SPICE simulator.
    
      
    
2. **Advanced Reliability and Aging Simulation:** Long-term device reliability is paramount for automotive and IoT applications. The OMI framework natively supports critical degradation mechanisms, including Hot Carrier Injection, Bias Temperature Instability featuring recovery effects, and Time Dependent Dielectric Breakdown. Foundries can map ML-derived degradation behaviors into the OMI shared library. Partners such as Siemens EDA and Fraunhofer IIS/EAS have heavily optimized the integration of these unified OMI aging models into the Analog FastSPICE (AFS) platform, permitting mutual customers to execute both one-step and gradual, highly accurate aging simulations across virtually any advanced process node.
    
      
    

## 8. Strategic Outlook and DTCO Acceleration (2026–2035)

The aggressive incorporation of Artificial Intelligence into device compact modeling is a foundational shift dictating the economic viability of future node scaling. Analysts project the global market for AI in semiconductor manufacturing to expand exponentially, surging from $60.9 billion in 2025 to $206.9 billion by 2035, reflecting the sheer critical necessity of algorithmic optimization in sub-nanometer fabrication.

  

The primary economic driver for MLCM adoption is the radical compression of the Design-Technology Co-Optimization (DTCO) loop. Historically, transitioning a speculative device architecture—such as a Forksheet FET or a 2D-material MoS2 memtransistor—into a fully characterized, SPICE-ready compact model required years of physical fabrication, tedious measurement, and manual parameter tuning. In 2026, utilizing frameworks like AutoPINN, active learning, and Keysight's ML Optimizer, highly accurate surrogate models are generated autonomously from sparse initial TCAD snapshots in mere weeks.

  

This extreme velocity allows foundries to deliver predictive, neural network-enhanced Process Design Kits (PDKs) to fabless designers concurrently with the physical pathfinding phase. Designers can optimize highly complex logic blocks, standard cell layouts, and backside power delivery networks utilizing predictive monolithic CFET models long before the first physical wafer is manufactured, effectively eliminating years from the time-to-market.

  

Simultaneously, the integration of Machine Learning directly into core EDA analysis engines—such as Cadence Spectre's ML-based FMC analysis for worst-case yield failure exploration—signals the impending dawn of fully Agentic EDA. In this imminent paradigm, autonomous AI agents will orchestrate the entire ASIC characterization pipeline, intelligently traversing multi-dimensional parameter spaces, synthesizing physical rules via Fourier KANs, and verifying long-term reliability through OMI layers with zero human intervention.

  

## 9. Conclusion

The transition from purely deterministic analytical formulations to AI-augmented and neural network-based compact modeling represents one of the most vital inflection points in the history of semiconductor engineering. Navigating past the fatal black-box limitations of early artificial neural networks, the industry has successfully deployed mathematically rigorous Physics-Informed Neural Networks, highly efficient Multi-Fidelity active learning algorithms, and symbolically interpretable Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks.

  

Flagship implementations like the BSIM-NN framework definitively prove that machine learning models can easily match the precision required for massive standard-cell library characterization, while concurrently providing order-of-magnitude computational speedups in electrothermal and Non-Quasi-Static RF simulations. Crucially, the robust standardization infrastructure erected by the Compact Model Coalition, enforced by the VAMPyRE Verilog-A linter and deployed globally via the simulator-agnostic Open Model Interface, ensures these phenomenally complex neural architectures execute securely and identically across the entire global EDA ecosystem. As transistor dimensions inevitably continue to scale vertically into CFET configurations and beyond, the seamless synergy of device physics and artificial intelligence remains the indispensable prerequisite for the continuation of Moore's Law.
