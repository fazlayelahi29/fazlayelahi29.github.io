# CHAPTER 11: THE PHYSICS OF 3D TRANSISTORS, FINFETS, AND THE BSIM-CMG STANDARD

## SECTION 1: THE END OF PLANAR CMOS

The continuous evolution of integrated circuit manufacturing has been historically governed by Dennard scaling, a paradigm that dictated the proportional shrinking of transistor dimensions to achieve higher density, faster switching speeds, and lower power consumption. However, as the physical gate length of the complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) transistor breached the 20-nanometer threshold, classical planar architectures encountered insurmountable quantum mechanical and electrostatic barriers. The semiconductor industry was physically forced to abandon the two-dimensional planar structure in favor of three-dimensional multi-gate architectures, primarily the Fin Field-Effect Transistor (FinFET). This architectural revolution fundamentally altered the governing physics of the device channel . 

Consequently, traditional threshold-voltage-based compact models, such as BSIM3 and BSIM4, became mathematically obsolete. To model these deeply scaled 3D structures, the Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model for Common Multi-Gate (BSIM-CMG) was developed by the BSIM Group at UC Berkeley to serve as the global industry standard . BSIM-CMG represents a foundational paradigm shift in compact modeling. It completely abandons the traditional charge-sheet approximation and hard threshold-voltage formulations in favor of an implicit, surface-potential-based mathematical framework . This chapter serves as a definitive exploration of the physics governing 3D transistors and details exactly how Synopsys HSPICE evaluates the BSIM-CMG standard. The analysis traverses the electrostatic limits of planar CMOS, the complex surface potential evaluations required for FinFETs, the quantum confinement of carriers in ultra-thin bodies, parasitic extractions in 3D environments, self-heating phenomena, and the eventual transition toward Gate-All-Around (GAA) nanosheets and Wide-Bandgap (WBG) devices.

To understand the absolute necessity of the FinFET and the BSIM-CMG model, one must first rigorously examine the physical collapse of the planar CMOS architecture at deep nanometer nodes. For several decades, the semiconductor industry relied on planar transistors where the gate electrode sat atop a flat silicon channel, separated by a thin silicon dioxide ($SiO_2$) dielectric. The primary function of the gate is to modulate the electrostatic potential barrier between the source and the drain, thereby controlling the flow of electrons in an n-type metal-oxide-semiconductor (NMOS) or holes in a p-type metal-oxide-semiconductor (PMOS) device. 

However, as the physical channel length ($L_g$) was aggressively scaled down to meet the geometric demands of Moore's Law, the electrostatic influence of the drain terminal began to catastrophically rival that of the gate. This phenomenon, categorized broadly as Short-Channel Effects (SCE), manifests most severely in planar devices because the electric field from the drain terminal penetrates deeply into the silicon substrate, far below the surface inversion layer where the gate exerts its strongest electrostatic control . The natural length ($\lambda$), which mathematically characterizes the electrostatic control of the gate over the channel, is expressed for a planar device as:

$\lambda = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{si}}{\epsilon_{ox}} t_{ox} X_{dep}}$

where $\epsilon_{si}$ and $\epsilon_{ox}$ are the relative permittivities of silicon and the gate oxide respectively, $t_{ox}$ is the physical thickness of the oxide, and $X_{dep}$ is the depletion width in the substrate. As $L_g$ approaches $2\lambda$, the device enters a regime of catastrophic electrostatic failure . 

In a deep-submicron planar transistor, the sub-surface leakage paths are located several nanometers below the silicon-oxide interface. Because the gate's electric field decays exponentially with depth into the bulk substrate, the gate cannot effectively modulate the potential barrier deep in the bulk silicon. Consequently, a highly positive drain voltage ($V_{ds}$) artificially lowers the potential barrier between the source and the drain, leading to massive Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL). Even when the gate voltage ($V_{gs}$) is nominally set to zero (the intended OFF state), the drain electric field essentially "pulls" carriers across the deep substrate, resulting in an unmanageable subthreshold leakage current ($I_{off}$) . 

Attempts to suppress this subsurface leakage in advanced planar nodes (such as 28nm and 22nm planar) relied heavily on ultra-steep halo doping implants to artificially narrow the depletion regions. However, this heavy body doping introduced severe, unintended physical consequences. First, it degraded carrier mobility ($\mu$) due to extreme Coulombic scattering from ionized impurities. Second, it caused massive threshold voltage ($V_{th}$) variability due to Random Dopant Fluctuation (RDF), where the statistical variation of just a few dopant atoms fundamentally altered the switching characteristics of the transistor . The planar CMOS architecture was physically trapped: it could not be scaled shorter without unacceptable leakage power, and it could not be doped heavier without catastrophic mobility loss and variability.

The physical solution was to abandon the bulk substrate entirely and extrude the silicon channel vertically into a highly confined "fin." By wrapping the gate electrode around three sides of this ultra-thin silicon fin, the gate regains absolute, three-dimensional electrostatic dominance. In a modern FinFET, there is no "deep substrate" for the carriers to leak through; the entire active volume of the silicon fin is in close physical proximity to the gate metal. The natural length for a double-gate or FinFET structure fundamentally changes, defined approximately by the boundary conditions of the new geometry:

$\lambda_{fin} = \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{si}}{2\epsilon_{ox}} t_{ox} t_{si}}$

where $t_{si}$ is the physical thickness of the silicon fin. As long as the fin thickness is maintained at roughly half the channel length ($T_{si} \approx L_g / 2$), the gate maintains perfect electrostatic control, DIBL is heavily suppressed, and the subthreshold swing ($SS$) approaches the ideal room-temperature thermodynamic limit of $60 \text{ mV/decade}$ . This profound geometric revolution required a completely new mathematical model for circuit simulation, leading directly to the obsolescence of BSIM4 for advanced logic and the rapid rise of BSIM-CMG.

## SECTION 2: INTRODUCTION TO BSIM-CMG (COMMON MULTI-GATE)

The Berkeley Short-channel IGFET Model for Common Multi-Gate (BSIM-CMG) is the global industry-standard compact model developed specifically to capture the highly complex three-dimensional physics of FinFETs and other common multi-gate structures . Unlike its predecessor, BSIM4, which was heavily parameterized and relied on regional mathematical smoothing functions to transition between weak inversion, moderate inversion, and strong inversion regimes, BSIM-CMG is a fully continuous, unified, surface-potential-based model. 

In Synopsys HSPICE, BSIM-CMG serves as the foundational computational engine for process nodes ranging from 14nm down to the 5nm boundaries. The architectural hierarchy of BSIM-CMG is conceptually divided into two distinct, rigorously defined layers: the core physical model and the real-device effects (RDE) sub-models . 

The core model mathematically describes a long-channel, ideal 3D transistor using the fundamental drift-diffusion transport equations coupled tightly with the Poisson equation. Unlike older bulk models that utilized the charge-sheet approximation—assuming that the inversion layer is an infinitely thin, two-dimensional sheet of charge located exactly at the oxide interface—BSIM-CMG natively calculates the volume inversion charge. Because a silicon fin is extremely thin (often sub-7nm in modern nodes), the electrostatic inversion layers generated by the opposing side-gates physically overlap. This means that carriers flow not just at the surface, but throughout the entire internal volume of the silicon fin, significantly boosting the drive current and altering the charge centroid . The core I-V drift-diffusion equation in BSIM-CMG is continuous and mathematically symmetric at $V_{ds} = 0$, explicitly integrating the charge density along the channel length without artificial regional boundaries:

$I_{ds} = \mu \frac{W_{eff}}{L_{eff}} \left( f(\psi_{s,s}) - f(\psi_{s,d}) \right)$

where $W_{eff}$ and $L_{eff}$ are the electrical effective width and length, $\mu$ is the highly-scaled carrier mobility, and $f(\psi_{s,s})$ and $f(\psi_{s,d})$ represent the surface potential functions evaluated precisely at the source and drain boundaries, respectively . 

The secondary layer of the BSIM-CMG architecture introduces the indispensable real-device effects (RDE). Because no manufactured FinFET is an ideal long-channel device, the core model is heavily modified dynamically during the HSPICE Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix evaluation. These real-device sub-models introduce complex physics such as velocity saturation, mobility degradation due to surface roughness and acoustic phonon scattering, quantum mechanical wave confinement, gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL), gate-induced source leakage (GISL), poly-depletion (in older nodes), and dynamic self-heating . 

When HSPICE parses a BSIM-CMG netlist, the simulator maps the geometric and electrical inputs into a compiled Verilog-A behavioral model. HSPICE uses advanced Newton-Raphson numerical solvers to iteratively determine the surface potentials at all internal nodes. Because the model relies on surface potentials rather than explicit threshold voltages, it achieves extraordinary accuracy in the moderate inversion regime. This continuity is a critical operational space for low-power mobile logic, sub-threshold SRAM, and high-gain analog mixed-signal design, ensuring that the transconductance ($g_m$) and its higher-order derivatives ($g_{m2}, g_{m3}$) remain mathematically smooth and free of non-physical discontinuities .

## SECTION 3: THE ELECTROSTATICS OF A FIN

The defining characteristic and computational heart of the BSIM-CMG core model is its total reliance on the exact mathematical solution of the 3D surface potential. In legacy planar SPICE models, the inversion charge $Q_{inv}$ was formulated as an explicit function of the terminal voltages. If the gate voltage exceeded an explicitly calculated $V_{th}$, the transistor turned on. In a deeply scaled FinFET, there is no mathematically rigid threshold voltage. Instead, the inversion charge is calculated dynamically using an implicit surface potential equation (SPE) derived from a rigorous solution of the 1D/2D Poisson equation mapped across the fin geometry .

To evaluate the electrostatics of a fin accurately, BSIM-CMG primarily considers an undoped or lightly doped body, which represents the standard foundry approach for FinFETs to prevent random dopant fluctuation. The exact implicit equation linking the applied terminal voltages to the surface potential $\psi_s$ and the inversion charge $Q_{inv}$ requires solving the boundary conditions of Gauss's Law across the oxide-silicon interface .

The fundamental relationship between the applied gate voltage ($V_g$), the flat-band voltage ($V_{fb}$), and the surface potential ($\psi_s$) is expressed mathematically as:

$V_g - V_{fb} - \Delta\Phi_{QM} = \psi_s + \frac{Q_{inv}(\psi_s) + Q_{bulk}(\psi_s)}{C_{ox}}$

where $\Delta\Phi_{QM}$ represents the quantum mechanical correction term for the subband energy shift, $C_{ox}$ is the oxide capacitance per unit area, and $Q_{bulk}$ is the depletion charge in the silicon body. Because the ultra-thin fin is fully depleted under all standard operating conditions, $Q_{bulk}$ is essentially a constant defined by the finite body doping concentration, $Q_{bulk} = -q N_{body} T_{si}$, derived using a rigorous perturbation approach to account for any residual ionized dopants . 

The calculation of the dynamic inversion charge, $Q_{inv}$, is derived from integrating the Fermi-Dirac carrier distribution within the potential well. The explicit functional form $f(\psi_s)$, which acts as the driving force for the core drift-diffusion current equation shown in Section 2, is analytically derived as:

$f(\psi_{s}) = \frac{Q_{inv}^2}{2 C_{ox}} + 2 \frac{kT}{q} Q_{inv} - \frac{kT}{q} Q_{bulk} \ln\left( \frac{Q_{inv} + Q_{bulk}}{Q_{bulk}} \right)$

where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant, $T$ is the local device temperature evaluated dynamically, and $q$ is the elementary charge ($1.60219 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$) . 

Because $\psi_s$ appears on both sides of these highly non-linear Poisson-derived equations, it cannot be isolated algebraically. This implicit nature strictly requires HSPICE to solve for $\psi_s$ using Newton-Raphson numerical iterations during every single simulation time-step. However, to ensure that the MNA matrix converges rapidly without excessive computational overhead or singular matrix failures, BSIM-CMG implements a highly accurate analytical approximation to serve as the initial guess for the Newton-Raphson solver . By formulating a continuous initial guess that bridges the subthreshold (weak inversion) and strong inversion regimes via mathematical smoothing functions—typically hyperbolic tangents and logarithmic expansions—HSPICE can converge on the exact surface potential with machine-level precision in merely two to three iterations .

Once $\psi_{s,source}$ and $\psi_{s,drain}$ are solved perfectly, all terminal node charges ($Q_g, Q_d, Q_s, Q_b$) are evaluated by integrating the charge density longitudinally along the channel length. The terminal transcapacitances ($C_{gd}, C_{gs}, C_{dd}, C_{gg}$, etc.) are then natively derived as the exact analytical partial derivatives of these charges with respect to the terminal voltages ($C_{ij} = \partial Q_i / \partial V_j$). This guarantees total charge conservation by construction, a strict requirement for avoiding fictitious energy generation in high-frequency RF and transient large-signal circuit simulations .

## SECTION 4: QUANTUM CONFINEMENT EFFECTS

As physical fin thicknesses ($T_{si}$) and fin widths scale aggressively below 10 nanometers, the classical continuous energy bands of bulk silicon break down completely, and the charge carriers must be treated as quantum mechanical waveforms. The structural dimensions of a modern FinFET are highly comparable to the de Broglie wavelength of an electron, meaning that the ultra-thin silicon fin acts as a one-dimensional or two-dimensional infinite potential well. BSIM-CMG natively incorporates Quantum Mechanical Effects (QME) into its core evaluation, fundamentally altering both the spatial charge distribution and the effective density of states (DOS) .

In a classical bulk transistor, the peak of the electron probability distribution—and thus the highest concentration of inversion charge—is assumed to lie exactly at the silicon-oxide interface. However, quantum mechanics dictates that the carrier wavefunction must go to absolute zero at the infinitely hard potential barrier of the gate oxide. Consequently, the probability density function $|\Psi|^2$ mathematically dictates that the peak of the inversion charge is physically pushed away from the interface and deeper into the spatial center of the silicon fin . 

This phenomenon, widely known as the charge centroid shift ($\Delta z$), behaves electrically as a parasitic quantum capacitance in series with the physical gate oxide capacitance. It effectively increases the equivalent oxide thickness (EOT) of the device, severely degrading the transconductance. HSPICE mathematically models this quantum degradation of the gate control using an effective width and effective capacitance model:

$EOT_{eff} = EOT_{phys} + \frac{\epsilon_{ox}}{\epsilon_{si}} \Delta z$

BSIM-CMG calculates the inversion charge centroid dynamically based on the bias-dependent surface potential and the physical geometry of the fin. Furthermore, the quantum confinement geometrically splits the continuous conduction band into discrete, quantized energy subbands. The lowest allowable energy state for an electron is no longer at the absolute bottom of the bulk conduction band ($E_c$), but is shifted upward by a significant energy quantum $\Delta E_0$ . 

This subband energy shift radically reduces the intrinsic carrier concentration and alters the effective Density of States ($N_c$). To accurately capture this behavior in SPICE, the model parameters dynamically modulate the density of states and the effective bandgap. The modified density of states in BSIM-CMG due to the 2D confinement is scaled geometrically as:

$N_C = N_{C0,SUB} \cdot \left( \frac{T_{eff}}{300.15} \right)^{3/2}$

where $N_{C0,SUB}$ is the base subband density of states and $T_{eff}$ accounts for the normalized thermodynamic scaling factor . Additionally, the upward shift in the ground state energy effectively widens the bandgap ($E_g$), resulting in an explicit increase in the absolute threshold voltage ($V_{th}$) of the device. This quantum-induced $V_{th}$ shift is modeled as an added potential barrier that the gate must physically overcome to generate the volume inversion layer. When a semiconductor foundry provides an HSPICE model card for an advanced 7nm or 5nm process, the parameters governing these quantum shifts are pre-calibrated to atomistic TCAD simulations and empirical silicon data, ensuring the analog circuit designer accurately sees the true, degraded transconductance ($g_m$) resulting from wavefunction confinement .

## SECTION 5: PARASITIC EXTRACTION IN 3D

While the core electrostatics of the FinFET provide vastly superior subthreshold characteristics compared to planar devices, the 3D topology introduces a monumental computational challenge regarding parasitic resistances and capacitances. In a planar transistor, the source and drain are vast, flat, highly conductive diffusion regions. In a FinFET, the source and drain are composed of the heavily doped, ultra-thin silicon fins themselves. Because the cross-sectional area of a single fin is incredibly small (e.g., 5nm wide by 40nm high), the sheet resistance is disastrously high .

To mitigate this extreme resistance, foundries utilize complex epitaxial growth to create Raised Source/Drain (RSD) structures, where lattice-mismatched silicon-germanium (for pFETs, adding compressive stress) or silicon-phosphide (for nFETs, adding tensile stress) is grown out of the fins, merging them into a diamond-shaped or faceted polygonal volume. While this reduces the macroscopic contact resistance, it introduces highly complex 3D current crowding and spreading resistance phenomena . BSIM-CMG includes a highly sophisticated, geometry-dependent parasitic resistance model that accounts for the non-linear current vectoring through the RSD regions into the narrow fin extension before reaching the actual channel . 

The source/drain series resistance ($R_{SD}$) in BSIM-CMG is evaluated dynamically. It includes the bias-independent spreading resistance from the RSD epitaxy, the contact resistance at the silicide interface, and a heavily bias-dependent accumulation resistance in the source/drain extension (SDE) region. As massive currents flow into the narrow fin under high electric fields, the velocity saturation of carriers inside the parasitic resistance itself is strictly modeled, causing dynamic current crowding and severe drive current roll-off .

Equally critical is the accurate modeling of the 3D parasitic fringe capacitance ($C_{fr}$). Because the gate electrode wraps around the fins and sits in highly precarious physical proximity to the elevated RSD epitaxy, a massive electrostatic coupling occurs through the spacer dielectrics. The outer fringe capacitance (between the gate sidewall and the RSD facet) and the inner fringe capacitance (between the gate and the source/drain extension) completely dominate the total gate capacitance ($C_{gg}$) in deeply scaled nodes, severely restricting switching speeds .

HSPICE evaluates $C_{fr}$ using heavily parameterized expressions that depend directly on the fin pitch, the spacer thickness, and the specific high-$\kappa$ dielectric composition. The model equations for fringe capacitance allow the foundry to calibrate the extraction via intrinsic geometric parameters:

$C_{fr} \propto \epsilon_{sp} \ln \left( 1 + \frac{H_{RSD}}{T_{sp}} \right)$

where $\epsilon_{sp}$ is the spacer permittivity, $H_{RSD}$ is the physical height of the raised source/drain epitaxy, and $T_{sp}$ is the spacer thickness. Because these 3D parasitic elements do not scale down linearly with the physical gate length (in fact, they often worsen as pitch shrinks), they constitute the primary bottleneck for the maximum switching frequency ($f_{max}$) of modern FinFET logic. Proper SPICE modeling of these 3D parasitics via BSIM-CMG is strictly required to predict the realistic RC delay, dynamic switching power consumption, and signal integrity of digital standard cells.

## SECTION 6: THE `NFIN` MULTIPLIER

The transition to 3D FinFET technology necessitated a radical, irreversible departure in how circuit designers size transistors in the HSPICE netlist. In planar CMOS architectures (evaluated by BSIM4), a designer possessed continuous freedom to dictate the channel width $W$ to explicitly fine-tune drive strength, analog current matching, or capacitance ratios. The designer could request a transistor width of 120nm, 121nm, or 122nm, and the lithography masks would print a flat, contiguous polygon to that exact specification. 

In the FinFET era, this continuous analog flexibility is dead. The height of the silicon fin ($H_{FIN}$) and the thickness of the fin ($T_{FIN}$) are strictly constrained by the foundry's highly specialized Self-Aligned Multi-Patterning (SAMP) or Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) epitaxy steps. A designer cannot alter the physical geometry of a single fin . Therefore, the traditional continuous parameter $W$ is mathematically obsolete.

In a BSIM-CMG HSPICE netlist, transistor sizing is achieved exclusively through the discrete, integer multiplier `NFIN` (Number of Fins) . To double the drive current, the designer cannot make a fin twice as wide; they must instantiate two identical fins in parallel (`NFIN=2`). Drive strength is fundamentally quantized.

BSIM-CMG translates the integer `NFIN` into an effective electrical width ($W_{eff}$) internally before evaluating the core surface potential and current transport equations. However, the calculation of $W_{eff}$ is governed explicitly by the structural topology of the fin, which is selected by the model parameter `GEOMOD` .

| `GEOMOD` Parameter | Physical Topology | Effective Width ($W_{eff}$) Formulation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `GEOMOD = 0` | Double Gate (No top conduction) | $W_{eff} = 2 \cdot H_{FIN}$ |
| `GEOMOD = 1` | Triple Gate (Standard FinFET) | $W_{eff} = 2 \cdot H_{FIN} + T_{FIN}$ |
| `GEOMOD = 2` | Quadruple Gate (Surrounding) | $W_{eff} = 2 \cdot H_{FIN} + 2 \cdot T_{FIN}$ |
| `GEOMOD = 5` | Gate-All-Around (GAA/Nanosheet) | Evaluates total enclosed sheet perimeter |

For a standard triple-gate FinFET (which represents the industry standard for 14nm to 5nm nodes, where `GEOMOD=1`), the high-$\kappa$ gate wraps over the two vertical sidewalls and the top horizontal surface. Therefore, the effective width for a single fin is the exact mathematical sum of the two sidewall heights and the fin top thickness . Furthermore, to account for real-world manufacturing deviations, BSIM-CMG supports trapezoidal fin modeling where $T_{FIN,TOP}$ differs from $T_{FIN,BASE}$, adjusting the perimeter calculation dynamically:

$W_{eff, single} = 2 \cdot \sqrt{H_{FIN}^2 + \left( \frac{T_{FIN,TOP} - T_{FIN,BASE}}{2} \right)^2} + T_{FIN,TOP}$

The total device effective width utilized in the drift-diffusion transport equation is then simply the multiplication of the individual fin width by the number of fins and the number of gate fingers:

$W_{eff, total} = W_{eff, single} \cdot NFIN \cdot NF$

where $NF$ is the number of gate fingers . HSPICE utilizes this exact $W_{eff, total}$ to dynamically scale all area-dependent parasitics, transconductance matrices, and intrinsic capacitances. 

Furthermore, `BULKMOD` is another critical netlist parameter tied closely to device topology. `BULKMOD=0` specifies that the FinFET is built on a Silicon-On-Insulator (SOI) substrate, isolating the fin completely. Conversely, `BULKMOD=1` indicates a bulk silicon substrate . For bulk FinFETs, the bottom of the silicon fin is directly tied to the substrate, creating a parasitic junction diode and unique sub-fin leakage paths that BSIM-CMG natively calculates by evaluating the isolation profile and the punch-through stopper doping implants .

## SECTION 7: SELF-HEATING EFFECTS (SHE)

One of the most severe physical penalties of the 3D FinFET architecture is the exacerbation of thermal confinement, referred to broadly as Self-Heating Effects (SHE). In legacy planar architectures, the silicon channel is contiguous with the massive bulk silicon substrate. Silicon is a highly efficient thermal conductor, allowing the immense heat generated by the lattice collisions of accelerated electrons to quickly dissipate down into the substrate and eventually out through the chip's heat sink.

A FinFET, conversely, represents an absolute thermal nightmare. The ultra-thin silicon fin protrudes vertically and is entirely encapsulated by the high-$\kappa$ gate dielectric, the work-function metals, and the inter-layer dielectric (ILD) oxides . Because silicon dioxide and high-$\kappa$ materials are exceptional thermal insulators, the heat generated inside the fin channel via Joule heating ($P = I_{ds} \cdot V_{ds}$) is effectively trapped. The local temperature of the fin can rise dynamically by tens of degrees Celsius over ambient on picosecond timescales .

Because temperature exponentially impacts carrier mobility (via increased acoustic and optical phonon scattering) and linearly degrades the threshold voltage, assuming a static ambient temperature across the die during an HSPICE simulation will lead to catastrophic predictive failures for high-current analog blocks and high-frequency digital clock trees. 

BSIM-CMG addresses SHE by embedding an auxiliary thermal network directly into the Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix alongside the electrical circuit. When the `SHMOD` (Self-Heating Model) switch is activated by the user or foundry, HSPICE generates an internal "temperature node" (denoted computationally as $T$) for each individual transistor . The local device temperature, $T_{dev}$, is evaluated dynamically based on an RC thermal equivalent circuit:

$T_{dev} = T_{nom} + V(r_{th})$

where $T_{nom}$ is the ambient nominal temperature (e.g., $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ or $125^{\circ}\text{C}$), and $V(r_{th})$ is the mathematical voltage evaluated at the internal thermal node, which is strictly analogous to the local temperature rise $\Delta T$. 

The physical heat dissipation is modeled as an injection of current into a parallel network of thermal resistances ($R_{th}$) and thermal capacitances ($C_{th}$). High-order thermal networks allow the model to accurately capture the multiple time constants of heat propagation: the immediate heat capacity of the silicon fin itself, followed by the slower thermal dissipation into the local routing interconnect layers, and finally the deep substrate . 

During a transient SPICE analysis, the instantaneous drain current generates a thermal power $P_{diss}$. This power is applied to the thermal RC network, updating $V(r_{th})$. The solver then feeds $T_{dev}$ directly back into the electrical core model, recalculating the degraded carrier mobility, adjusting the threshold voltage, and exponentially modulating the subthreshold leakage. This electro-thermal feedback loop is evaluated continuously via Newton-Raphson iterations until both the electrical currents and thermal nodes converge simultaneously .

## SECTION 8: GATE-ALL-AROUND (GAA-FET) AND NANOSHEETS

As the physical limits of FinFETs were reached at the 3nm technology node, the aspect ratio of the fins became too extreme to manufacture reliably without structural collapse, and the electrostatic control at the bottom of the fin (the sub-fin leakage path) began to irreversibly degrade. The evolutionary mandate for the 2nm era and beyond is the Gate-All-Around Field-Effect Transistor (GAA-FET), specifically implemented as vertically stacked nanosheets or forksheets .

In a nanosheet architecture, the traditional silicon fin is laterally sliced into discrete, suspended horizontal sheets. The high-$\kappa$ gate dielectric and the atomic-layer-deposited (ALD) work-function metals are deposited such that they completely wrap around 360 degrees of the silicon channel. This provides the ultimate theoretical electrostatic control, driving the natural length $\lambda$ to its absolute physical minimum and allowing extreme $L_g$ scaling without subthreshold leakage degradation .

To model these devices, Synopsys HSPICE leverages two approaches: the extension of BSIM-CMG via `GEOMOD=5` for common multi-gate nanosheets, and the deployment of a separate, specialized standard model known as BSIM-IMG (Independent Multi-Gate) for highly asymmetric structures .

When operating BSIM-CMG with `GEOMOD=5`, the mathematical geometry module natively shifts from computing fin perimeters to calculating the cross-sectional area and perimeter of stacked nanowires or nanosheets . The model relies on four core geometric parameters to define the electrostatics: the cross-sectional area of the sheet ($A_{ch}$), the electrical perimeter for carrier transport ($W_{eff}$), the channel doping ($N_{body}$), and the insulator capacitance per unit length ($C_{ins}$). For a stacked nanosheet with a rectangular cross-section of width $W_{sheet}$ and thickness $T_{sheet}$, the effective width becomes:

$W_{eff, single} = 2 \cdot (W_{sheet} + T_{sheet})$

The core surface potential equation in BSIM-CMG elegantly accommodates this geometry via quantum subband modeling optimized for deeply confined spaces. The confinement in a 5nm-thick nanosheet occurs strictly in two dimensions (height and width), restricting the density of states to highly discrete 1D/2D boundaries, significantly altering the subband energies and effective mass of the carriers .

Conversely, in specialized architectures like Fully Depleted Silicon-On-Insulator (FDSOI) or specific quantum-logic nanosheets, the front gate and the back gate may not be electrically shorted together. They might possess different work functions, different oxide thicknesses, and be driven by independent voltage potentials. To simulate this, the industry relies on BSIM-IMG .

BSIM-IMG computes the surface potential by directly coupling the front and back gates. Because a voltage bias on the back gate alters the electric field penetration through the ultra-thin body, it fundamentally shifts the threshold voltage of the front gate. The back-gate potential coupling is modeled by solving the dual-boundary Poisson equation without assuming vertical symmetry. The exact coupling relationship utilized to find the initial guess for the surface potential iterates the front surface potential ($\psi_f$) against the back surface potential ($\psi_b$) via the capacity of the thin silicon body ($C_b$) and the respective oxide capacitances:

$\psi_{f, coupled} = f(V_{gf}, V_{gb}, C_{ox,front}, C_{ox,back}, C_{body})$

This rigorous mathematical coupling allows HSPICE to accurately predict complex behaviors like threshold voltage modulation (body-biasing) and radiation-induced Total Ionizing Dose (TID) effects, where trapped charges in the back-oxide exponentially degrade the subthreshold response in extreme environments .

## SECTION 9: WIDE-BANDGAP (WBG) MODELS

While silicon FinFETs and nanosheets absolutely dominate high-density logic and memory, high-power and radio-frequency (RF) applications have fundamentally transitioned to Wide-Bandgap (WBG) semiconductors, primarily Gallium Nitride (GaN) and Silicon Carbide (SiC). These materials possess bandgaps significantly larger than silicon ($3.4 \text{ eV}$ for GaN vs. $1.1 \text{ eV}$ for Si), resulting in immense critical breakdown fields, superior electron saturation velocities, and exceptional thermal conductivity. To evaluate GaN High Electron Mobility Transistors (HEMTs) in HSPICE, the Compact Model Coalition standardized the Advanced SPICE Model for HEMTs (ASM-HEMT) .

Unlike the surface inversion-layer physics of a silicon FinFET, a GaN HEMT operates via a Two-Dimensional Electron Gas (2DEG). The 2DEG is formed entirely at the heterojunction interface between an AlGaN barrier layer and the GaN channel. Due to the stark differences in the crystal lattice structures between the two materials, massive spontaneous and piezoelectric polarization fields are permanently generated. These intrinsic physical fields induce a incredibly dense, highly mobile sheet of electrons at the interface without the need for any physical dopants, practically eliminating ionized impurity scattering . 

The ASM-HEMT model is a surface-potential-based model conceptually analogous to BSIM-CMG, but rigorously derived for the unique physics of the triangular quantum well formed at the AlGaN/GaN heterojunction . Solving the Schrödinger equation for the triangular potential well yields the quantized energy levels $E_0$ and $E_1$:

$E_0 = \left( \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*} \right)^{1/3} \left( \frac{3}{2} \pi q E \cdot \frac{3}{4} \right)^{2/3}$

where $E$ is the transverse electric field and $m^*$ is the effective mass of the electron in the triangular well. The charge density of the 2DEG ($n_s$) is then evaluated analytically by summing the density of states over the populated subbands via Fermi-Dirac statistics:

$n_s = DOS \cdot V_{tv} \ln \left[ \left(1 + e^{\frac{E_F - E_0}{V_{tv}}}\right) \left(1 + e^{\frac{E_F - E_1}{V_{tv}}}\right) \right]$

where $E_F$ is the Fermi level and $V_{tv}$ is the thermal voltage ($V_{tv} = k_B T_{dev} / q$) . 

One of the most mathematically complex phenomena in GaN HEMTs, which HSPICE must simulate perfectly for power electronics, is dynamic $R_{DS(on)}$ collapse (also known as the current collapse or kink effect). During high-voltage switching, highly energetic electrons are injected into deep trap states located in the GaN buffer layer or at the surface passivation interface. When the device turns on, these trapped electrons repel the 2DEG, temporarily depleting the channel and causing a massive, transient increase in the ON-resistance ($R_{DS(on)}$) . 

The ASM-HEMT model incorporates specific sub-models (activated via `TRAPMOD=4`) to capture this dynamic trapping. The shift in the effective threshold voltage due to trap occupation is modeled via time-constant-dependent RC sub-circuits, dynamically modifying the core voltage calculations. The altered threshold voltage ($V_{off}'$) is modeled as:

$V_{off}' = (1 + CK(V_{ds}, V_{gs})) \cdot V_{off}$

where $CK$ is a dynamic trapping function utilizing hyperbolic tangents to match the physical emission and capture rates of the trap states .

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $C_{iss}$ | Input Capacitance ($C_{gs} + C_{gd}$) | Highly non-linear; depends on 2DEG depletion |
| $C_{rss}$ | Reverse Transfer Capacitance ($C_{gd}$) | Millimeter-wave feedback; dictates Miller plateau |
| $C_{oss}$ | Output Capacitance ($C_{ds} + C_{gd}$) | Determines hard-switching power loss in power converters |

Furthermore, because WBG devices operate entirely on majority carrier physics, they lack a reverse-recovery charge ($Q_{rr} = 0$). The ASM-HEMT capacitance core accurately models the highly non-linear $C_{gs}$, $C_{gd}$, and $C_{ds}$ transcapacitances derived directly from the exact derivatives of the 2DEG surface potential, completely omitting the bipolar minority carrier extraction required in traditional silicon models .

## SECTION 10: CONCLUSION AND TRANSITION

The modeling of deeply scaled modern transistors represents an absolute triumph of applied condensed matter physics, quantum mechanics, and computational mathematics. As exhaustively detailed in this chapter, a single silicon transistor in the FinFET and Gate-All-Around era can no longer be abstracted as a simple electrically controlled switch. It is a highly complex three-dimensional structure governed tightly by the Schrödinger-Poisson equations, bounded by quantum mechanical wavefunction confinement, dominated by external 3D parasitic fringing fields, and choked by its own extreme thermodynamic self-heating. 

The widespread adoption of the BSIM-CMG standard, alongside its counterpart BSIM-IMG and the WBG ASM-HEMT, transformed Synopsys HSPICE into a simulator capable of resolving the implicit, highly non-linear surface potential dynamics of these quantum wells. By shifting the computational burden to analytical initial-guess algorithms and internal Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) matrix expansions for self-heating and dynamic trap states, the foundry can deliver predictive accuracy down to the sub-2nm nodes, ensuring silicon matches simulation with unprecedented fidelity.

However, resolving the exact physics of a singular, isolated transistor is only the foundation of digital and analog integrated circuit design. While the active devices govern the generation of current and the switching of logic states, these multi-gate transistors do not exist in a vacuum. A modern microprocessor contains upwards of fifty billion FinFETs or nanosheets, packed within square millimeters, and connected by a staggering, highly dense labyrinth of back-end-of-line (BEOL) copper and ruthenium interconnects. 

As the drive currents increase and the switching transition times ($t_r, t_f$) plummet to the low picosecond domain, the interconnect wires themselves cease to behave as ideal electrical shorts. They mutate into complex transmission lines dominated by skin effects, inductive cross-talk, and extreme RC delay—often dwarfing the intrinsic delay of the advanced transistors we have just mathematically defined. To understand how HSPICE predicts the behavior of a fully routed integrated circuit, one must transition from the quantum mechanics of the silicon lattice to the macroscopic electrodynamics of Maxwell's equations. This leads directly into the analysis of interconnect parasitics, transmission line theory, and electromagnetic wave propagation, which will be the rigorous focus of Chapter 12.
